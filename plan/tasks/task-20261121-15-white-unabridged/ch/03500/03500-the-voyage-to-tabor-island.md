# The Voyage to Tabor Island (draft 1)

CHAPTER XXXV.

DEPARTURE DECIDED UPON—HYPOTHESES—PREPARATIONS—THE THREE PASSENGERS—THE FIRST NIGHT—THE SECOND NIGHT—TABOR ISLAND—SEARCH ON THE SHORE—SEARCH IN THE WOODS—NO ONE—ANIMALS—PLANTS—A HOUSE—DESERTED.

“Some one shipwrecked!” cried Pencroff, “abandoned some hundreds of miles from us upon Tabor Island! Oh! Mr. Smith, you will no longer oppose my project of a voyage now!”

“No, Pencroff,” replied Smith, “and you shall leave as soon as possible.”

“To-morrow?”

“To-morrow.”

The engineer held the paper which he had taken from the bottle in his hand. He considered for a few moments, and then spoke:—

“From this paper, my friends,” said he, “and from the manner in which it is worded, we must conclude that, in the first place, the person cast away upon Tabor Island is a man having a fairly advanced knowledge of seamanship, since he gives the latitude and longitude of the island, agreeing with those which we found ourselves, and to within a minute; secondly, that he is English or American, since the paper is written in English.”

“That is perfectly logical,” answered Spilett, “and the presence of this person explains the arrival of the box on our coast. There has been a shipwreck, since some one has been shipwrecked. As for him, whoever he may be, it is fortunate for him that Pencroff had the idea of building this boat and of trying it this very day, for, had it been a day later, this bottle might have been broken on the reefs.”

“Indeed,” said Herbert, “it is a happy chance that the Bonadventure passed there, at the very time when this bottle was still floating!”

“Don’t it seem to you odd?” asked Smith of Pencroff.

“It seems fortunate, that’s all,” replied the sailor. “Do you see anything extraordinary in it, sir? This bottle must have gone somewhere, and why not here as well as anywhere else?”

“Perhaps you are right, Pencroff,” responded the engineer, “and nevertheless—”

“But,” interrupted Herbert, “nothing proves that this bottle has floated in the water for a long time.”

“Nothing,” responded Spilett, “and moreover the paper seems to have been recently written. What do you think, Cyrus?”

“It is hard to verify, and, besides, we shall know!” answered Smith.

Meanwhile Pencroff had not been idle. He had gone about, and the Bonadventure, with a free wind, all her sails drawing, was speeding toward Claw Cape. Each one thought of the castaway on Tabor Island. Was there still time to save him? This was a great event in the lives of the colonists. They too were but castaways, but it was to be feared that another had not been as favored as they had been, and it was their duty to hasten at once to this one’s relief. Claw Cape was doubled, and towards 4 o’clock the Bonadventure anchored at the mouth of the Mercy.

That evening all the details of the new expedition were arranged. It seemed fitting that Herbert and Pencroff, who understood the management of a boat, were to undertake the voyage alone. By leaving the next day, the 11th of October, they might reach the island in the course of the 13th, for, with the wind which then prevailed, not more than forty-eight hours would be needed to make this passage of a hundred and fifty miles. Allowing for one day there, and three or four days to return in, they could calculate on being at Lincoln Island again on the 17th. The weather was good, the barometer rose steadily, the wind seemed as if it would continue, everything favored these brave men, who were going so far from their island to do a humane act.

Thus it had been agreed that Smith, Neb, and Spilett were to remain at Granite House; but an objection was raised, and the latter, who did not forget his calling as reporter to the New York Herald, having declared that he would swim rather than lose such an opportunity, was allowed to take part in the voyage.

The evening was employed in putting on board the Bonadventure some bedding, utensils, arms, munitions, a compass, and provisions for about eight days; and, this lading having been quickly done, the colonists went up again to Granite House. The next morning, by 5 o’clock, the good-byes were spoken, not without some emotion on both sides, and Pencroff, hoisting the sails, headed for Claw Cape, which had to be doubled before taking the route directly to the southwest. The Bonadventure was already a quarter of a mile from shore when her passengers saw upon the heights of Granite House two men signalling farewells. They were Smith and Neb.

“Our friends!” exclaimed Spilett. “This is our first separation in fifteen months!”

Pencroff, Herbert, and the reporter made a last signal of farewell, and soon Granite House disappeared behind the high rocks of the Cape.

During the morning, the Bonadventure remained constantly in view of the southern coast of Lincoln Island, which soon appeared only like a green basket, above which rose Mount Franklin. The heights, lessened by distance, gave it an appearance little calculated to attract ships on its coasts. Towards 1 o’clock Reptile Promontory was passed, but ten miles out. From this distance it was no longer possible to distinguish anything of the western coast, which extended to the spurs of Mount Franklin, and three hours later, all that was Lincoln Island had disappeared below the horizon.

The Bonadventure behaved admirably. She rode lightly over the seas and sailed rapidly. Pencroff had set his topsail, and with all sail set he followed a straight course by the compass. Occasionally Herbert took the tiller, and the hand of the young lad was so sure, that the sailor had nothing to correct.

Spilett chatted with one and the other, and lent a hand when necessary in manœuvring the sloop. Captain Pencroff was perfectly satisfied with his crew, and talked of nothing less than treating them to “a quart of wine a watch!”

In the evening the crescent of the moon, which would not be in its first quarter until the 16th, glimmered in the twilight, and soon went out. The night came on dark, but thick with stars, with the promise of a fine day on the morrow. Pencroff thought it prudent to take in the topsail, not wishing to run the risk of being surprised by too strong a breeze with canvas at the masthead. This was perhaps an excess of caution in so still a night, but he was a careful sailor, and was not to be blamed.

The reporter slept during part of the night, Herbert and Pencroff taking two-hour turns at the helm. The sailor had as much confidence in his pupil as he had in himself, and his trust was justified by the coolness and judgment of the lad. Pencroff set the course as a captain to his helmsman, and Herbert did not allow the Bonadventure to deviate a point from her direction.

The night passed well, and the 12th of October went by in the same conditions. The Bonadventure held strictly to her southwest course all that day, and, unless she was drawn aside by some unknown current, she would make Tabor Island exactly. As to the sea over which the boat was then passing, it was completely deserted. Sometimes a great bird, an albatross or frigate-bird, passed within gun-shot distance, and Spilett wondered whether it was to one of these powerful birds that he had entrusted his last chronicle addressed to the New York Herald. These birds were the only creatures which appeared to frequent this part of the ocean lying between Tabor Island and Lincoln Island.

“And yet,” said Herbert, “this is the season when the whalers usually come towards the southern part of the Pacific. Truly, I don’t believe that there is a sea more deserted than this!”

“It is not altogether deserted,” responded Pencroff.

“What do you mean?” asked the reporter.

“Why we are here. Do you take us for porpoises or our sloop for driftwood?” And Pencroff laughed at his pleasantry.

By evening, by dead reckoning, they calculated that the Bonadventure had traversed a distance of 120 miles since her departure from Lincoln Island, that is to say in thirty-six hours, which gave a speed of three and a third miles an hour. The breeze was light, and was dying away. Nevertheless they had reason to hope, supposing their reckoning to be right and their course to have been correct, that they would sight Tabor Island at daylight next day.

Thus neither Spilett, nor Herbert, nor Pencroff slept during this night of the 12th to the 13th of October. While waiting for morning they experienced the liveliest emotions. There was so much uncertainty in their enterprise. Were they near the island? Was the island still inhabited by the shipwrecked man to whose relief they were going? Who was he? Might not his presence bring some trouble into the little colony, hitherto so united? Would he, indeed, consent to exchange one prison for another? All these questions, which would doubtless be answered the next day, kept them alert, and at the earliest dawn they began to scan every point of the western horizon in turn.

Towards 6 o’clock in the morning Pencroff shouted—

“Land!”

And as it was inadmissible that Pencroff should be mistaken, it was evident that the land was there.

One may judge of the joy of the little crew of the Bonadventure! In a few hours they would be upon the shore of the island!

Tabor Island, a sort of low coast, raised but a little above the waves, was not more than fifteen miles away. The sloop’s head, which had been a little to the south of the island, was put directly upon it, and, as the sun rose, a few elevations became visible here and there.

“It is only an islet, much smaller than Lincoln Island,” said Herbert, “and probably owes its origin to some like submarine upheaval.”

By 11 o’clock the Bonadventure was only two miles distant from shore, and Pencroff, while seeking some place to land, sailed with extreme caution through these unknown waters. They could see the whole extent of this island, on which were visible groups of verdant gum-trees and other large trees of the same species as those on Lincoln Island. But, what was rather astonishing, no rising smoke indicated that the place was inhabited, nor was any signal visible upon the shore. Nevertheless the paper had been precise: it stated that there was a shipwrecked man here; and he should have been upon the watch.

Meanwhile the Bonadventure went in through the tortuous passages between the reefs, of which Pencroff watched the least windings with the most extreme attention. He had put Herbert at the helm, and, stationed forward, he examined the waters, with the halliards in his hand, ready to run down the sail. Spilett, with the spy-glass, examined all the shore without perceiving anything. At last, about noon, the sloop’s stem struck upon a sandy beach, the anchor was let go, the sails furled, and the crew stepped on shore.

There could be no doubt that that was Tabor Island, since the most recent maps gave no other island in all this part of the Pacific, between New Zealand and the American coast.

After having securely moored the sloop, so that the ebb of the tide could not carry her away, Pencroff and his two companions, well armed, ascended the coast towards a sort of cone, some 250 or 300 feet high, which was distant about half a mile.

“From the summit of that hill,” said Spilett, “we shall doubtless be able to get a general view of the islet, which will make our search easier.”

“That is doing here,” replied Herbert, “what Mr. Smith did first of all on Lincoln Island, when he climbed Mount Franklin.”

“Exactly,” replied the reporter, “and it is the best way of proceeding!”

While talking, the explorers followed the edge of a grassy plain which ended at the foot of the hill. Flocks of rock-pigeons and sea-swallows, like those of Lincoln Island, rose before them, and in the woods bordering the plain to the left they heard rustlings in the bushes and saw movements in the grass indicating the presence of very timid animals, but nothing, so far, indicated that the island was inhabited.

Having reached the hill the party soon climbed to its summit, and their gaze traversed the whole horizon. They were certainly upon an island, not more than six miles in circumference, in shape a long oval, its outline but little fringed with capes or promontories, and but little hollowed into coves or creeks. All around it, the sea, absolutely deserted, stretched away to the horizon. There was no land, not a sail, in sight!

This islet, wooded over its entire surface, did not offer the variety of aspect of Lincoln Island, arid and wild in one part, but fertile and rich in the other. Here was a uniform mass of verdure, above which rose two or three hills of no great height. Obliquely to the oval of the island a small stream crossed a large grassy plain and emptied into the sea on the western side by a narrow mouth.

“The place is small,” said Herbert.

“Yes,” replied the sailor. “It would have been rather small for us!”

“And,” added the reporter, “it seems uninhabited.”

“Indeed,” replied Herbert, “nothing here betrays the presence of man.”

“Let us go down and search,” said Pencroff.

The party returned to the sloop, and they decided to walk round the entire island before venturing into its interior, so that no place could escape their investigation.

The shore was easily followed, and in a few places only was it cut by great rocks, which could easily be got round. The explorers proceeded towards the south, starting up flocks of aquatic birds and herds of seals, which latter threw themselves into the sea as soon as they caught sight of the party.

“Those beasts are not looking on man for the first time. They fear them, therefore they know them,” said the reporter.

An hour after their departure the three had reached the southern point of the islet, which terminated in a sharp cape, and they turned towards the north, following the western shore, which was formed, like the other, of sand and rocks, and bordered in the background by thick woods.

In four hours after they had set out the party had made the circuit of the island, without having seen any trace of a habitation, and not even the print of a human foot. It was most extraordinary, to say the least, and it seemed necessary to believe that the place was not, or was no longer, inhabited. Perhaps, after all, the paper was already several months, or even several years, old, and it was possible, in that case, that the shipwrecked one had been taken home or that he had died from suffering.

The little party, discussing all sorts of possibilities, made a hasty dinner on board the sloop, so as to resume their excursion and continue it until night. This was done at 5 o’clock, when they ventured into the woods.

Numerous animals fled before their approach, principally, indeed solely, goats and pigs, which it was easy to see were of European origin. Doubtless some whaler had left them here, and they had rapidly multiplied. Herbert made up his mind to catch one or two pairs alive to take back to Lincoln Island.

There was no longer any doubt that men had, at some time or other, visited the island. This was the more evident as in passing through the forest they saw the traces of pathways, and the trunks of trees felled by the hatchet, and all about, marks of human handiwork; but these trees, which were falling into decay, had been felled many years before; the hatchet marks were velvetted with moss, and the pathways were so overgrown with grass that it was difficult to discover them.

“But,” observed Spilett, “this proves that men not only landed here, but that they lived here for some time. Now who and how many were these men, and how many remain?”

“The paper speaks of but one,” replied Herbert.

“Well,” said Pencroff, “if he is still here we cannot help finding him.”

The exploration was continued. The sailor and his companions naturally followed the path which crossed the island diagonally, and by this means they came to skirt the little stream which flowed towards the sea.

If animals of European origin, if works of human hands proved conclusively that man had once been here, many specimens of the vegetable kingdom also evidenced the fact. In certain clear places it was plain that kitchen vegetables had been planted, probably at a somewhat distant period. And Herbert was overjoyed when he discovered potatoes, succory, sorrel, carrots, cabbage, and turnips, of which it was only necessary to gather the seed to enrich the soil of Lincoln Island!

“Good! good!” replied Pencroff, “this will suit Neb finely, and us too. So, if we don’t find the man, at least our voyage will not have been useless, and God will have rewarded us!”

“Doubtless,” replied Spilett, “but to judge from the condition of these fields, it is to be feared that the place has not been inhabited for a long time.”

“Indeed,” replied Herbert, “an inhabitant, whoever he was, would not have neglected so important a crop!”

“Yes!” said Pencroff, “this man has gone! That is to be supposed—”

“We must then admit that the paper had been written a long time ago?”

“Undoubtedly.”

“And that the bottle had been floating in the sea a good while before it arrived at Lincoln Island?”

“Why not?” said Pencroff. “But, see, it is getting dark,” he added, “and I think we had better give over the search.”

“We will go aboard, and to-morrow we will begin again,” replied the reporter.

This was the wisest course, and they were about adopting this counsel, when Herbert, pointing to something dimly visible through the trees, exclaimed:—

“There’s a house!”

All three at once directed their steps towards the place indicated, and they made out in the twilight that it was built of planks, covered with heavy tarpaulin. The door, half closed, was pushed back by Pencroff, who entered quickly.

The place was empty!
