# The Unknown Flees and the Mill Turns (draft 1)

CHAPTER XXXVIII.

A MYSTERY TO BE CLEARED UP—THE FIRST WORDS OF THE UNKNOWN—TWELVE YEARS ON THE ISLET—CONFESSIONS WHICH ESCAPE HIM—THE DISAPPEARANCE—CYRUS SMITH'S CONFIDENCE—CONSTRUCTION OF A MILL—THE FIRST BREAD—AN ACT OF DEVOTION—HONEST HANDS.

Yes! the unhappy man had wept! Some recollection, doubtless, had crossed his mind, and, to use the expression of Cyrus Smith, he had made himself a man again by his tears.

The colonists left him for some time on the plateau, and even went a little way off, so that he might feel himself free; but he had no thought whatever of profiting by this liberty, and Cyrus Smith soon decided to bring him back to Granite House.

Two days after this scene, the Unknown appeared to wish, little by little, to take part in the common life. It was evident that he heard and that he understood. It was no less evident that he showed a strange obstinacy in not speaking to the colonists; for one evening Pencroff, listening at the door of his room, heard these words escape from his lips:—

“No! here! I! never!”

The sailor reported these words to his companions.

“There is some painful mystery there!” said Cyrus Smith.

The Unknown had begun to use the tools of husbandry, and he worked in the kitchen garden. When he stopped in his task, which often happened, he remained as if wrapped up in himself; but, at the engineer's request, the solitude which he seemed to wish to keep was respected. If one of the colonists approached him, he drew back, and sobs heaved his breast, as if it had been too full!

Was it remorse, then, which thus weighed him down? It might be believed so, and Spilett could not help making this observation one day:—

“If he does not speak, it is, I believe, because he would have things too grave to tell!”

They must be patient and wait.

A few days later, on the 3rd of November, the Unknown, working on the plateau, had stopped, after letting his spade fall to the ground; and Cyrus Smith, who was watching him from a little distance, saw once more the tears flowing from his eyes. A sort of irresistible pity drew him towards the man, and he touched his arm lightly.

“My friend?” said he.

The look of the Unknown sought to avoid him, and, when Cyrus Smith wished to take his hand, he drew back quickly.

“My friend,” said Cyrus Smith, in a firmer voice, “look at me; I wish it!”

The Unknown looked at the engineer, and seemed to be under his influence, as a magnetized subject under the power of his magnetizer. He wished to fly. But then a sort of transformation took place in his countenance. His eyes flashed. Words strove to escape from his lips. He could no longer contain himself! At last he folded his arms; then, in a hollow voice:—

“Who are you?” he asked Cyrus Smith.

“Castaways like you,” replied the engineer, whose emotion was deep. “We have brought you here, among your fellow-men.”

“My fellow-men!—I have none!”

“You are among friends—”

“Friends!—to me! friends!” exclaimed the Unknown, hiding his face in his hands. “No—never—leave me! leave me!”

Then he fled towards the side of the plateau which overlooked the sea, and there he remained motionless for a long time.

Cyrus Smith had rejoined his companions, and was relating to them what had just happened.

“Yes! there is a mystery in the life of that man,” said Spilett, “and it seems that he has come back into humanity only by the way of remorse.”

“I do not well know what sort of man we have brought back with us,” said the sailor. “He has secrets—”

“Which we will respect,” replied Cyrus Smith quickly. “If he has committed any fault, he has cruelly expiated it, and, in our eyes, he is absolved.”

For two hours the Unknown remained alone on the shore, evidently under the influence of recollections which brought back to him all his past—a fatal past, no doubt; and the colonists, without losing sight of him, did not try to disturb his solitude.

However, after the two hours, he appeared to have taken a resolution, and came to find Cyrus Smith. His eyes were red with the tears which he had shed, but he wept no longer. His whole countenance bore the stamp of a deep humility. He seemed fearful and ashamed, and to make himself very small, and his eyes were constantly cast down to the ground.

“Sir,” said he to Cyrus Smith, “your companions and you, are you English?”

“No,” replied the engineer, “we are Americans.”

“Ah!” said the Unknown, and he murmured these words:—

“I like that better!”

“And you, my friend?” asked the engineer.

“English,” he replied hastily.

And, as if these few words had weighed upon him to say, he went off along the beach, which he paced from the cascade to the mouth of the Mercy, in a state of extreme agitation.

Then, having at one moment passed near Herbert, he stopped, and, in a stifled voice:—

“What month?” he asked him.

“November,” replied Herbert.

“What year?”

“1866.”

“Twelve years! twelve years!” he exclaimed. Then he left him abruptly.

Herbert had reported to the colonists the questions which had been put to him, and the answer which he had made.

“This unfortunate man,” observed Spilett, “no longer knew either the months or the years!”

“Yes!” added Herbert, “and he had already been twelve years on the islet when we found him there!”

“Twelve years!” replied Cyrus Smith. “Ah! twelve years of solitude, after an accursed existence, perhaps, may well impair a man's reason!”

“I am inclined to believe,” said Pencroff then, “that this man did not come to Tabor Island by shipwreck, but that he was left there in consequence of some crime.”

“You must be right, Pencroff,” replied the reporter, “and if it is so, it is not impossible that those who left him on the island may come back one day to look for him there!”

“And they will not find him any longer,” said Herbert.

“But then,” returned Pencroff, “we should have to go back, and—”

“My friends,” said Cyrus Smith, “let us not discuss this question before we know where we stand. I believe that this unhappy man has suffered, that he has severely expiated his faults, whatever they may be, and that the need of unburdening himself is stifling him. Let us not provoke him to tell us his history! He will tell it to us, no doubt, and when we have learned it, we shall see what course it will be fitting to follow. Besides, he alone can tell us whether he has kept more than the hope, the certainty, of being one day restored to his country; but I doubt it!”

“And why?” asked the reporter.

“Because, if he had been sure of being delivered at a fixed time, he would have waited for the hour of his deliverance, and would not have thrown that document into the sea. No, it is rather probable that he was condemned to die on that islet, and that he was never to see his fellow-men again!”

“But,” observed the sailor, “there is one thing which I cannot explain to myself.”

“What is that?”

“If it is twelve years since this man was left on Tabor Island, we may well suppose that he had already been for several years in that state of savagery in which we found him!”

“That is probable,” replied Cyrus Smith.

“Then, consequently, it would be several years since he wrote that document!”

“No doubt—and yet the document seemed to have been recently written!—”

“Besides, how can we admit that the bottle which held the document took several years to come from Tabor Island to Lincoln Island?”

“That is not absolutely impossible,” replied the reporter. “Might it not have been already for a long time in the waters about the island?”

“No,” replied Pencroff, “for it was still floating. We cannot even suppose that, after lying a longer or a shorter time on the shore, it could have been taken up again by the sea; for the south coast is all rocks, and it would infallibly have been broken there!”

“That is true,” replied Cyrus Smith, who remained thoughtful.

“And then,” added the sailor, “if the document was several years old, if it had been shut up in that bottle for several years, it would have been damaged by the damp. Now, there was nothing of the kind, and it was in a perfect state of preservation.”

The sailor's observation was very just, and there was in it a fact beyond comprehension; for the document seemed to have been recently written when the colonists found it in the bottle. Moreover, it gave the situation of Tabor Island in latitude and longitude with precision, which implied in its author a tolerably complete knowledge of hydrography, such as a common sailor could not have.

“There is in this, once again, something inexplicable,” said the engineer; “but let us not provoke our new companion to speak. When he wishes it, my friends, we shall be ready to hear him!”

During the days which followed, the Unknown did not pronounce a word, and did not once leave the precincts of the plateau. He worked at the soil without losing an instant, without taking a moment's rest, but always apart. At meal-times he did not go up to Granite House, although the invitation had been made to him several times, and he contented himself with eating a few raw vegetables. When night came, he did not return to the room which had been assigned to him, but remained out there, under some clump of trees, or, when the weather was bad, he crouched in some hollow of the rocks. Thus he still lived as in the time when he had had no other shelter than the forests of Tabor Island; and, all urging to lead him to change his life having been in vain, the colonists waited patiently. But the moment was at last coming when, imperiously, and as if driven against his will by his conscience, terrible confessions were to escape him.

On the 10th of November, towards eight o'clock in the evening, just as it was beginning to grow dark, the Unknown presented himself unexpectedly before the colonists, who were assembled under the veranda. His eyes shone strangely, and his whole person had taken on again the savage aspect of his bad days.

Cyrus Smith and his companions were as if struck dumb on seeing that, under the sway of a terrible emotion, his teeth were chattering like those of a man in a fever. What was the matter with him, then? Was the sight of his fellow-men insupportable to him? Had he had enough of this existence among honest people? Was the longing for his brutish state taking hold of him again? They were obliged to think so, when they heard him express himself thus, in incoherent phrases:—

“Why am I here?—By what right have you torn me from my islet?—Can there be any tie between you and me?—Do you know who I am—what I have done—why I was there—alone? And who told you that I was not abandoned there—that I was not condemned to die there?—Do you know my past?—Do you know whether I have not robbed, murdered—whether I am not a wretch—an accursed being—fit to live like a wild beast—far from all—speak—do you know it?”

The colonists listened without interrupting the wretched man, from whom these half-confessions escaped, so to speak, in spite of himself. Cyrus Smith then wished to calm him by going up to him, but he drew back quickly.

“No! no!” he exclaimed. “One word only—am I free?”

“You are free,” replied the engineer.

“Farewell, then!” he cried, and he fled like a madman.

Neb, Pencroff, and Herbert ran at once towards the edge of the wood—but they came back alone.

“We must let him do as he will!” said Cyrus Smith.

“He will never come back—” exclaimed Pencroff.

“He will come back,” replied the engineer.

And since then many days went by; but Cyrus Smith—was it a sort of presentiment?—persisted in the unshaken idea that the unhappy man would return sooner or later.

“It is the last revolt of that rough nature,” said he, “which remorse has touched, and which a fresh solitude would terrify.”

Meanwhile, work of all sorts was carried on, as much on Prospect Plateau as at the corral, where Cyrus Smith intended to build a farm. It need hardly be said that the seeds gathered by Herbert on Tabor Island had been carefully sown. The plateau now formed a vast kitchen garden, well laid out and well kept, which did not let the arms of the colonists lie idle. There was always work to do there. As the kitchen plants had multiplied, it had been necessary to enlarge the simple beds, which tended to become real fields and to take the place of the meadows. But fodder abounded in the other parts of the island, and the onaggas had no reason to fear that they would ever be put on short rations. It was better, besides, to turn Prospect Plateau into a kitchen garden, defended as it was by its deep belt of creeks, and to carry outside it the meadows, which had no need to be protected against the depredations of quadrumana and quadrupeds.

On the 15th of November the third harvest was made. Here was a field which had grown in surface since the first grain of wheat had been sown, eighteen months before! The second crop, of six hundred thousand grains, produced this time four thousand bushels, or more than five hundred millions of grains! The colony was rich in wheat; for it was enough to sow ten bushels or so for the harvest to be secured every year, and for all, men and beasts, to be able to feed upon it.

The harvest, then, was made, and the last fortnight of the month of November was given to the work of bread-making.

In fact, they had the grain, but not the flour, and it was necessary to set up a mill. Cyrus Smith might have used the second fall which poured into the Mercy to establish his motor, the first being already occupied in working the stampers of the fulling-mill; but, after discussion, it was decided that a simple windmill should be set up on the heights of Prospect Plateau. The construction of the one offered no more difficulty than the construction of the other; and they were sure, besides, that the wind would never fail on that plateau, exposed as it was to the breezes from the open sea.

“Without reckoning,” said Pencroff, “that a windmill will be gayer, and will have a good effect in the landscape!”

They set to work, therefore, choosing timber for the body and the machinery of the mill. Some large sandstones which were found in the north of the lake could easily be made into millstones; and as to the sails, the inexhaustible envelope of the balloon would furnish them with the necessary cloth.

Cyrus Smith made the plans, and the site of the mill was chosen a little to the right of the poultry-yard, near the bank of the lake. The whole body was to rest on a pivot held in stout timbers, so that it could turn, with all the machinery which it contained, as the wind required.

This work was rapidly accomplished. Neb and Pencroff had become very skilful carpenters, and had only to follow the patterns furnished by the engineer. So a sort of cylindrical sentry-box, a true pepper-box, capped with a pointed roof, soon rose on the spot chosen. The four frames which formed the sails had been firmly set into the shaft, so as to make a certain angle with it, and they were fixed by means of iron tenons. As to the different parts of the inner machinery, they were made without trouble. These were the box intended to hold the two millstones, the bed-stone and the runner; the hopper, a sort of large square trough, wide at the top and narrow at the bottom, which was to let the grain fall upon the stones; the oscillating spout intended to regulate the passage of the grain, to which its perpetual tick-tack has given the name of “babbler;” and lastly the bolter, which, by the process of sifting, separates the bran from the flour. The tools were good, and the work was not difficult; for, after all, the parts of a mill are very simple. It was only a question of time.

Every one had worked at the construction of the mill, and on the 1st of December it was finished.

As always, Pencroff was delighted with his work, and he did not doubt that the apparatus was perfect.

“Now for a good wind,” said he, “and we shall grind our first harvest finely!”

“A good wind, by all means,” replied the engineer, “but not too much wind, Pencroff.”

“Bah! our mill will only go round the faster!”

“It is not necessary that it should go round so fast,” replied Cyrus Smith. “It is known by experience that the greatest quantity of work is produced by a mill when the number of turns made by the sails in a minute is six times the number of feet travelled by the wind in a second. With a moderate breeze, which gives twenty-four feet a second, the wind will give the sails sixteen turns in a minute, and no more is needed.”

“Just so!” cried Herbert. “There is a fine breeze blowing from the northeast, which will suit our purpose well!”

There was no reason for putting off the opening of the mill, for the colonists were in haste to taste the first piece of bread of Lincoln Island. That day, then, in the morning, two or three bushels of wheat were ground; and the next day, at breakfast, a magnificent loaf, a little heavy perhaps, although raised with beer-yeast, appeared on the table of Granite House. Every one bit into it with a will, and with what pleasure may well be understood!

Meanwhile the Unknown had not reappeared. Several times Spilett and Herbert had gone over the forest round about Granite House, without meeting him, without finding any trace of him. They were seriously uneasy at this prolonged disappearance. Certainly the former savage of Tabor Island could find no difficulty in living in these forests of the Far West, so full of game; but was it not to be feared that he would take up his old habits again, and that this independence would revive his savage instincts? However, Cyrus Smith, by a sort of presentiment, no doubt, still persisted in saying that the fugitive would return.

“Yes, he will come back!” he repeated, with a confidence which his companions could not share. “When this unfortunate man was on Tabor Island, he knew himself to be alone! Here he knows that his fellow-men are waiting for him! Since he has half spoken of his past life, the poor penitent, he will come back to tell it all; and on that day he will be ours!”

The event was to prove Cyrus Smith right.

On the 3rd of December, Herbert had left Prospect Plateau and had gone to fish on the southern bank of the lake. He was unarmed; and until then there had never been any precaution to take, since dangerous animals had not shown themselves in that part of the island.

Meanwhile Pencroff and Neb were working at the poultry-yard, while Cyrus Smith and the reporter were busy at the Chimneys making soda, the stock of soap being exhausted.

Suddenly cries rang out:—

“Help! help me!”

Cyrus Smith and the reporter, being too far off, had not been able to hear these cries. Pencroff and Neb, leaving the poultry-yard in all haste, had rushed towards the lake.

But before them, the Unknown, whose presence in that place no one could have suspected, was crossing Glycerine Creek, which separated the plateau from the forest, and was bounding upon the opposite bank.

There Herbert was face to face with a formidable jaguar, like the one which had been killed at Reptile End. Taken by surprise, he was standing with his back against a tree, while the animal, gathered together, was about to spring—But the Unknown, with no other weapon than a knife, rushed upon the dreadful beast, which turned upon this new adversary.

The struggle was short. The Unknown was of prodigious strength and skill. He had seized the jaguar by the throat with a hand as powerful as a pair of shears, without heeding whether the claws of the beast were entering his flesh; and with the other hand he was searching its heart with his knife.

The jaguar fell. The Unknown pushed it with his foot, and was about to fly, at the moment when the colonists were arriving on the scene of the struggle, when Herbert, clinging to him, cried:—

“No! no! you shall not go away!”

Cyrus Smith went towards the Unknown, whose brows knitted when he saw him approach. The blood was flowing from his shoulder under his torn jacket, but he paid no heed to it.

“My friend,” said Cyrus Smith to him, “we have just contracted a debt of gratitude towards you. To save our child you have risked your life!”

“My life!” murmured the Unknown. “What is it worth? Less than nothing!”

“You are wounded?”

“It is no matter.”

“Will you give me your hand?”

And as Herbert was trying to seize this hand, which had just saved him, the Unknown folded his arms, his breast heaved, his eyes grew dim, and he seemed about to fly; but, making a violent effort over himself, he said in an abrupt tone:—

“Who are you, and what do you claim to be to me?”

It was the history of the colonists which he thus asked for, and for the first time. Perhaps, this history once told, he would tell his own?

In a few words Cyrus Smith related all that had happened since their departure from Richmond; how they had got out of their difficulties, and what resources were now at their disposal.

The Unknown listened to him with extreme attention.

Then the engineer told what they all were, Spilett, Herbert, Pencroff, Neb, and himself; and he added that the greatest joy which they had felt since their arrival on Lincoln Island had been on their return from the islet, when they had been able to count one companion more.

At these words the Unknown flushed, his head sank upon his breast, and confusion was painted on his whole person.

“And now that you know us,” added Cyrus Smith, “will you give us your hand?”

“No,” replied the Unknown, in a hollow voice; “no! You are honest men, you! And I—!”
