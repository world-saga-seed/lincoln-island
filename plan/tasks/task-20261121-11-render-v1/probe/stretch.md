
CHAPTER XIX.

THE PLAN OF CYRUS SMITH—THE FACADE OF GRANITE HOUSE—THE ROPE LADDER—PENCROFF'S DREAMS—THE AROMATIC HERBS—A NATURAL WARREN—WATER LED OFF FOR THE NEEDS OF THE NEW DWELLING—THE VIEW FROM THE WINDOWS OF GRANITE HOUSE.

The next day, the 22nd of May, the works intended for the fitting up of the new dwelling were begun. The colonists were, in fact, impatient to exchange the insufficient shelter of the Chimneys for this vast and healthy retreat, hollowed out of the solid rock, sheltered from the waters of the sea and of the sky. The Chimneys, however, were not to be entirely abandoned, and the engineer's project was to make of them a workshop for the heavy work.

The first care of Cyrus Smith was to find out at what precise point the facade of Granite House lay. He went to the beach, at the foot of the enormous wall. As the pick, which had slipped from the reporter's hands, must have fallen perpendicularly, it was enough to find this pick again to know the place where the hole had been pierced in the granite.

The pick was easily found. In fact, a hole opened in a perpendicular line above the point where it had stuck in the sand, about eighty feet above the beach. Some rock pigeons were already flying in and out of this narrow opening. It really seemed as if Granite House had been discovered for them!

The engineer's intention was to divide the right-hand portion of the cavern into several rooms, preceded by an entrance passage, and to light it by means of five windows and a door pierced in the facade. Pencroff quite admitted the five windows. But he did not understand the use of the door, since the old outlet offered a natural staircase, by which it would always be easy to get into Granite House.

"My friend," answered Cyrus Smith, "if it is easy for us to reach our dwelling by the outlet, it will be equally easy for others besides ourselves. I mean, on the contrary, to stop up this outlet at its mouth, to close it hermetically, and, if it should be necessary, even to hide its entrance altogether, by raising the waters of the lake by means of a dam."

"And how shall we get in?" asked the sailor.

"By an outside ladder," answered Cyrus Smith; "a rope ladder, which, once drawn up, will make access to our dwelling impossible."

"But why so many precautions?" said Pencroff. "Up to now the animals have not seemed to us very formidable. As to our island being inhabited by natives, it is not!"

"Are you quite sure of that, Pencroff?" asked the engineer, looking at the sailor.

"Of course we shall not be sure of it until we have explored it in every part," answered Pencroff.

"Yes," said Cyrus Smith, "for as yet we know only a small portion of it. But at any rate, if we have no enemies within, they may come from without, for these parts of the Pacific are bad parts. Let us therefore take our precautions against every chance."

Cyrus Smith spoke wisely, and, without making any further objection, Pencroff prepared to carry out his orders.

The facade of Granite House was then to be lighted by five windows and a door, serving what made up the "apartment," properly so called. A wide bay and some bull's-eye windows would let the light enter in profusion into that marvellous nave which was to serve as the great hall. This facade, situated at a height of eighty feet above the ground, was exposed to the east, and the rising sun greeted it with its first rays. It extended over that portion of the curtain which lay between the salient forming an angle over the mouth of the Mercy and a line traced perpendicularly above the heap of rocks which formed the Chimneys. Thus the bad winds, that is to say, those from the northeast, struck it only obliquely, for it was protected by the very bearing of the salient. Besides, until the sashes of the windows should be made, the engineer meant to close the openings with thick shutters, which would let through neither wind nor rain, and which he could hide if need be.

The first work, then, was to hollow out these openings. Working the pick upon this hard rock would have been too slow. It is known that Cyrus Smith was the man for great means. He had still a certain quantity of nitro-glycerine at his disposal, and he employed it to good purpose. The effect of the explosive substance was properly localized. Under its force, the granite broke in at the very places chosen by the engineer. Then the pick and the mattock finished the ogival outline of the five windows, of the wide bay, of the bull's-eyes, and of the door. They trued the framings, whose profiles were rather fancifully finished off. A few days after the beginning of the works, Granite House was abundantly lighted by the morning light, which penetrated even to its most secret depths.

According to the plan settled by Cyrus Smith, the apartment was to be divided into five compartments looking out upon the sea: on the right, an entrance, served by a door at which the ladder would end; then a first room, a kitchen, thirty feet wide; a dining room, measuring forty feet; a dormitory of equal width; and lastly a "spare room," asked for by Pencroff, which adjoined the great hall.

These rooms, or rather this suite of rooms, which formed the apartment of Granite House, were not to occupy the whole depth of the cavity. They were to be served by a corridor contrived between them and a long storeroom, in which the utensils, the provisions and the reserves would find ample room. All the produce gathered on the island, that of the flora as well as that of the fauna, would be kept there in excellent condition, and completely sheltered from damp. Space was not wanting, and each object could be methodically arranged. Besides, the colonists had still at their disposal the little grotto situated above the great cavern, which would be, as it were, the garret of the new dwelling.

This plan settled, nothing remained but to carry it out. The miners, then, became brickmakers again; then the bricks were brought and laid at the foot of Granite House.

Until then Cyrus Smith and his companions had entered the cavern only by the old outlet. This mode of communication obliged them first to climb to Prospect Plateau, making a round by the bank of the river, to descend two hundred feet by the passage, and then to climb as much again when they wished to return to the plateau. Hence a loss of time and considerable fatigue. Cyrus Smith therefore resolved to proceed without delay to the making of a strong rope ladder, which, once drawn up, would render the entrance of Granite House absolutely inaccessible.

This ladder was made with extreme care. Its uprights, formed of the fibres of the "curry-rush," twisted by means of a winch, had the strength of a thick cable. As to the rungs, a kind of red cedar, with light and strong branches, furnished them. The apparatus was worked with a master's hand by Pencroff.

Other ropes were also made of vegetable fibres, and a sort of rough tackle was fixed at the door. In this way the bricks could easily be hoisted to the level of Granite House. The carriage of the materials was thus very much simplified, and the arrangement of the interior, properly so called, began at once. There was no want of lime, and some thousands of bricks were there, ready to be used. The framework of the partitions, very rudimentary besides, was easily set up. In a very short time, the apartment was divided into rooms and a storeroom, according to the plan agreed upon.

These various works went on rapidly, under the direction of the engineer, who himself handled the hammer and the trowel. No labor was foreign to Cyrus Smith, who thus set the example to intelligent and zealous companions. They worked with confidence, gayly even, Pencroff always having his joke ready, now carpenter, now ropemaker, now mason, and communicating his good humor to all the little band. His faith in the engineer was absolute. Nothing could have troubled it. He believed him capable of undertaking anything and of succeeding in everything. The question of clothes and of shoes—a serious question, assuredly—that of lighting during the winter nights, the cultivation of the fertile portions of the island, the transformation of this wild flora into a civilized flora, everything appeared easy to him, with Cyrus Smith's help. All would be done in its time. He dreamed of canalized rivers, facilitating the transport of the riches of the soil; of quarries and mines to be worked; of machines fit for every industrial use; of railways—yes, of railways!—whose network would certainly one day cover Lincoln Island.

The engineer let Pencroff talk. He did not abate the exaggerations of this brave heart. He knew how catching confidence is. He even smiled to hear him talk, and said nothing of the uneasiness which the future sometimes caused him. In fact, in this part of the Pacific, out of the course of vessels, he might fear that they would never be relieved. It was, then, upon themselves, upon themselves alone, that the colonists must reckon, for the distance from Lincoln Island to any other land was such that to venture out in a boat, of a construction necessarily indifferent, would be a serious and perilous thing.

"But, as the sailor said, they surpassed by a hundred cubits the Robinsons of former times, for whom everything was a miracle to be performed."

And, in fact, they "knew"; and the man who "knows" succeeds where others would vegetate and would inevitably perish.

During these works Herbert distinguished himself. He was intelligent and active; he understood quickly and carried out well. Cyrus Smith became more and more attached to the boy. Herbert felt for the engineer a warm and respectful friendship. Pencroff saw well the close sympathy which was forming between these two beings, but he was not in the least jealous of it.

Neb was Neb. He was what he would always be, courage, zeal, devotion, self-denial personified. He had the same faith in his master as Pencroff, but he showed it less noisily. When the sailor grew enthusiastic, Neb always had the air of answering him, "Why, nothing is more natural." Pencroff and he were very fond of each other, and had not been long in coming to "thee" and "thou."

As to Gideon Spilett, he took his share of the common work, and was not the most awkward at it, at which the sailor was always a little astonished. A "journalist" clever, not only at understanding everything, but at doing everything!

The ladder was finally fixed on the 28th of May. It counted no fewer than a hundred rungs over that perpendicular height of eighty feet which it measured. Cyrus Smith had been able, fortunately, to divide it into two parts, by taking advantage of an overhang of the wall which jutted out some forty feet above the ground. This projection, carefully levelled with the pick, became a sort of landing, to which the first ladder was fixed. Its swing was thus lessened by half, and a rope allowed it to be drawn up to the level of Granite House. As to the second ladder, it was secured at its lower end, which rested on the projection, as well as at its upper end, which was made fast to the door itself. In this way the ascent became much easier. Besides, Cyrus Smith counted on setting up later a hydraulic lift, which would spare the inhabitants of Granite House all fatigue and all loss of time.

The colonists quickly grew used to making use of this ladder. They were light and active. Pencroff, as a sailor, accustomed to running up the ratlines of the shrouds, was able to give them lessons. But he was obliged to give them to Top as well. The poor dog, with his four paws, was not built for this exercise. But Pencroff was so zealous a master that Top ended by performing his ascents properly, and soon went up the ladder as his fellows commonly do in the circus. Whether the sailor was proud of his pupil cannot be told. And yet, more than once, Pencroff carried him up on his back, of which Top never complained.

It must be remarked here that during these works, which were nevertheless pushed on actively, for the bad season was approaching, the question of food had not been neglected. Every day the reporter and Herbert, who had decidedly become the purveyors of the colony, spent a few hours in the chase. As yet they hunted only in Jacamar Wood, on the left of the river, for, for want of a bridge and of a boat, the Mercy had not yet been crossed. All those immense forests to which the name of the forests of the Far West had been given, were thus not explored at all. This important excursion was reserved for the first fine days of the next spring. But Jacamar Wood was well enough stocked with game; kangaroos and wild boars abounded there. The hunters' iron-tipped spears, the bow and the arrows did wonders. Moreover, Herbert discovered, toward the southwest angle of the lagoon, a natural warren, a sort of slightly damp meadow, covered with willows and with aromatic herbs which scented the air, such as thyme, wild thyme, basil, savory, all odorous species of the labiate family, of which rabbits show themselves so fond.

Upon the reporter's remark, that, since the table was laid for rabbits, it would be astonishing if rabbits were wanting, the two hunters explored this warren attentively. At any rate, it produced useful plants in abundance. A naturalist would have had there the opportunity of studying many specimens of the vegetable kingdom. Herbert thus gathered a certain quantity of shoots of basil, of rosemary, of balm, of betony, etc., which possess various therapeutic properties, some pectoral, astringent, febrifuge, others antispasmodic or antirheumatic. And when, later, Pencroff asked what was the use of all this harvest of herbs,—

"To doctor ourselves," answered the lad, "to treat ourselves when we are ill."

"Why should we be ill, since there are no doctors on the island?" answered Pencroff, quite seriously.

To this there was no reply, but the lad none the less gathered his harvest, which was very well received at Granite House. All the more so, since to these medicinal plants he was able to add a considerable quantity of Monarda didyma, which are known in North America by the name of "Oswego tea," and make an excellent drink.

At last, that day, by searching well, the two hunters came upon the true site of the warren. The ground there was perforated like a skimmer.

"Burrows!" cried Herbert.

"Yes," answered the reporter, "I see them well enough."

"But are they inhabited?"

"That is the question."

The question was not long in being answered. Almost at once, hundreds of little animals, like rabbits, fled in every direction, and with such rapidity that Top himself could not have outrun them. Hunters and dog ran in vain; these rodents escaped them easily. But the reporter was quite resolved not to leave the spot before he had captured at least half a dozen of these quadrupeds. He wished to stock the larder with them first of all, and to domesticate those which they might take later on. With a few snares set at the mouths of the burrows, the operation could not fail to succeed. But at this moment there were no snares, nor anything to make them with. They had then to resign themselves to visiting each hole, to rummaging it with a stick, to doing by dint of patience what could not be done otherwise.

At last, after an hour's searching, four rodents were taken in their holes. They were rabbits much like their European fellows, and are commonly known by the name of "American rabbits."

The produce of the chase was therefore brought back to Granite House, and it figured at the evening meal. The tenants of this warren were not to be despised, for they were delicious. It was a precious resource for the colony, and one which seemed likely to be inexhaustible.

On the 31st of May the partitions were finished. Nothing remained but to furnish the rooms, which would be the work of the long days of winter. A fireplace was built in the first room, which served as a kitchen. The pipe meant to carry the smoke outside gave some trouble to the improvised chimney-builders. It appeared simpler to Cyrus Smith to make it of brick clay. As it was not to be thought of to give it an issue by the upper plateau, a hole was pierced in the granite above the window of the said kitchen. It was at this hole that the pipe, directed obliquely, came out, like that of a sheet-iron stove. Perhaps, indeed no doubt, in the great east winds which beat full upon the facade, the chimney would smoke. But these winds were rare, and, besides, Master Neb, the cook, did not look so closely into it.

When these interior arrangements had been finished, the engineer set about stopping the mouth of the old outlet which opened on the lake, so as to forbid all access by that way. Blocks of rock were rolled to the opening and strongly cemented. Cyrus Smith did not yet carry out the plan which he had formed of drowning this opening under the waters of the lake, by bringing them back to their former level by means of a dam. He contented himself with hiding the obstruction by means of grass, shrubs or brushwood, which were planted in the interstices of the rocks, and which the next spring would cause to grow luxuriantly.

However, he made use of the outlet so as to bring a little stream of the fresh water of the lake to the new dwelling. A small cut, made below its level, produced this result. This diversion of a pure and inexhaustible spring yielded from twenty-five to thirty gallons(1) a day. Water, then, would never be wanting in Granite House.

(1) The gallon is about four liters and a half.

At last all was finished, and it was time, for the bad season was coming. Thick shutters allowed the windows of the facade to be closed, until the engineer should have had time to make window glass.

Gideon Spilett had very artistically arranged, in the projections of the rock, around the windows, plants of various kinds, as well as long streaming grasses, and, in this way, the openings were framed in a picturesque greenery of charming effect.

The inhabitants of this solid, healthy and safe dwelling could not but be delighted with their work. The windows allowed their gaze to extend over a boundless horizon, which the two Mandible Capes closed on the north and Claw Cape on the south. All Union Bay spread out magnificently before them. Yes, these worthy colonists had good reason to be satisfied, and Pencroff did not stint his praises of what he humorously called "his apartment on the fifth floor above the entresol!"


CHAPTER XIX bis.

PENCROFF'S LINES—A ROUND THING ON THE BAR—HERBERT TAKEN INTO CONFIDENCE—A DAY OF RAIN—THE BALL OF GLASS—THE GOOSE BARNACLES—A FEW LETTERS—THE SQUALL—A FLASH ON THE ROCKS OF THE ISLET—THE SMOOTH SAND.

On the 1st of June, Pencroff was out at first light. The night had been one of squalls, and, though the wind was dropping, the sea still ran high in Union Bay and broke in long rolls upon the islet, under a sky that was grey and low. The sailor went down the ladder, crossed the strip of shore at the foot of the wall, and took his way along the beach toward the mouth of the Mercy, where, near the Chimneys, on the left bank of the river, he had set his lines.

These lines were his own affair; he had armed them with iron hooks from the forge, and he went every morning to visit them, as a captain goes round his ship.

He walked with his eyes on the sea, for a man who has passed his life on board reads a shore at the waterline, where the last tide has left its mark, and he looks first for what is changed. Much was changed that morning, for the squalls of the last days had worked upon this coast as the great storm of March had worked upon it. Here the sand had been cut away from the foot of a rock, there a bank showed where there had been none before. The tide was near its lowest, and the greater part of the sands which formed the bed of the channel were uncovered; between the islet and the coast there remained only the narrow gut through which the current ran.

Off the mouth of the Mercy, on the coast side of this gut, a bar of sand lay bare, a cable or two from the beach. Pencroff's eyes, which were two veritable telescopes, ran along it, and stopped.

Something lay on the bar, half buried, and it was round.

The sailor stood still. He knew stones, as every man knows them who has walked a beach at low water: the sea rounds them, it is true, but it does not make them so regular as that, nor leave them sitting up in the sand like the top of a buoy on a mud-bank. The thing might measure a foot across, and what it was, at that distance, was more than he could say; but it was the wrong shape for a stone, and that was enough to hold him there a full minute.

He would have gone out to it then and there; but the flood was already making. He could hear the current quicken in the gut, and he had crossed that channel on the first day, and knew what it was worth. A man alone on the bar, with the sea coming in on either hand, would have no one to give him a rope. Pencroff was bold, but he was a seaman, and he did not go out alone.

He went on to his lines, lifted them and set them again, and all the while he looked over his shoulder at the bar. Before he had come back to the foot of the ladder, the sea had covered it.

At breakfast he said nothing; but later in the morning, finding Herbert alone on the beach, he went up to him.

"My boy," said he, "will you lend me a hand with my lines to-morrow, at the morning ebb? I mean to lift them and set them afresh."

"Willingly, Pencroff."

"Good." The sailor lowered his voice, though there was no one within a quarter of a mile but the gulls. "And there is something else. On the bar yonder, off the mouth of the river, I saw a thing this morning that has no business to be there."

"What thing?"

"A round thing, half in the sand. It may be a stone, after all."

"But you do not think so."

"I do not think so. If it is what I think, it was made by men's hands; and if it was made by men's hands—"

"Then a ship—!" cried Herbert.

"Gently, gently. It may have come a long way. That is why I say nothing to the others. I'll not have Mr. Smith and Mr. Spilett and Neb all hoping, and tumbling down the ladder, for a thing that may turn out to be a stone with a coat of weed on it. We will go and see, the two of us. If it is worth the telling, we tell it."

Herbert promised; and no one would wonder to see the lad go down with the sailor to the lines, for it was the errand of every day.

The rest of that day passed at the work of Granite House. The evening ebb fell after dark, when nothing could be done on the sands, and the sailor, who was not a patient man, had to wait the night through for the low water of the next morning.

The 2nd of June dawned in rain. A cold rain, driven by a wind which had gone round to the east-north-east and blew straight into the bay, beat upon the facade, and from first light the thick shutters were kept closed. Granite House was lit only by the fire of the kitchen. The engineer, the reporter and Neb were at work within, upon the furnishing of the rooms, and Top lay at his master's feet. No one looked out at the bar.

There were picks and shovels in the storeroom, and Pencroff thought of them. But a man does not carry a pick down a ladder to lift his lines, and the engineer would have asked what it was for. The tools stayed behind, and the sailor had his knife, one of the two blades made from Top's collar; and that was all.

At the foot of the wall the two companions took off their shoes, which were nearly worn through, set them under the rock, and rolled their trousers to the knee. The sand was hard under their feet, and the water in the pools was cold enough to numb them. They went first to the lines at the mouth of the Mercy, and Pencroff hauled them in, one after the other; the hooks came up as clean as they had gone down.

"Empty," said he. "The fish have more sense than we have, my boy, and keep indoors."

The tide was out, and from the river's mouth the bared sands of the channel stretched toward the islet, ribbed by the ebb and shining with water. And on the coast side of the gut, where the current still ran deep and fast, the bar rose a little above them. There was no need to cross the gut; the two walked out over the wet sand, with the rain in their faces, and in a few minutes they stood beside the thing.

It was a ball of glass.

Pencroff knelt down beside it in the sand. About a third of it stood above the bar, and the rest was buried, so that it showed above the sand as the back of a turtle shows above the sea. Over all its upper part lay a crust of grey-white shells, dead and empty, packed one against another, thickest on the top, where it had faced the sky. Here and there a shell had broken away, and there the glass showed through, dark green, like the glass of a bottle. From under the crust, on the side toward the gut, a dark cord came out of the sand, rotten and swollen with water, with the meshes of a net choked with sand along it; and it ran down again into the bar.

"A net," said Pencroff.

They tried to turn the ball, but it was heavy with the sand that held it, and stuck as fast as a rock. The sailor felt round it with his fingers as far as he could reach. A cord of the same kind as the other passed under it, on the hidden side, and went down into the sand, and his knife could not get at it. To free the ball they must dig; and they had nothing to dig with but their hands, and the sand, as fast as they scooped it out, filled again with water.

Pencroff sat back on his heels and looked at the thing.

"It's a float," said he at last. "A float for a net. But it's no cork, and it was never made in America."

He rapped the glass with his knuckle, where the shells had broken away.

"Glass! I have put into a good many ports, my boy, and I never saw the like."

Herbert picked up a stone from the bar and began to knock away the crust, a little at a time, holding his hand very lightly, for fear of cracking the glass. Pencroff, on his knees, held the ball steady with both hands, and kept one eye upon the water.

And while the lad worked, the sailor made his reckoning. A foot of good glass! At that moment Granite House sat behind its shutters, in the light of the fire, because the engineer had not yet had the time to make glass for its windows. Here was glass already made. What Mr. Smith could do with it Pencroff could only guess; but he had seen him make a lens of two watch glasses, and he was sure that he could make a window of a ball. And failing a window, it would be the finest float for his lines that a fisherman ever had; but he kept all this to himself.

The shells came away more easily than Herbert had feared, and he looked at them closely as they fell.

"These are goose barnacles," said he.

"Barnacles, I know well enough," answered Pencroff. "I have scraped them off many a ship's bottom."

"Yes, but these are of the kind that hang by a stalk. They fasten upon anything that floats—driftwood, a buoy, a ship—and they live only near the surface, where the sea brings them their food. These are dead, and long dead. The stalks have rotted away, and only the shells are left."

"And what does that tell you?"

"That this ball floated a long while before ever the sand took it. It has been in the sea a long time, Pencroff; and it must have come from far off."

The sailor looked out over the bay, where the rain hid the horizon.

"From far off," he repeated. "So there is no ship to look for in the offing."

"Not near, perhaps."

"Far off is a long word, my boy," said Pencroff, after a moment. "Ships go a long way."

Herbert went on with his stone. He was working now lower down, close to the sand, on the side toward the gut, and there, under the crust, his fingers felt a ridge in the glass. He cleared it with care; it was a raised edge, curving, the rim of a round seal about a palm across, which had been moulded in the glass itself. But the greater part of the seal lay below, toward the sand, and only a piece of it could be seen. Along the inside of this rim there ran letters, capitals, standing out from the glass.

"Letters!" cried Pencroff.

Herbert scraped again. The first was a B. After it came a place where the crust held fast and would not come away under the stone. Then an L; then an I. Beyond the I, the ring went down into the sand.

"Can you read them, my boy?"

Herbert looked at them a long while, his stone in his hand.

"Latin, I think," said he.

He said no more. The two bent over these three letters, the lad trying the crust again between the B and the L, the sailor with his eyes upon them, and for a while even Pencroff forgot to watch the water.

A sound made them look up.

The squall was on them. It came out of the east, across the bay, a grey wall of rain and wind. The sea rose with it. The flood had turned while they knelt there, and now the flood and the squall came in together. A wave ran up the bar and broke over Pencroff's knees.

"Back!" cried the sailor.

They could not stay. Already the sea was running over the sands they had crossed dry an hour before. The water was at their knees, then above them. Pencroff took the lad by the arm. They waded, ran, waded again. The wind tore at them. Behind them the waves broke upon the bar, and when Pencroff turned his head he could still see the float, dark in the white of the surf.

They reached the beach. They went up it to the granite wall and stood close under it, near the foot of the ladder, out of the worst of the wind. From there they could still see the bar, or the place where the bar had been, and the float upon it, now hidden, now showing between two seas.

The squall did not slacken. The sea came in heavier with every minute, and the rain drove along the face of the wall. At last what was left of the rotten cord gave way; the backwash drew the freed ball off the bar into the gut, and the current of the channel carried it toward the point of the islet.

Then, for a moment, the clouds broke under the squall. A pale light fell across the channel. Out on the rocks of the islet, in the spray, something flashed, once, as wet glass flashes in the sun.

Then nothing.

Pencroff looked at the bar. The float was gone from it. He looked again at the islet, and searched its rocks from one end to the other, and he saw only the spray. Neither of them spoke. They both knew that glass thrown upon those rocks by such a sea was in pieces. Where the bar had been there was only broken water, and the flood ran over it.

Toward one o'clock the squall eased; the flood had long since covered the bar. The two companions went back for their shoes, and climbed the ladder, which streamed with water, and came into Granite House wet to the skin.

"Well, Pencroff," said the reporter, "what has the sea given you this morning?"

"The lines took nothing, and the sea took the pair of us."

Neb laughed loudly, and set about making up the fire, and the engineer smiled. The two wet men stood before the hearth and dried their clothes upon their backs, as sailors do. That was all they told, and it was true, as far as it went.

Pencroff might have told the rest then, by the fire. He did not. The sea had uncovered the thing once; it might give back something else at the next low water, the net, or some piece of what had been on the bar. The sailor meant to go back and see, and to bring home with his own hands whatever he found, and lay it on the table before them all.

The 3rd of June was grey and still, for in the night the wind had gone round to the south-west, and the sea was down. Toward the middle of the morning, at low water, the two went down once more to the lines, and from the lines out over the sands.

The bar had moved.

Where the ball had lain the day before, the sand ran smooth and level to the gut, and of the net not a thread showed.

Pencroff stood a long time and looked at it.

"Five of us and the picks, and we'd have had it," said he.

"We had it, Pencroff," said Herbert. "For an hour."

The sailor looked at him, and answered nothing; then they went back across the sand to the lines.


CHAPTER XX.

THE RAINY SEASON—THE QUESTION OF CLOTHES—A SEAL HUNT—THE MANUFACTURE OF CANDLES—WORK INSIDE GRANITE HOUSE—THE TWO BRIDGES—RETURN FROM A VISIT TO THE OYSTER BED—WHAT HERBERT FINDS IN HIS POCKET.

The winter season really began with this month of June, which corresponds to the month of December in the northern hemisphere. It opened with showers and squalls, which followed one another without respite. The tenants of Granite House could now appreciate the advantages of a dwelling which the inclemency of the weather could not reach. The shelter of the Chimneys would have been quite insufficient against the rigors of a winter, and it was to be feared that the high tides, driven by the winds from the open sea, might break into it again. Cyrus Smith even took some precautions against this event, in order to preserve, as far as possible, the forge and the furnaces which were set up there.

During the whole of this month of June the time was employed in various works, which excluded neither hunting nor fishing, and the stores of the larder could be abundantly kept up. Pencroff proposed, as soon as he should have leisure, to set traps, from which he expected the greatest good. He had made snares of woody fibre, and there was not a day when the warren did not furnish its contingent of rodents. Neb spent nearly all his time in salting or smoking meat, which assured him excellent provisions.

The question of clothes was then very seriously discussed. The colonists had no other garments than those which they wore when the balloon threw them upon the island. These garments were warm and strong; they had taken extreme care of them, as well as of their linen, and they kept them perfectly clean; but all would soon need to be replaced. Besides, if the winter were severe, the colonists would suffer greatly from the cold.

On this point the ingenuity of Cyrus Smith was at fault. He had been obliged to provide for what was most pressing, to make the dwelling and to secure the food, and the cold might come upon him before the question of clothes had been solved. They must therefore resign themselves to pass this first winter without complaining too much. When the fine season came, they would make a serious hunt for those mouflons whose presence had been noticed during the exploration of Mount Franklin. Once the wool was gathered, the engineer would know how to make warm and strong stuffs… How? He would think about it.

"Well, we shall get off with toasting our shins at Granite House!" said Pencroff. "Fuel is plentiful, and there is no reason to spare it."

"Besides," answered Gideon Spilett, "Lincoln Island is not situated in a very high latitude, and it is probable that the winters here are not severe. Did you not tell us, Cyrus, that this thirty-fifth parallel corresponded to that of Spain in the other hemisphere?"

"No doubt," replied the engineer, "but some winters are very cold in Spain! Snow and ice, nothing is wanting there, and Lincoln Island may be as severely tried. However, it is an island, and, as such, I hope that the temperature will be more moderate."

"And why, Mr. Smith?" asked Herbert.

"Because the sea, my boy, may be considered as an immense reservoir, in which the heat of summer is stored up. When winter comes, it gives this heat back; and that assures to the regions near the oceans a mean temperature, less high in summer, but less low in winter."

"We shall see," answered Pencroff. "I ask leave not to trouble myself any further about the cold there will be or will not be. What is certain is that the days are short already, and the evenings long. Suppose we treated a little of the question of lighting."

"Nothing is easier," answered Cyrus Smith.

"To treat?" asked the sailor.

"To solve."

"And when shall we begin?"

"To-morrow, by getting up a seal hunt."

"To make tallow candles?"

"Fie, Pencroff! Stearine candles."

Such was, in fact, the engineer's project; a project that could be carried out, since he had lime and sulphuric acid, and the amphibians of the islet would furnish him with the fat necessary for the manufacture.

It was now the 4th of June. It was Pentecost Sunday, and it was unanimously agreed to observe this feast. All work was suspended, and prayers rose toward Heaven. But these prayers were now thanksgivings. The colonists of Lincoln Island were no longer the miserable castaways thrown upon the islet. They no longer asked; they gave thanks.

The next day, the 5th of June, in rather uncertain weather, they set out for the islet. They were again obliged to take advantage of low water to ford the channel. On this subject it was agreed that they would build, after a fashion, a canoe, which would make communication easier. It would also allow them to ascend the Mercy, at the time of the great exploration of the southwest of the island, which was put off till the first fine days.

The seals were numerous, and the hunters, armed with their iron-tipped spears, easily killed half a dozen. Neb and Pencroff skinned them, and brought back to Granite House only their fat and their skins, these skins being intended for the manufacture of strong shoes.

The result of this hunt was this: about three hundred pounds of fat, which were to be used entirely in the manufacture of the candles.

The operation was extremely simple, and, if it did not give absolutely perfect products, they were at least fit for use. Had Cyrus Smith had at his disposal only sulphuric acid, he could, by heating this acid with the neutral fatty bodies—in this case the fat of the seals—have isolated the glycerine. Then, from the new combination, he would easily have separated the olein, the margarin and the stearin, by employing boiling water. But, in order to simplify the operation, he preferred to saponify the fat by means of lime. He thus obtained a calcareous soap, easy to decompose by sulphuric acid, which precipitated the lime in the state of sulphate and set the fatty acids free.

Of these three acids, the oleic, the margaric and the stearic, the first, being liquid, was driven out by sufficient pressure. As to the other two, they formed the very substance which was to serve for the moulding of the candles.

The operation did not last more than twenty-four hours. The wicks, after several trials, were made of vegetable fibres; and, dipped in the liquefied substance, they formed real stearine candles, moulded by hand, which lacked nothing but bleaching and polishing. They did not offer, no doubt, that advantage which wicks impregnated with boracic acid possess, of vitrifying as they burn and of being entirely consumed. But Cyrus Smith having made a handsome pair of snuffers, these candles were greatly appreciated during the evenings in Granite House.

Throughout this month there was no lack of work in the interior of the new dwelling. The joiners had plenty to do. The tools, which were very rudimentary, were improved. They were also completed.

Scissors, among other things, were made, and the colonists were at last able to cut their hair, and, if not to shave their beards, at least to trim them to their fancy. Herbert had none, Neb had scarcely any; but their companions were bristling with them in a way that justified the making of the said scissors.

The manufacture of a handsaw, of the kind called fox-tail saws, cost infinite pains; but at last they obtained an instrument which, vigorously handled, could divide the woody fibres of the timber. They made, therefore, tables, seats and wardrobes, which furnished the principal rooms, and bedsteads, whose whole bedding consisted of mattresses of zostera. The kitchen, with its shelves, on which stood the earthenware utensils, its brick stove, and its stone sink, had a very good appearance. And Neb worked in it gravely, as if he had been in a chemist's laboratory.

But the joiners were soon obliged to give place to the carpenters. In fact, the new outlet, made by blasting, rendered necessary the construction of two little bridges, one on Prospect Plateau, the other on the beach itself. For now the plateau and the beach were cut across by a watercourse, which must of necessity be crossed whenever they wished to reach the north of the island. To avoid it, the colonists would have been obliged to make a considerable detour, and to go up westward beyond the sources of Red Creek. The simplest thing, therefore, was to set up, on the plateau and on the beach, two little bridges, from twenty to twenty-five feet long, of which a few trees, merely squared with the axe, made all the framework. It was the work of a few days. The bridges once built, Neb and Pencroff took advantage of them to go as far as the oyster bed which had been discovered off the dunes. They had dragged with them a sort of rough cart, which replaced the old hurdle, really too inconvenient; and they brought back some thousands of oysters. These were quickly acclimatized among the rocks, which formed so many natural parks at the mouth of the Mercy. The mollusks were of excellent quality, and the colonists consumed them almost daily.

As may be seen, Lincoln Island, though its inhabitants had explored only a very small portion of it, already supplied almost all their wants. And it was probable that, searched into its most secret recesses, over all that wooded region which stretched from the Mercy to Reptile End, it would lavish new treasures upon them.

One privation alone still cost the colonists of Lincoln Island dear. They lacked neither nitrogenous food nor the vegetable products which were to temper its use. The woody roots of the dragon trees, submitted to fermentation, gave them an acidulated drink, a sort of beer much preferable to pure water. They had even made sugar, without canes or beets, by collecting that liquor which the "acer saccharinum" distills, a kind of maple of the family of the acerineæ, which flourishes in all the temperate zones, and of which the island possessed a great number. They made a very agreeable tea by using the monardas brought from the warren. Lastly, they had salt in abundance, the only one of the mineral products which enters into food… but bread was wanting.

Perhaps, in time, the colonists might replace this food by some equivalent, sago flour or the starch of the breadfruit tree. It was possible, indeed, that the forests of the south might count these precious trees among their kinds; but up to that time they had not been met with.

However, Providence was, on this occasion, to come directly to the help of the colonists, in an infinitesimal proportion, it is true. But, after all, Cyrus Smith, with all his intelligence, all his ingenuity, could never have produced that which, by the greatest chance, Herbert found one day in the lining of his vest, which he was busy mending.

On that day—it was raining in torrents—the colonists were assembled in the great hall of Granite House, when the lad suddenly cried out:—

"Look, Mr. Smith. A grain of wheat!"

And he showed his companions a grain, one single grain, which, from his pocket, that had a hole in it, had worked its way into the lining of his vest.

The presence of this grain was explained by the habit which Herbert had, when at Richmond, of feeding some wood pigeons which Pencroff had given him.

"A grain of wheat?" said the engineer quickly.

"Yes, Mr. Smith, but one only, only one!"

"Eh! my boy," cried Pencroff, smiling, "we are a great deal better off, upon my word! What could we ever do with a single grain of wheat?"

"We will make bread of it," answered Cyrus Smith.

"Bread, cakes, tarts!" replied the sailor. "Come! The bread that this grain of wheat will give us will not choke us very soon!"

Herbert, attaching but little importance to his discovery, was about to throw away the grain in question. But Cyrus Smith took it, examined it, found that it was in good condition, and, looking the sailor full in the face:—

"Pencroff," he asked quietly, "do you know how many ears of wheat one grain can produce?"

"One, I suppose!" answered the sailor, surprised at the question.

"Ten, Pencroff. And do you know how many grains an ear bears?"

"Upon my word, no."

"Eighty, on an average," said Cyrus Smith. "So, if we plant this grain, at the first harvest we shall reap eight hundred grains, which at the second will produce six hundred and forty thousand, at the third five hundred and twelve millions, at the fourth more than four hundred thousand millions of grains. That is the proportion."

Cyrus Smith's companions listened to him without answering. These figures astounded them. They were exact, nevertheless.

"Yes, my friends," continued the engineer. "Such are the arithmetical progressions of fruitful nature. And still, what is this multiplication of the grain of wheat, whose ear bears only eight hundred grains, compared with those poppy plants which bear thirty-two thousand seeds, with those tobacco plants which produce three hundred and sixty thousand? In a few years, but for the numerous causes of destruction which check their fertility, these plants would overrun the whole earth."

But the engineer had not finished his little examination.

"And now, Pencroff," he went on, "do you know how many bushels four hundred thousand millions of grains would make?"

"No," answered the sailor; "but what I do know is that I am nothing but a blockhead!"

"Well, it would make more than three millions, at a hundred and thirty thousand to the bushel, Pencroff."

"Three millions!" cried Pencroff.

"Three millions."

"In four years?"

"In four years," answered Cyrus Smith, "and even in two years, if, as I hope, we can, in this latitude, obtain two harvests a year."

To this, according to his custom, Pencroff did not think he could reply otherwise than by a tremendous hurrah.

"So, Herbert," added the engineer, "you have made there a discovery of extreme importance to us. Everything, my friends, everything can serve us in the condition in which we are. I beg you, do not forget it."

"No, Mr. Smith, no, we will not forget it," answered Pencroff; "and if ever I find one of those seeds of tobacco, which multiply by three hundred and sixty thousand, I assure you I will not throw it to the wind! And now, do you know what remains for us to do?"

"It remains for us to plant this grain," answered Herbert.

"Yes," added Gideon Spilett, "and with all the regard which is due to it, for it carries in it our harvests to come."

"Provided it grows!" cried the sailor.

"It will grow," answered Cyrus Smith.

It was now the 20th of June. The moment was therefore favorable for sowing this single and precious grain of wheat. It was at first proposed to plant it in a pot; but, on reflection, they resolved to trust more frankly to nature, and to commit it to the earth. This was done the same day, and it is needless to add that every precaution was taken that the operation might succeed.

The weather having cleared a little, the colonists climbed the heights above Granite House. There, on the plateau, they chose a spot well sheltered from the wind, and on which the midday sun would pour all its heat. The spot was cleared, carefully weeded, and even dug over, to drive out the insects or worms. A bed of good earth, improved with a little lime, was laid upon it; it was surrounded with a palisade; then the grain was pressed into the damp bed.

Did it not seem as if these colonists were laying the first stone of an edifice? It reminded Pencroff of the day on which he had lighted his only match, and of all the care he had brought to that operation. But this time the matter was more serious. In fact, the castaways would always have succeeded in procuring fire, by some process or other; but no human power could make them this grain of wheat again, if, by misfortune, it should perish!
