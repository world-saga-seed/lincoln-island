# The Wild Man of Tabor Island (final)

CHAPTER XXXVI.

THE INVENTORY—THE NIGHT—SOME LETTERS—THE SEARCH CONTINUED—PLANTS AND ANIMALS—HERBERT IN DANGER—ABOARD—THE DEPARTURE—BAD WEATHER—A GLIMMER OF INSTINCT—LOST AT SEA—A TIMELY LIGHT.

Pencroff, Spilett and Herbert stood silent in the darkness. Then the former gave a loud call. There was no answer. The sailor then struck a light and lit a twig, and the light illuminated for a moment a small room, seemingly quite deserted. At the far end was a rough chimney, containing some cold cinders and an armful of dry wood. Pencroff threw the lighted twig into it, and the wood crackled and gave out a bright light.

The sailor and his companions thereupon discovered a bed in disorder, its damp and yellowed covers proving that it had been long unused; in the corner of the fireplace were two rusty kettles and an overturned pot; a clothes-press with some sailors’ clothing, partially moulded; on the table a tin plate, and a Bible, eaten away by the dampness; in a corner some tools, a shovel, a mattock, a pick, two shot guns, one of which was broken; on a shelf was a barrel of powder still untouched, a barrel of lead, and a number of boxes of caps. All were covered with a thick coating of dust, which long years, perhaps, had accumulated.

“There is no one here,” said the reporter.

“Not a soul,” replied Pencroff.

“This room has not been occupied in a long time,” observed Herbert.

“Yes, since a very long time,” replied the reporter.

“Mr. Spilett,” said Pencroff, “I think that instead of going on board we had better stay here all night.”

“You are right, Pencroff,” replied Spilett, “and if the proprietor returns, well! he will not complain, perhaps, at finding the place occupied!”

“He won’t come back, though,” said the sailor, shaking his head.

“Do you think he has left the island?” asked the reporter.

“If he had left the island he would have taken his arms and his tools with him,” replied Pencroff. “You know how much a shipwrecked person would be attached to these objects, which are the last relics of the wreck. No, no,” repeated the sailor, in the tone of a man perfectly convinced; “no, he has not left the island! If he had escaped in a boat of his own making, he would still less have abandoned these things, which are of the first necessity. No, he is on the island!”

“Alive?” asked Herbert.

“Alive or dead. But if he is dead he could not have buried himself, I suppose,” replied Pencroff, “and we will at least find his remains!”

It was therefore agreed to pass the night in this abandoned house, and a supply of wood in the corner gave them the means of heating it sufficiently. The door having been closed, the three explorers, seated upon a bench, spoke little, but remained deep in thought. They were in the mood to suppose anything, as well as to expect anything, and they listened eagerly for any sound from without. If the door had suddenly opened and a man had stood before them, they would not have been much surprised, in spite of all the evidence of desolation throughout the house; and their hands were ready to clasp the hands of this man, of this shipwrecked one, of this unknown friend whose friends awaited him.

But no sound was heard, the door did not open, and the hours passed by.

The night seemed interminable to the sailor and his companions. Herbert, alone, slept for two hours, as at his age, sleep is a necessity. All were anxious to renew the search of the day before, and to explore the innermost recesses of the islet. Pencroff’s conclusions were perfectly just, and it was almost certain that, since the house was abandoned while the tools, the utensils and the arms were still in it, its occupant had died. They determined, therefore, to search for the remains of its inhabitant, and to give them at least Christian burial.

As soon as it was daylight they began to examine the house. It was prettily situated on the farther slope of a small hill, which five or six magnificent gum trees sheltered. Before its front, through the trees, the axe had cleared a large space, giving a view over the sea. A small lawn, surrounded by a dilapidated wooden fence, led to the shore, on the left of which opened the mouth of the little stream. The house had been built of planks, and it was easy to see that these planks had come from the hull or the deck of a ship. It seemed likely, therefore, that a disabled ship had been thrown upon the coast of the island, that at least one of the crew had been saved, and that this man, having tools at his disposal, had built this house from the wreck. This became still more evident when Spilett, after going round the dwelling, saw on one of the planks—probably one of those which had formed the bulwarks of the wrecked ship—these letters, already half effaced:—

BR ... TAN ... A.

“Britannia,” exclaimed Pencroff, who had been called by the reporter to look at it; “that is a common name among ships, and I cannot say whether it is English or American!”

“It does not matter, Pencroff!”

“It don’t matter, indeed,” replied the sailor; “to whatever country the survivor of her crew belongs, we will save him, if he is still alive. But before we begin our search again let us first go back to the Bonadventure!”

Pencroff had been seized with a sort of anxiety about his sloop. Supposing the island was inhabited, and some one had taken it—but he shrugged his shoulders at this unlikely thought. Nevertheless the sailor was not unwilling to go on board to breakfast. The route already marked was not more than a mile in length, and they started on their walk, looking carefully about them in the woods and underbrush, through which ran hundreds of pigs and goats.

Twenty minutes after leaving the house, the party came again in sight of the eastern coast of the island, and of the Bonadventure, held by her anchor, which bit deep into the sand. Pencroff could not keep back a sigh of satisfaction.

After all, this boat was his baby, and it is a father’s right to be often anxious beyond reason.

All went on board and breakfasted, so as not to want anything before a late dinner; then the exploration was renewed, and conducted with the utmost carefulness. As it was very likely that the solitary inhabitant of this island was dead, the party sought rather to find his remains than any traces of him living. But their search was vain, and for half the day they explored to no purpose the clumps of trees which covered the islet. They were obliged to admit, then, that if the castaway was dead, no trace of his body now remained, and that some wild beast had doubtless devoured it to the last bone.

“We will leave to-morrow at daylight,” said Pencroff to his companions, who towards 2 o’clock were resting for a few moments in the shade of a group of pines.

“I think we need not hesitate to take those things which belonged to him?” queried Herbert.

“I think not,” answered Spilett; “and these arms and tools will add materially to the stock at Granite House. If I am not mistaken, the store of powder and lead is considerable.”

“Yes,” replied Pencroff, “but we must not forget to capture one or two pairs of these pigs, of which Lincoln Island has none—”

“Nor to gather this seed,” added Herbert, “which will give us all the vegetables of the old and the new continents.”

“Perhaps it would be better to spend another day on Tabor Island, in order to get together everything that may be useful to us,” suggested the reporter.

“No, Mr. Spilett,” replied the sailor; “I will ask you to leave to-morrow at daybreak. The wind seems to be shifting to the west, and, after having had a fair wind coming, we shall have a fair wind going back.”

“Then don’t let us lose any time,” said Herbert, rising.

“We will not,” replied Pencroff. “Herbert, you get the seed, which you know better than we do. Meanwhile Mr. Spilett and I will chase the pigs, and although we haven’t Top, I hope we will catch some.”

Herbert, therefore, followed the path which led to the cultivated part of the island, while the others plunged at once into the forest. Many pigs fled before them, and these animals, singularly agile, did not seem in the humor to let themselves be approached. However, after half an hour’s chasing the hunters had captured a couple which had made their lair in a thick copse, when cries were heard a few hundred paces to the north of the islet, mingled with horrible hoarse sounds, having nothing human in them. Pencroff and Spilett sprang to their feet, and the pigs took advantage of the movement to escape, just as the sailor was getting ready cords to bind them.

“It is Herbert’s voice!” cried the reporter.

“Hurry!” cried the sailor, as the two ran with their utmost speed towards the place from whence the cries came.

They had need to hasten, for at a turn in the path, near a clearing, they saw the lad prostrate beneath a wild creature, doubtless a gigantic ape, who was about to do him harm.

To throw themselves on this monster and pinion him to the ground in his turn, dragging Herbert away, and then holding him fast, was the work of a moment for Pencroff and Spilett. The sailor had herculean strength. Spilett, too, was very muscular, and, in spite of the resistance of the monster, it was bound so that it could not move.

“You are not wounded, Herbert?” asked Spilett.

“No, oh no.”

“Ah! if it had hurt you, this ape—” cried Pencroff.

“But he is not an ape!” cried Herbert.

At these words Pencroff and Spilett looked again at the singular being lying on the ground. In fact, it was not an ape, but a human being—a man! But what a man! He was a savage, in all the horrible acceptation of the word; and, what was more frightful, he seemed to have fallen to the last degree of brutishness.

Bristling hair, tangled beard descending to his breast, his body almost naked, save for a rag of blanket about his loins, wild eyes, enormous hands, nails immeasurably long, mahogany-colored skin, feet as hard as if they had been made of horn; such was the miserable creature which it was, nevertheless, necessary to call a man. But one might well question whether this body still contained a soul, or whether the low, brutish instinct alone survived.

“Are you perfectly sure that this is a man, or that it has been one?” questioned Pencroff of the reporter.

“Alas! there can be no doubt of it,” replied Spilett.

“Can he be the person shipwrecked?” asked Herbert.

“Yes,” responded the reporter, “but the poor creature is no longer human.”

Spilett was right. Evidently, if the castaway had ever been civilized, isolation had made him a savage, and worse, perhaps, a real creature of the woods. He gave utterance to hoarse sounds, from between teeth which were as sharp as those of carnivorous animals, and fit now only to grind raw flesh. Memory had doubtless long ago left him, and he had long since forgotten the use of arms and tools, and even how to make a fire. One could see that he was active and supple, but that his physical qualities had developed to the detriment of his moral qualities.

Spilett spoke to him, but he did not appear to understand, or even to hear; and yet, looking him well in the eye, the reporter thought he could see that all reason was not extinct in him. Nevertheless, the prisoner did not struggle or strive to break his bonds. Was he cowed by the presence of these men, whom he had once resembled? Was there in some corner of his brain a flitting remembrance which drew him towards humanity? Free, would he have fled or would he have remained? They did not know, and they did not put him to the proof. After having looked at the miserable creature with extreme attention, Spilett said:—

“Whatever he is, whatever he has been, and whatever he may become, it is our duty to take him with us to Lincoln Island.”

“Oh yes, yes,” exclaimed Herbert, “and perhaps we can, with care, restore to him some degree of intelligence.”

“The soul never dies,” answered the reporter, “and it would be a great thing to bring back this creature of God’s making from his brutishness.”

Pencroff shook his head doubtfully.

“It is necessary to try at all events,” said the reporter, “humanity requires it of us.”

It was, indeed, their duty as civilized and Christian beings. All three understood it, and they well knew that Smith would approve of their course.

“Shall we leave him bound?” inquired the sailor.

“Perhaps if we unfasten his feet he will walk,” said Herbert.

“Well, let us try,” replied the sailor.

And the cords binding the creature’s legs were loosened, although his arms were kept firmly bound. He rose of his own accord, without manifesting any desire to escape. His tearless eyes darted sharp glances upon the three men who marched beside him, and nothing denoted that he remembered being or having been like them. A continuous whistling escaped from his lips, and his aspect was wild, but he made no resistance.

By the advice of the reporter, the poor wretch was taken back to his house, where, perhaps, the sight of the objects which had belonged to him might make some impression upon him! Perhaps a single spark would suffice to revive his darkened mind, to rekindle his extinguished soul!

The house was near by, and in a few minutes they were there; but the prisoner recognized nothing—he seemed to have lost consciousness of everything. What could be conjectured from this degree of brutishness into which the miserable being had fallen, unless that his imprisonment on the island was already of long date, and that, having come here a reasoning being, his isolation had reduced him to this state?

The reporter thought that perhaps the sight of fire might affect him, and in a moment one of those lovely flames which attract even animals lit up the fireplace. The sight of this flame seemed at first to attract the attention of the unfortunate man, but soon he drew back, and his unconscious look died out. Evidently, for the present at least, there was nothing to do but take him aboard the Bonadventure, which was accordingly done. He was left in charge of Pencroff, while Herbert and Spilett returned to the island to finish their work, and some hours after came back to the shore, bringing the arms and implements, a harvest of garden seeds, some game, and two pairs of pigs which they had caught. Everything was put on board, and the sloop rode ready to hoist anchor as soon as the next morning’s tide would permit.

The prisoner had been placed in the forward cabin, where he lay calm, quiet, deaf and mute at once. Pencroff offering him some cooked meat to eat, he pushed it away, as it doubtless no longer suited him; and, indeed, on being shown one of the ducks which Herbert had killed, he pounced on it with bestial avidity and devoured it.

“You think he’ll be himself again?” asked the sailor, shaking his head.

“Perhaps,” replied the reporter. “It is not impossible that our attentions will react on him, since it is the isolation that has done this; and he will be alone no longer.”

“The poor fellow has doubtless been this way for a long time,” said Herbert.

“Perhaps so,” replied Spilett.

“How old do you think he is?” asked the lad.

“That is hard to say,” replied the reporter, “as it is impossible to see his features under the thick beard which covers his face; but he is no longer young, and I should say he was at least fifty years old.”

“Have you noticed, Mr. Spilett, how deeply his eyes are sunk under their brows?” asked the lad.

“Yes, Herbert, but I add that they are more human than one would suspect from his general appearance.”

“Well, we will see,” said Pencroff; “and I am curious to have Mr. Smith’s opinion of our savage. We went to find a human being, and we are bringing back a monster. Any how, one does what he can!”

The night passed, and whether the prisoner slept or not no one knew, but at any rate, although he had been unbound, he did not move. He was like one of those beasts that in the first moments of their capture submit, and to whom the rage returns later.

At daybreak the next day, the 15th of October, the change in the weather was as Pencroff had predicted. The wind hauled round to the northwest and favored the return of the Bonadventure; but at the same time it had freshened, so as to make the sailing more difficult. At 5 o’clock the anchor was raised, Pencroff took a reef in the mainsail and headed east-north-east, so as to sail directly towards Lincoln Island.

The first day passed without incident. The prisoner rested quietly in the forward cabin, and, as he had once been a sailor, the motion of the sea produced upon him a sort of salutary reaction. Did it recall to him some remembrance of his former occupation? At least he rested tranquil, astonished rather than dejected.

On the 16th the wind freshened considerably, coming round more to the north, and therefore in a direction less favorable to the course of the Bonadventure, which bounded over the waves. Pencroff was soon obliged to hold her nearer to the wind, and without saying so, he began to be anxious at the state of the sea, which broke violently over the bow of his boat. Certainly, unless the wind moderated, it would take longer to reach Lincoln Island than it had taken to reach Tabor Island.

On the morning of the 17th they had been forty-eight hours out, and yet nothing indicated they were in the neighborhood of Lincoln Island. It was, moreover, impossible to reckon their course, or even to estimate the distance traversed, as the direction and the speed had been too irregular. Twenty-four hours later there was still no land in view. The wind was dead ahead, and an ugly sea running. It was necessary to work the sails rapidly, for the seas swept over them, to take in reefs, and often to change tack, making short boards. It even happened, on the 18th, that the Bonadventure was entirely covered by a wave, and had not the crew been lashed to the deck, they would have been swept overboard.

On this occasion Pencroff and his companions, very busy in clearing things away, received an unhoped-for assistance from the prisoner, who sprang from the hatchway as if his sailor instinct had returned to him, and breaking the rail by a vigorous blow with a spar, enabled the water on the deck to flow off more freely. Then, the boat cleared, without having said a word, he returned to his cabin.

Pencroff, Spilett, and Herbert, utterly stupefied, had let him act.

Nevertheless, the situation was bad, and the sailor had cause to believe himself lost upon this vast sea, without the possibility of regaining his course. The night of the 18th to the 19th was dark and cold. But about 11 o’clock the wind lulled, the sea fell, and the sloop, less tossed about, moved more rapidly. Besides, she had held the sea marvellously. Neither Pencroff, nor Spilett, nor Herbert thought of taking even an hour’s sleep. They kept watch with extreme care, as either Lincoln Island must be near at hand and they would discover it at daybreak, or the sloop, carried by the currents, had drifted to leeward, and it would be next to impossible to rectify the direction.

Pencroff, anxious to the last degree, did not, however, despair, for he had a soul of strong temper; and, seated at the helm, he tried obstinately to see through the thick darkness around him. Towards 2 o’clock he suddenly started up, crying:—“A fire! a fire!” It was indeed a bright light appearing twenty miles to the northeast. Lincoln Island was there, and this light, evidently lit by Smith, indicated the direction to be followed.

Pencroff, who had been heading much too far towards the north, changed his course, and steered directly towards the light, which gleamed above the horizon like a star of the first magnitude.
