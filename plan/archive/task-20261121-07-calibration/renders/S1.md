# S1: I.4 ¶9

¶9

The high wall, as has been said, rose to a height of three hundred feet, but the block was solid throughout, and, even at its base, scarcely licked by the sea, it did not present the smallest fissure which could serve as a temporary dwelling. It was a perpendicular wall, of a very hard granite, which the waves had never eaten away. Towards the summit fluttered a whole world of aquatic birds, and particularly various species of the order of palmipedes, with long, compressed, and pointed beaks,—very clamorous birds, little frightened by the presence of man, who, for the first time, doubtless, thus disturbed their solitude. Among these palmipedes Pencroff recognized several skuas, a sort of gull to which the name of stercoraries is sometimes given, and also some small voracious sea-mews, which nested in the crevices of the granite. A gun-shot, fired into the midst of this swarm of birds, would have brought down a great number; but, to fire a gun-shot, one must have a gun, and neither Pencroff nor Harbert had one. Besides, these sea-mews and skuas are scarcely eatable, and even their eggs have a detestable taste.
