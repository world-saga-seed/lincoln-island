# S2: I.6 ¶41 and its note

¶41

Pencroff had found in the grass half a dozen grouse's nests, each holding from two to three eggs. He took great care not to touch these nests, to which their owners could not fail to return. It was around them that he resolved to stretch his lines,—not snare-lines, but true lines with hooks. He led Harbert to some distance from the nests, and there he prepared his singular tackle with the care which a disciple of Isaac Walton[n1] would have brought to it. Harbert followed this work with an interest easy to understand, though he doubted of its success. The lines were made of slender creepers, fastened one to another, and from fifteen to twenty feet long. Large, very strong thorns, with curved points, furnished by a bush of dwarf acacias, were tied to the ends of the creepers by way of hooks. As for the bait, some large red worms which were crawling on the ground served the purpose.

[n1] A celebrated author of a treatise on angling.
