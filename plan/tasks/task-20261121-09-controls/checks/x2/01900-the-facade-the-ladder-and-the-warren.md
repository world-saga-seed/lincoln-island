CHAPTER XIX.

SMITH'S PLAN—THE FRONT OF GRANITE HOUSE—THE ROPE-LADDER—PENCROFF'S DREAMS—THE AROMATIC HERBS—A NATURAL WARREN—WATER BROUGHT TO THE NEW DWELLING—THE VIEW FROM THE WINDOWS OF GRANITE HOUSE.

On the following day, the 22d of May, they began the work of fitting up the new dwelling. The colonists were, indeed, impatient to exchange their insufficient shelter at the Chimneys for this vast and healthful retreat, hollowed in the solid rock, and protected from the waters of the sea and from the sky. The Chimneys were not, however, to be entirely abandoned, for the engineer intended to make a workshop of them for the heavier labors.

Smith's first care was to determine at what precise point the front of Granite House lay. He went down to the beach at the foot of the enormous wall. As the pick, slipping from the reporter's hands, must have fallen perpendicularly, he had only to find the pick in order to know the spot where the hole had been pierced in the granite.

The pick was easily found, and, sure enough, a hole opened in a perpendicular line above the point where it had stuck in the sand, about eighty feet above the beach. Some rock-pigeons were already flying in and out of this narrow opening. It really seemed as if Granite House had been discovered for their benefit!

The engineer intended to divide the right-hand portion of the cavern into several chambers, with an entrance-passage before them, and to light it by means of five windows and a door, pierced in the front. Pencroff quite approved of the five windows, but he could not see the use of the door, since the old outlet furnished a natural staircase, by which it would always be easy to get into Granite House.

"My friend," answered Smith, "if it is easy for us to reach our dwelling by the outlet, it will be equally easy for others besides ourselves. I mean, on the contrary, to stop up the outlet at its mouth, to close it hermetically, and even, if need be, to conceal the entrance completely, by damming the lake and raising its waters."

"And how shall we get in?" asked the sailor.

"By an outside ladder," replied Smith; "a rope-ladder, which, when it is drawn up, will make access to our dwelling impossible."

"But why so many precautions?" said Pencroff. "So far the animals have not seemed very dangerous. And as for natives, our island has none!"

"Are you quite sure of that, Pencroff?" asked the engineer, looking at the sailor.

"Of course we shall not be sure of it until we have explored every part of it," answered Pencroff.

"Yes," said Smith, "for as yet we know only a small portion of it. But at any rate, if we have no enemies within, they may come from without, for these Pacific waters are bad waters. Let us take our precautions, then, against every chance."

Smith spoke wisely; and Pencroff, without making any further objection, prepared to carry out his orders.

The front of Granite House was therefore to be lighted by five windows and a door, which would serve what was properly the "apartment," and by a wide bay-window and some bull's-eyes, which would let the light pour in abundantly upon that marvelous nave which was to serve as their great hall. This front, eighty feet above the ground, looked toward the east, and the rising sun greeted it with its first rays. It occupied that portion of the curtain of rock which lay between the projecting angle over the mouth of the Mercy, and a line drawn perpendicularly above the heap of rocks which formed the Chimneys. Thus the bad winds, that is to say, those from the northeast, struck it only obliquely, for it was sheltered by the very direction of the projection. Moreover, until the window-sashes could be made, the engineer intended to close the openings with thick shutters, which would keep out both wind and rain, and which he could conceal if necessary.

The first work, then, was to cut out these openings. To use the pick upon this hard rock would have been too slow, and it is known that Smith was a man of bold measures. He still had a certain quantity of nitro-glycerine at his disposal, and he employed it to good purpose. The force of the explosive was properly confined, and, under its stroke, the granite was broken in at the very places chosen by the engineer. Then the pick and the mattock finished the pointed, ogival outline of the five windows, the bay, the bull's-eyes, and the door; they trued the frames, whose outlines were finished somewhat fancifully; and, a few days after the work had begun, Granite House was amply lighted by the morning sun, which penetrated even to its most secret recesses.

According to the plan settled upon by Smith, the apartment was to be divided into five compartments looking out upon the sea: on the right, an entry, served by a door at which the ladder would end; next, a kitchen-room, thirty feet wide; a dining-room, forty feet wide; a sleeping-room of equal width; and, lastly, a "spare room," which Pencroff insisted upon, and which adjoined the great hall.

These rooms, or rather this suite of rooms, which formed the apartment of Granite House, were not to occupy the whole depth of the cavern. They were to be served by a corridor running between them and a long store-room, in which tools, provisions, and reserve stores would find ample space. All the products gathered on the island, those of the flora as well as those of the fauna, would there be kept in excellent condition, and quite safe from damp. There was no lack of room, and every object could be methodically arranged. Besides, the colonists still had at their disposal the little grotto above the great cavern, which would serve as a garret for the new dwelling.

This plan once settled, it only remained to carry it out. The miners therefore became brick-makers again; then the bricks were brought and laid at the foot of Granite House.

Until now, Smith and his companions had entered the cavern only by the old outlet. This mode of communication obliged them, first, to climb Prospect Plateau, by making a detour along the bank of the river; then to descend two hundred feet through the passage; and then to climb as many again whenever they wished to return to the plateau. Hence there was great loss of time and much fatigue. Smith therefore determined to proceed without delay to the making of a strong rope-ladder, which, once drawn up, would render the entrance of Granite House absolutely inaccessible.

This ladder was made with extreme care, and its uprights, formed of the fibres of the "curry-jonc," twisted by means of a winder, had the strength of a stout cable. As for the rungs, they were furnished by a sort of red cedar, with light and tough branches; and the whole contrivance was put together by Pencroff with a master hand.

Other ropes were made in the same way from vegetable fibres, and a rough sort of tackle was fixed at the door. In this way the bricks could easily be hoisted to the level of Granite House. The carrying of materials was thus very much simplified, and the arrangement of the interior, properly so called, was begun at once. There was no lack of lime, and some thousands of bricks were at hand, ready for use. The framework of the partitions, which was very rude, was easily set up; and in a very short time the apartment was divided into chambers and store-room, according to the plan agreed upon.

These various labors went on rapidly under the direction of the engineer, who himself handled the hammer and the trowel. No kind of handiwork was strange to Smith, and he thus set an example to companions who were intelligent and zealous. They worked with confidence, and even gayly, for Pencroff always had a joke ready; now carpenter, now rope-maker, now mason, he communicated his good humor to the whole little company. His faith in the engineer was absolute; nothing could have shaken it. He believed him capable of undertaking anything and of succeeding in everything. The question of clothes and shoes—a serious question, certainly—that of lighting during the winter nights, the cultivation of the fertile parts of the island, the transformation of this wild flora into a civilized one—all seemed easy to him, with Smith's help, and everything would be done in its time. He dreamed of canalized rivers to carry the riches of the soil, of quarries and mines to be worked, of machines fit for every kind of industry, of railroads—yes, railroads!—whose network would certainly one day cover Lincoln Island.

The engineer let Pencroff talk. He did not check the exaggerations of this brave heart. He knew how contagious confidence is; he even smiled to hear him, and said nothing of the anxieties which the future sometimes caused him. For in this part of the Pacific, out of the track of ships, he might well fear that help would never come. It was upon themselves, then, and upon themselves alone, that the colonists must depend; for the distance from Lincoln Island to any other land was so great, that to venture out in a boat, necessarily of indifferent construction, would be a serious and perilous matter.

"But," as the sailor said, "they outstripped by a hundred cubits the Robinsons of former days, for whom everything was a miracle to be wrought."

And, in fact, they "knew"; and the man who "knows" succeeds where others would vegetate and inevitably perish.

During these labors Herbert distinguished himself. He was intelligent and active; he understood quickly, and carried out well; and Smith grew more and more attached to the boy. Herbert felt for the engineer a warm and respectful affection. Pencroff saw clearly the close sympathy which was growing up between the two, but he was not at all jealous of it.

Neb was Neb. He was what he would always be—courage, zeal, devotion, self-denial personified. He had the same faith in his master as Pencroff, but he showed it less noisily. When the sailor grew enthusiastic, Neb always had the air of answering, "But nothing could be more natural." He and Pencroff were very fond of each other, and had soon fallen into the familiar "thou" of old friends.

As for Gideon Spilett, he took his share of the common labor, and was not the most awkward at it—which always rather astonished the sailor. A "journalist" clever, not only at understanding everything, but at doing everything!

The ladder was finally put in place on the 28th of May. It had no less than a hundred rungs over the perpendicular height of eighty feet which it covered. Smith had fortunately been able to divide it into two parts, by taking advantage of an overhanging ledge of the wall, which projected some forty feet above the ground. This ledge, carefully leveled with the pick, became a sort of landing, to which they fastened the first ladder, whose swing was thus diminished by half, and which could be drawn up by a rope to the level of Granite House. As for the second ladder, it was made fast both at its lower end, which rested upon the ledge, and at its upper end, which was secured to the door itself. In this way the ascent became considerably easier. Besides, Smith intended later to set up a hydraulic lift, which would spare the inhabitants of Granite House all fatigue and loss of time.

The colonists quickly grew used to the ladder. They were nimble and skilful, and Pencroff, as a sailor accustomed to run up the ratlines of the shrouds, was able to give them lessons. But he had to give lessons to Top as well. The poor dog, with his four legs, was not built for such exercise. But Pencroff was so zealous a teacher, that Top at last performed his ascents very creditably, and soon went up the ladder as his fellows commonly do in circuses. How proud the sailor was of his pupil cannot be told. Nevertheless, more than once, Pencroff carried him up on his back, of which Top never complained.

It should be observed here that during these labors, which were carried on briskly, for the bad season was drawing near, the question of food had not been neglected. Every day the reporter and Herbert, who had now become regularly the purveyors of the colony, spent some hours in hunting. As yet they hunted only in Jacamar Wood, on the left of the river; for, having neither bridge nor boat, they had not yet crossed the Mercy. All those immense forests to which they had given the name of the Far West forests were therefore still unexplored. This important excursion was reserved for the first fine days of the next spring. But Jacamar Wood was well enough stocked with game; kangaroos and wild boars abounded there, and the hunters' iron-spiked spears, bow, and arrows did wonders. Moreover, Herbert discovered, toward the southwest angle of the lagoon, a natural warren, a sort of slightly damp meadow, covered with willows and with aromatic herbs which perfumed the air—thyme, wild thyme, basil, savory—all those fragrant species of the Labiatae family of which rabbits are so fond.

The reporter remarked that, since the table was laid for rabbits, it would be strange if the rabbits were wanting; and the two hunters explored the warren carefully. At any rate, it produced useful plants in abundance, and a naturalist would have had there the chance of studying many specimens of the vegetable kingdom. Herbert thus gathered a quantity of shoots of basil, rosemary, balm, betony, etc., which possess various medicinal properties, some pectoral, astringent, or febrifuge, others antispasmodic or antirheumatic. And when, later, Pencroff asked what was the use of all this harvest of herbs:—

"To take care of ourselves," answered the boy; "to doctor us when we are sick."

"Why should we be sick, since there are no doctors on the island?" replied Pencroff, very seriously.

To this there was no answer; but the boy went on with his harvest none the less, and it was very well received at Granite House. All the more so, because to these medicinal plants he was able to add a good quantity of Monarda didyma, which is known in North America by the name of "Oswego tea," and makes an excellent beverage.

At last, that day, after a careful search, the two hunters came upon the real site of the warren. The ground there was perforated like a skimmer.

"Burrows!" cried Herbert.

"Yes," answered the reporter, "I see them."

"But are they inhabited?"

"That is the question."

The question was not long in being answered. Almost at once, hundreds of little animals, like rabbits, scampered off in every direction, and so swiftly that even Top could not have outrun them. Hunters and dog ran in vain; these rodents easily escaped them. But the reporter was quite determined not to leave the spot until he had captured at least half a dozen of these quadrupeds. He wished first of all to stock the larder with them, leaving the taming of those which might be caught later for another time. With a few snares set at the mouths of the burrows, the operation could not fail to succeed. But at the moment they had no snares, nor anything to make them of. They had, therefore, to resign themselves to visiting each hole, poking into it with a stick, and accomplishing by dint of patience what could not be done otherwise.

At last, after an hour's searching, four rodents were taken in their holes. They were rabbits, much like their European kindred, and are commonly known by the name of "American rabbits."

The game was therefore carried back to Granite House, and figured at the evening meal. The tenants of this warren were by no means to be despised, for they were delicious. This was a valuable resource for the colony, and one which seemed likely to be inexhaustible.

On the 31st of May the partitions were finished. It only remained to furnish the rooms, which would be the work of the long winter days. A fireplace was built in the first room, which served as the kitchen. The flue, which was to carry the smoke outside, gave the improvised stove-fitters some trouble. Smith thought it simplest to make it of brick-clay; and, as it was out of the question to carry it out through the plateau above, they pierced a hole in the granite over the window of the said kitchen, and to this hole the flue, slanting upward, was led, like that of a sheet-iron stove. Perhaps—indeed, without doubt—the chimney would smoke in the strong east winds which beat directly upon the front; but these winds were rare, and, besides, Master Neb, the cook, was not so very particular.

When these interior arrangements had been completed, the engineer set about stopping up the mouth of the old outlet which opened on the lake, so as to cut off all access by that way. Blocks of rock were rolled to the opening and strongly cemented. Smith did not yet carry out the plan he had formed of drowning this mouth beneath the waters of the lake, by raising them to their former level with a dam. He contented himself with concealing the obstruction by means of grass, shrubs, and brushwood, which were planted in the crevices of the rocks, and which the next spring would cause to grow luxuriantly.

He made use of the outlet, however, to bring a little stream of the fresh water of the lake to the new dwelling. A small cut, made below the level of the water, produced this result; and this tapping of a pure and unfailing source gave a supply of from twenty-five to thirty gallons* a day. Granite House, therefore, would never be in want of water.

At last all was finished; and it was high time, for the bad season was at hand. Thick shutters made it possible to close the windows of the front, until the engineer should have had time to make window-glass.

Gideon Spilett had very artistically arranged plants of various kinds, as well as long trailing grasses, in the projections of the rock around the windows; and in this way the openings were framed in a picturesque verdure, with a charming effect.

The inhabitants of this solid, healthful, and safe dwelling could not but be delighted with their work. From the windows their view extended over a boundless horizon, closed in on the north by the two Mandible Capes, and on the south by Claw Cape. All Union Bay spread out magnificently before them. Yes, these brave colonists had reason to be satisfied; and Pencroff was not sparing of his praises for what he humorously called "his apartment on the fifth floor above the entresol!"

*The gallon is equal to about four liters and a half.
