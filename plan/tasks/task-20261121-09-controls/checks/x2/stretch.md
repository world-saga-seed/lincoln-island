CHAPTER XIX.

SMITH'S PLAN—THE FRONT OF GRANITE HOUSE—THE ROPE-LADDER—PENCROFF'S DREAMS—THE AROMATIC HERBS—A NATURAL WARREN—WATER BROUGHT TO THE NEW DWELLING—THE VIEW FROM THE WINDOWS OF GRANITE HOUSE.

On the following day, the 22d of May, they began the work of fitting up the new dwelling. The colonists were, indeed, impatient to exchange their insufficient shelter at the Chimneys for this vast and healthful retreat, hollowed in the solid rock, and protected from the waters of the sea and from the sky. The Chimneys were not, however, to be entirely abandoned, for the engineer intended to make a workshop of them for the heavier labors.

Smith's first care was to determine at what precise point the front of Granite House lay. He went down to the beach at the foot of the enormous wall. As the pick, slipping from the reporter's hands, must have fallen perpendicularly, he had only to find the pick in order to know the spot where the hole had been pierced in the granite.

The pick was easily found, and, sure enough, a hole opened in a perpendicular line above the point where it had stuck in the sand, about eighty feet above the beach. Some rock-pigeons were already flying in and out of this narrow opening. It really seemed as if Granite House had been discovered for their benefit!

The engineer intended to divide the right-hand portion of the cavern into several chambers, with an entrance-passage before them, and to light it by means of five windows and a door, pierced in the front. Pencroff quite approved of the five windows, but he could not see the use of the door, since the old outlet furnished a natural staircase, by which it would always be easy to get into Granite House.

"My friend," answered Smith, "if it is easy for us to reach our dwelling by the outlet, it will be equally easy for others besides ourselves. I mean, on the contrary, to stop up the outlet at its mouth, to close it hermetically, and even, if need be, to conceal the entrance completely, by damming the lake and raising its waters."

"And how shall we get in?" asked the sailor.

"By an outside ladder," replied Smith; "a rope-ladder, which, when it is drawn up, will make access to our dwelling impossible."

"But why so many precautions?" said Pencroff. "So far the animals have not seemed very dangerous. And as for natives, our island has none!"

"Are you quite sure of that, Pencroff?" asked the engineer, looking at the sailor.

"Of course we shall not be sure of it until we have explored every part of it," answered Pencroff.

"Yes," said Smith, "for as yet we know only a small portion of it. But at any rate, if we have no enemies within, they may come from without, for these Pacific waters are bad waters. Let us take our precautions, then, against every chance."

Smith spoke wisely; and Pencroff, without making any further objection, prepared to carry out his orders.

The front of Granite House was therefore to be lighted by five windows and a door, which would serve what was properly the "apartment," and by a wide bay-window and some bull's-eyes, which would let the light pour in abundantly upon that marvelous nave which was to serve as their great hall. This front, eighty feet above the ground, looked toward the east, and the rising sun greeted it with its first rays. It occupied that portion of the curtain of rock which lay between the projecting angle over the mouth of the Mercy, and a line drawn perpendicularly above the heap of rocks which formed the Chimneys. Thus the bad winds, that is to say, those from the northeast, struck it only obliquely, for it was sheltered by the very direction of the projection. Moreover, until the window-sashes could be made, the engineer intended to close the openings with thick shutters, which would keep out both wind and rain, and which he could conceal if necessary.

The first work, then, was to cut out these openings. To use the pick upon this hard rock would have been too slow, and it is known that Smith was a man of bold measures. He still had a certain quantity of nitro-glycerine at his disposal, and he employed it to good purpose. The force of the explosive was properly confined, and, under its stroke, the granite was broken in at the very places chosen by the engineer. Then the pick and the mattock finished the pointed, ogival outline of the five windows, the bay, the bull's-eyes, and the door; they trued the frames, whose outlines were finished somewhat fancifully; and, a few days after the work had begun, Granite House was amply lighted by the morning sun, which penetrated even to its most secret recesses.

According to the plan settled upon by Smith, the apartment was to be divided into five compartments looking out upon the sea: on the right, an entry, served by a door at which the ladder would end; next, a kitchen-room, thirty feet wide; a dining-room, forty feet wide; a sleeping-room of equal width; and, lastly, a "spare room," which Pencroff insisted upon, and which adjoined the great hall.

These rooms, or rather this suite of rooms, which formed the apartment of Granite House, were not to occupy the whole depth of the cavern. They were to be served by a corridor running between them and a long store-room, in which tools, provisions, and reserve stores would find ample space. All the products gathered on the island, those of the flora as well as those of the fauna, would there be kept in excellent condition, and quite safe from damp. There was no lack of room, and every object could be methodically arranged. Besides, the colonists still had at their disposal the little grotto above the great cavern, which would serve as a garret for the new dwelling.

This plan once settled, it only remained to carry it out. The miners therefore became brick-makers again; then the bricks were brought and laid at the foot of Granite House.

Until now, Smith and his companions had entered the cavern only by the old outlet. This mode of communication obliged them, first, to climb Prospect Plateau, by making a detour along the bank of the river; then to descend two hundred feet through the passage; and then to climb as many again whenever they wished to return to the plateau. Hence there was great loss of time and much fatigue. Smith therefore determined to proceed without delay to the making of a strong rope-ladder, which, once drawn up, would render the entrance of Granite House absolutely inaccessible.

This ladder was made with extreme care, and its uprights, formed of the fibres of the "curry-jonc," twisted by means of a winder, had the strength of a stout cable. As for the rungs, they were furnished by a sort of red cedar, with light and tough branches; and the whole contrivance was put together by Pencroff with a master hand.

Other ropes were made in the same way from vegetable fibres, and a rough sort of tackle was fixed at the door. In this way the bricks could easily be hoisted to the level of Granite House. The carrying of materials was thus very much simplified, and the arrangement of the interior, properly so called, was begun at once. There was no lack of lime, and some thousands of bricks were at hand, ready for use. The framework of the partitions, which was very rude, was easily set up; and in a very short time the apartment was divided into chambers and store-room, according to the plan agreed upon.

These various labors went on rapidly under the direction of the engineer, who himself handled the hammer and the trowel. No kind of handiwork was strange to Smith, and he thus set an example to companions who were intelligent and zealous. They worked with confidence, and even gayly, for Pencroff always had a joke ready; now carpenter, now rope-maker, now mason, he communicated his good humor to the whole little company. His faith in the engineer was absolute; nothing could have shaken it. He believed him capable of undertaking anything and of succeeding in everything. The question of clothes and shoes—a serious question, certainly—that of lighting during the winter nights, the cultivation of the fertile parts of the island, the transformation of this wild flora into a civilized one—all seemed easy to him, with Smith's help, and everything would be done in its time. He dreamed of canalized rivers to carry the riches of the soil, of quarries and mines to be worked, of machines fit for every kind of industry, of railroads—yes, railroads!—whose network would certainly one day cover Lincoln Island.

The engineer let Pencroff talk. He did not check the exaggerations of this brave heart. He knew how contagious confidence is; he even smiled to hear him, and said nothing of the anxieties which the future sometimes caused him. For in this part of the Pacific, out of the track of ships, he might well fear that help would never come. It was upon themselves, then, and upon themselves alone, that the colonists must depend; for the distance from Lincoln Island to any other land was so great, that to venture out in a boat, necessarily of indifferent construction, would be a serious and perilous matter.

"But," as the sailor said, "they outstripped by a hundred cubits the Robinsons of former days, for whom everything was a miracle to be wrought."

And, in fact, they "knew"; and the man who "knows" succeeds where others would vegetate and inevitably perish.

During these labors Herbert distinguished himself. He was intelligent and active; he understood quickly, and carried out well; and Smith grew more and more attached to the boy. Herbert felt for the engineer a warm and respectful affection. Pencroff saw clearly the close sympathy which was growing up between the two, but he was not at all jealous of it.

Neb was Neb. He was what he would always be—courage, zeal, devotion, self-denial personified. He had the same faith in his master as Pencroff, but he showed it less noisily. When the sailor grew enthusiastic, Neb always had the air of answering, "But nothing could be more natural." He and Pencroff were very fond of each other, and had soon fallen into the familiar "thou" of old friends.

As for Gideon Spilett, he took his share of the common labor, and was not the most awkward at it—which always rather astonished the sailor. A "journalist" clever, not only at understanding everything, but at doing everything!

The ladder was finally put in place on the 28th of May. It had no less than a hundred rungs over the perpendicular height of eighty feet which it covered. Smith had fortunately been able to divide it into two parts, by taking advantage of an overhanging ledge of the wall, which projected some forty feet above the ground. This ledge, carefully leveled with the pick, became a sort of landing, to which they fastened the first ladder, whose swing was thus diminished by half, and which could be drawn up by a rope to the level of Granite House. As for the second ladder, it was made fast both at its lower end, which rested upon the ledge, and at its upper end, which was secured to the door itself. In this way the ascent became considerably easier. Besides, Smith intended later to set up a hydraulic lift, which would spare the inhabitants of Granite House all fatigue and loss of time.

The colonists quickly grew used to the ladder. They were nimble and skilful, and Pencroff, as a sailor accustomed to run up the ratlines of the shrouds, was able to give them lessons. But he had to give lessons to Top as well. The poor dog, with his four legs, was not built for such exercise. But Pencroff was so zealous a teacher, that Top at last performed his ascents very creditably, and soon went up the ladder as his fellows commonly do in circuses. How proud the sailor was of his pupil cannot be told. Nevertheless, more than once, Pencroff carried him up on his back, of which Top never complained.

It should be observed here that during these labors, which were carried on briskly, for the bad season was drawing near, the question of food had not been neglected. Every day the reporter and Herbert, who had now become regularly the purveyors of the colony, spent some hours in hunting. As yet they hunted only in Jacamar Wood, on the left of the river; for, having neither bridge nor boat, they had not yet crossed the Mercy. All those immense forests to which they had given the name of the Far West forests were therefore still unexplored. This important excursion was reserved for the first fine days of the next spring. But Jacamar Wood was well enough stocked with game; kangaroos and wild boars abounded there, and the hunters' iron-spiked spears, bow, and arrows did wonders. Moreover, Herbert discovered, toward the southwest angle of the lagoon, a natural warren, a sort of slightly damp meadow, covered with willows and with aromatic herbs which perfumed the air—thyme, wild thyme, basil, savory—all those fragrant species of the Labiatae family of which rabbits are so fond.

The reporter remarked that, since the table was laid for rabbits, it would be strange if the rabbits were wanting; and the two hunters explored the warren carefully. At any rate, it produced useful plants in abundance, and a naturalist would have had there the chance of studying many specimens of the vegetable kingdom. Herbert thus gathered a quantity of shoots of basil, rosemary, balm, betony, etc., which possess various medicinal properties, some pectoral, astringent, or febrifuge, others antispasmodic or antirheumatic. And when, later, Pencroff asked what was the use of all this harvest of herbs:—

"To take care of ourselves," answered the boy; "to doctor us when we are sick."

"Why should we be sick, since there are no doctors on the island?" replied Pencroff, very seriously.

To this there was no answer; but the boy went on with his harvest none the less, and it was very well received at Granite House. All the more so, because to these medicinal plants he was able to add a good quantity of Monarda didyma, which is known in North America by the name of "Oswego tea," and makes an excellent beverage.

At last, that day, after a careful search, the two hunters came upon the real site of the warren. The ground there was perforated like a skimmer.

"Burrows!" cried Herbert.

"Yes," answered the reporter, "I see them."

"But are they inhabited?"

"That is the question."

The question was not long in being answered. Almost at once, hundreds of little animals, like rabbits, scampered off in every direction, and so swiftly that even Top could not have outrun them. Hunters and dog ran in vain; these rodents easily escaped them. But the reporter was quite determined not to leave the spot until he had captured at least half a dozen of these quadrupeds. He wished first of all to stock the larder with them, leaving the taming of those which might be caught later for another time. With a few snares set at the mouths of the burrows, the operation could not fail to succeed. But at the moment they had no snares, nor anything to make them of. They had, therefore, to resign themselves to visiting each hole, poking into it with a stick, and accomplishing by dint of patience what could not be done otherwise.

At last, after an hour's searching, four rodents were taken in their holes. They were rabbits, much like their European kindred, and are commonly known by the name of "American rabbits."

The game was therefore carried back to Granite House, and figured at the evening meal. The tenants of this warren were by no means to be despised, for they were delicious. This was a valuable resource for the colony, and one which seemed likely to be inexhaustible.

On the 31st of May the partitions were finished. It only remained to furnish the rooms, which would be the work of the long winter days. A fireplace was built in the first room, which served as the kitchen. The flue, which was to carry the smoke outside, gave the improvised stove-fitters some trouble. Smith thought it simplest to make it of brick-clay; and, as it was out of the question to carry it out through the plateau above, they pierced a hole in the granite over the window of the said kitchen, and to this hole the flue, slanting upward, was led, like that of a sheet-iron stove. Perhaps—indeed, without doubt—the chimney would smoke in the strong east winds which beat directly upon the front; but these winds were rare, and, besides, Master Neb, the cook, was not so very particular.

When these interior arrangements had been completed, the engineer set about stopping up the mouth of the old outlet which opened on the lake, so as to cut off all access by that way. Blocks of rock were rolled to the opening and strongly cemented. Smith did not yet carry out the plan he had formed of drowning this mouth beneath the waters of the lake, by raising them to their former level with a dam. He contented himself with concealing the obstruction by means of grass, shrubs, and brushwood, which were planted in the crevices of the rocks, and which the next spring would cause to grow luxuriantly.

He made use of the outlet, however, to bring a little stream of the fresh water of the lake to the new dwelling. A small cut, made below the level of the water, produced this result; and this tapping of a pure and unfailing source gave a supply of from twenty-five to thirty gallons* a day. Granite House, therefore, would never be in want of water.

At last all was finished; and it was high time, for the bad season was at hand. Thick shutters made it possible to close the windows of the front, until the engineer should have had time to make window-glass.

Gideon Spilett had very artistically arranged plants of various kinds, as well as long trailing grasses, in the projections of the rock around the windows; and in this way the openings were framed in a picturesque verdure, with a charming effect.

The inhabitants of this solid, healthful, and safe dwelling could not but be delighted with their work. From the windows their view extended over a boundless horizon, closed in on the north by the two Mandible Capes, and on the south by Claw Cape. All Union Bay spread out magnificently before them. Yes, these brave colonists had reason to be satisfied; and Pencroff was not sparing of his praises for what he humorously called "his apartment on the fifth floor above the entresol!"

*The gallon is equal to about four liters and a half.

CHAPTER XIX bis.

PENCROFF AT HIS LINES—A SAILOR’S EYE—SOMETHING ON THE BAR—A SECRET BETWEEN TWO—THE RAIN—EMPTY LINES—THE GREEN BALL—WHAT HERBERT READ IN THE BARNACLES—THREE LETTERS—THE SQUALL—THE BAR AT THE NEXT TIDE.

The first day of June found the colonists fairly settled in their new abode. The partitions were up, the chimney drew, and the shutters, closed upon the five windows, kept out wind and rain well enough. Within, the hall was as dark as a ship’s hold in the daytime, and their only light came from the kitchen fire. Smith had promised glass from the sand of the shore, and candles from the fat of the seals, but neither could be had in a day. Meanwhile there was furniture to be made, and every hand was wanted for it.

Pencroff had not forgotten the sea. He had set some lines at the mouth of the Mercy, a little above the point where the river fell into the channel, and he visited them at every low water. He went down the ladder that morning a little after sunrise, with Top at his heels. The sky was gray and low, the wind was in the southeast, and the channel between the shore and Safety Islet was nearly bare.

Now a sailor does not look at the water as a landsman does. Where the landsman sees only a flat sheet, more or less wrinkled, the sailor reads the color of it, the run of the ripples, the line of foam that marks a shoal, the smooth patch that betrays an eddy. Pencroff, while he hauled in his first line, which was empty, cast his eye along the channel from habit, and saw, about halfway to the islet, a long sandbar which the ebb had just laid bare. Nothing could be more ordinary. But upon this bar, at its southern end, the water broke where it should not have broken; and when the little wave had passed, Pencroff saw something there which was neither sand nor rock nor weed.

It was round, about a foot across, and half buried. It caught the gray light dully, as a wet stone does not.

The sailor stood still, with the line in his hand. A round thing on a bar is no common thing. A cask, a buoy, the top of a wreck—any of these may come ashore upon a coast where ships pass. And if ships passed this coast—

But the flood was already making. Pencroff knew it by the bubbles which began to rise along the edge of the bar, and by the turn of the floating weed in the channel. The bar was a good quarter of a mile away, and the sand between was beginning to cover. He could not reach it and come back dry, and he did not care to be caught midway by the tide with nothing to show for it.

He finished with his lines, therefore, which gave him two small fish of no account, and he watched the bar until the water closed over it. Then he went back slowly towards the Chimneys, thinking.

He had a strong desire to run up the ladder and tell the whole thing to Smith. But what was there to tell? A round thing on the sand, which might be a stone after all, or a lump of pumice from the volcano, or a dead sea-urchin of extraordinary size. If he spoke of a buoy, they would think of a ship; if they thought of a ship, they would watch the horizon all winter long; and if it came to nothing, he would have done his friends a poor service. Pencroff had seen men at sea grow sick with looking for a sail. He resolved to say nothing until he knew.

Yet he wanted a second pair of hands, and a second pair of eyes, and above all he wanted to share it with someone. Towards noon, finding Herbert alone at the foot of the cliff, where the boy was gathering driftwood for the kitchen fire, the sailor drew him aside under the wall.

“Herbert,” said he, in a low voice, “will you help me with the lines to-morrow morning at low water?”

“Certainly, Pencroff,” answered the lad, rather surprised, for the lines had never needed two men.

“And say nothing about it upstairs?”

Herbert looked at him.

“There’s something on the bar out yonder,” said Pencroff, pointing with his chin towards the channel, which was now full. “I saw it this morning. It may be nothing at all. I don’t want to make a fuss over nothing. We’ll go and look, you and I, and if it’s worth the telling, we’ll tell it.”

Herbert promised. He was not used to keeping anything from Smith, and the promise cost him a little; but the sailor was his oldest friend, and the reason seemed a good one.

The next morning, June 2, broke in rain. It was not a squall, but a steady, cold, slanting rain from the southeast, which hid the islet and the sea beyond it in a gray mist. The shutters of Granite House remained closed. Smith and Spilett were at work in the hall upon the frame of a table; Neb was salting the meat of the day before. Nobody thought it strange when Pencroff said that he and Herbert would go down and see to the lines.

“You will be wet through,” said the reporter.

“Fish don’t mind it,” answered the sailor, “and no more do I.”

They went down the ladder. Top would have followed them, but Pencroff sent him back, for the dog would have run splashing and barking across the sand and brought every head to the windows. At the bottom, the sailor stood a moment and looked up at the cliff. There were picks in the storehouse, two of them, good ones, forged by Smith himself. He had thought of them more than once in the night. But a man does not carry a pick down a ladder in the rain to look at fishing lines, and if he had been asked what he wanted with it, he would have had to answer. He had his knife, and that must serve. It was one of the only two which the colony possessed, made by the engineer from the halves of Top’s collar, and Pencroff valued it above any blade he had ever owned.

They went first to the lines. It was the right order, and besides, the ebb was not yet at its lowest. The lines were empty, every one. The bait had been taken clean from two of the hooks, by crabs, no doubt.

“Bad luck,” said Herbert.

“We didn’t come for fish,” answered Pencroff.

The channel was now bare, or nearly so. A thin sheet of water still ran in the deeper part, but the sand was firm and ribbed, and they could cross it without wetting their knees. The rain beat upon their backs. The bar rose before them, long and low, and at its southern end, exactly where the sailor had seen it the day before, lay the round thing, half sunk in the sand, with a little pool of water about it.

They knelt down beside it.

It was a ball of glass. Its color was a dark green, so dark that it seemed almost black where the sand lay against it, and the rain ran off it in little streams. It was thick with barnacles, which covered it like a crust over nearly all its upper half, so that only here and there could the glass itself be seen. Around it, and under it, ran the remains of a lashing of cord, black and rotten, and this lashing, when Pencroff cleared away the sand with his hands, was found to be knotted into the meshes of a net. The net itself went down into the bar, and no one could say how far.

Pencroff took hold of the ball with both hands and tried to turn it. It did not move. He tried to lift it. It did not move. He set his feet and pulled, until the rotten cord creaked; but whatever held the net below held it fast, and the sand, packed by a hundred tides, held all the rest.

“It’s a float,” said he at last, sitting back upon his heels. “A net float. That’s plain enough. But it isn’t cork, and it isn’t American. I’ve been in fishing towns from Nantucket to Gloucester, and I never saw a float of glass, nor heard of one. Nor in England either. I don’t know where they make such things.”

He drew his knife and tried to follow the lashing down under the ball, but it ran away under the glass, deep into the sand, where his hand could not reach. He dug with his fingers. The water came in as fast as he dug, and the sand with it.

Herbert, meanwhile, was looking closely at the barnacles. He pried one off with his thumbnail, and turned it over, and looked at it for some time.

“These are dead, Pencroff,” said he. “Every one of them. Long dead. There is nothing inside them at all.”

“Well?”

“And they are not the kind that grow on rocks. They are the kind that grow only on things that float—on drifting logs, and ships’ bottoms, and weed at the surface of the sea. They have long stalks when they are living. I have seen pictures of them.” He showed the sailor where the stalk had been. “So this ball was afloat for a long time before it came here. Long enough for all these to grow on it, and then to die. Years, perhaps.”

“Afloat where?”

“I don’t know. A long way off, I think. It must have drifted from very far.”

Pencroff said nothing. He understood well enough what the boy meant, and what he did not say. A float lost yesterday from a fishing boat would have meant a fishing boat within a few miles. A float that had drifted for years meant only that, somewhere in the world, there were fishermen. It did not bring a ship one league nearer to Lincoln Island.

“Well,” said he at length, “it’s a float, anyhow.”

Herbert had picked up a stone from the bar, a flat piece of granite which fitted his hand, and he began to chip away the crust of barnacles from the glass. They came off in flakes, with the sand that had settled among them. Under them the glass was smooth and dark and clean, as if it had been made that morning. Pencroff watched him.

The sailor was thinking, though he did not say it, what such a thing would be worth to them. A ball of glass! Smith had promised them glass, but in some future time, when the furnaces were ready and the sand was washed. And here was glass already, thick and sound, with the shutters of Granite House closed upon five dark windows above them. It would not make a window, no doubt; but set in a hole in a shutter, it would let in a green light, and a green light is better than none. And if it would not serve for that, it would serve him for a float on his lines, the best float a man ever had, which no fish could pull under. He turned it over in his mind as a man counts money in his pocket. But he said nothing of it to Herbert. It was not yet theirs.

“Pencroff,” said the lad suddenly, “look here.”

Where he had cleared the barnacles from the side of the ball, there was a seal moulded in the glass, a round raised boss, like the seal upon a bottle. Part of it was broken away, and part was still crusted over; but upon the part that showed there was a run of capital letters, standing up from the glass in relief. Herbert wet his finger and passed it over them. There was a B, then an L, then an I. Before and after these, the letters were broken, or hidden.

Pencroff bent over them, but he could make nothing of them.

“Latin, I think,” said Herbert, and no more.

He went on chipping, slowly and carefully, hoping to bare the rest of the seal; but the crust lay thickest there, and the stone was clumsy, and he dared not strike hard, for fear of breaking the glass. The letters that came out were only pieces of letters, a stroke here and a curve there, which might have been anything.

So the hour of low water went by. They did not notice it going. They knelt in the rain on either side of the ball, the sailor with his knife, the boy with his stone, now scraping at the barnacles, now feeling under the sand for the end of the lashing, now trying once more to stir the thing from its bed. They read it a good deal more than they worked at it, which was perhaps natural, since they could not free it. Five men with picks could have had it out in a quarter of an hour, net and all. Two men, with a knife and a stone, could do nothing but look at it.

It was Pencroff who first raised his head. The wind had shifted a point or two, and it came now with a weight in it that he knew. Out beyond the islet, the mist had grown darker, and there was a white line under it.

“The flood,” said he, “and something with it. Come, Herbert. Quick, now.”

They rose and ran back across the sand. The water was already coming in over the low places, and before they reached the shore it was about their ankles. The squall overtook them as they came up under the cliff. It came with a roar, and the rain, which had been falling slantwise, began to drive straight against the face of the granite, so thick that they could hardly see one another.

They took shelter at the foot of the wall, a little to the south of the ladder, where an overhanging ledge of rock broke the force of the wind. They were wet already, and could be no wetter. From there they could still see the bar, or rather the place where it had been, for the flood was now running over it, and the sea broke upon it in long gray lines of surf.

For a long time they could make out nothing but foam. Then, in a lull, Herbert caught the sailor’s arm. Between two waves, at the end of the bar, the dark ball showed for an instant, rocking. It was free of the sand, or nearly so. The next wave covered it. The one after that lifted it, and they saw the net trail up behind it, black, like a handful of weed.

Neither of them spoke. The surge came on in heavier seas, and with each sea the ball rose and fell and dragged at its mooring; and they knew well enough that what was left of the rotten cord could not hold long. At last a great wave broke over the bar, higher than the rest, and when it had passed, the ball was not there.

A moment later the clouds opened a little in the east, and a gleam of pale light fell across the channel. And in that gleam, on the black rocks at the point of Safety Islet, in the midst of the spray, there was a flash, green and bright, as of something that caught the sun.

Then the clouds closed, the rain came down again, and it was gone.

They waited under the wall until the worst of the squall had passed, and then they went up the ladder. It was early afternoon when they came into Granite House, wet through, and so cold that their fingers could hardly hold the rungs. Neb cried out at the sight of them and threw more wood upon the fire.

“Well, Pencroff,” said the reporter, “where is the fish?”

“The lines took nothing,” answered the sailor, wringing out his jacket, “and the sea took the pair of us.”

Everybody laughed, and Pencroff laughed with them. He said no more. It was not only that he did not care to confess an hour wasted on the bar; it was that he had not yet given up. The ball was on the rocks of the islet, or under them, or somewhere on the sand of the channel; the sea gives back what it takes, now and then, to a man who knows how to look. He meant still to find it, and to bring it home himself, and to lay it upon the new table before them all without a word, and watch their faces. That would be worth something. Herbert, looking at him across the fire, understood, and held his tongue.

That night the wind fell. On the next morning, June 3, the two went down again at low water, the sailor saying merely that he had a fancy to try the lines once more.

The channel was bare, as before. But the bar was not where it had been. The squall and the flood had moved it bodily, as the sea moves such bars, and it now lay farther to the north, lower and longer, with a new channel cut across its southern end where the ball had lain. They walked over the whole of it. They walked over the sand between it and the islet, and along the foot of the rocks, and among the rocks themselves, as far as the tide allowed. There was nothing: no glass, no float, no fragment of glass, not even the net. The sand was smooth and ribbed and new, as if nothing had ever lain in it.

Pencroff stood a long while at the end of the new bar, with his hands in his pockets, looking at the water.

“Five of us and the picks,” said he at last, “and we’d have had it.”

“We had it, Pencroff,” said Herbert. “For an hour.”

The sailor looked at him, and then nodded, and they went back to the lines, which were empty again.

Nothing more was said of the matter, either then or afterwards. The next day was Whit Sunday, which the colonists kept as a day of rest, and after it came the seals, and the candles, and all the labors of the winter; and if Pencroff, during the months that followed, looked oftener than was needful at the rocks of Safety Islet, nobody asked him why. Neither he nor Herbert ever spoke of the green ball to Smith, or to the reporter, or to Neb, or even to each other.

CHAPTER XX.

THE RAINY SEASON—THE QUESTION OF CLOTHING—A SEAL HUNT—CANDLE-MAKING—WORK INSIDE GRANITE HOUSE—THE TWO BRIDGES—RETURN FROM A VISIT TO THE OYSTER-BED—WHAT HERBERT FOUND IN HIS POCKET.

The winter season began in earnest with the month of June, which corresponds to December in the Northern Hemisphere. It opened with showers and squalls, which followed one another without respite. The tenants of Granite House could now appreciate the advantages of a dwelling which no weather could reach. The shelter of the Chimneys would have been altogether insufficient against the rigors of a winter, and it was to be feared that the high tides, driven by the winds from the open sea, might break in there again. Smith even took some precautions against this, so as to protect, as far as possible, the forge and the furnaces which had been set up there.

Throughout the month of June the time was given to various labors, which did not exclude either hunting or fishing, and the larder was kept abundantly supplied. Pencroff intended, as soon as he had leisure, to set traps, from which he expected great things. He had made snares of woody fibre, and not a day passed but the warren furnished its share of rodents. Neb spent nearly all his time in salting or smoking meat, which gave him excellent preserved provisions.

The question of clothing was now very seriously discussed. The colonists had no other garments than those they had worn when the balloon threw them upon the island. These clothes were warm and strong; they had taken the greatest care of them, as well as of their linen, and kept them perfectly clean; but all would soon need to be replaced. Besides, if the winter should be severe, the colonists would suffer greatly from the cold.

Here Smith's ingenuity was at fault. He had been obliged to provide first for the most pressing wants, to make the dwelling and to secure their food, and the cold might come upon him before the question of clothing was solved. They must therefore make up their minds to pass this first winter without complaining too much. When the fine season came, they would make a regular hunt for those musmons which had been seen during the exploration of Mount Franklin; and, once the wool was gathered, the engineer would know how to make warm and strong stuffs of it. How? He would think about that.

"Well," said Pencroff, "we shall get off with toasting our shins at Granite House! There is plenty of fuel, and no reason for sparing it."

"Besides," said Spilett, "Lincoln Island is not in a very high latitude, and the winters are probably not severe. Did you not tell us, Cyrus, that this thirty-fifth parallel corresponded to that of Spain in the other hemisphere?"

"No doubt," said the engineer; "but some winters are very cold in Spain! Snow and ice, nothing is wanting; and Lincoln Island may be as severely tried. Still, it is an island, and, as such, I hope the temperature will be more moderate."

"Why so, Mr. Smith?" asked Herbert.

"Because the sea, my boy, may be regarded as an immense reservoir, in which the heat of summer is stored up. When winter comes, the sea gives back this heat, which secures to the regions bordering upon the oceans a medium temperature, less high in summer, but less low in winter."

"We shall see," said Pencroff. "I don't mean to trouble myself any further about the cold there will be or won't be. What is certain is, that the days are already short and the evenings long. Suppose we take up the question of lighting a little."

"Nothing is easier," said Smith.

"To take up?" asked the sailor.

"To solve."

"And when shall we begin?"

"To-morrow, by getting up a seal hunt."

"To make tallow dips?"

"Fie, Pencroff! Candles!"

This was, in fact, the engineer's plan; and a practicable one, for he had lime and sulphuric acid, and the amphibians of the islet would furnish him the fat needed for the work.

It was the 4th of June. It was Whitsunday, and all agreed to keep the feast. All work was suspended, and prayers went up to Heaven. But these prayers were now thanksgivings. The colonists of Lincoln Island were no longer the miserable castaways thrown upon the islet. They no longer asked; they gave thanks.

The next day, June 5, in rather uncertain weather, they set out for the islet. They had again to wait for low tide to ford the channel, and in this connection it was agreed that they would build, as best they could, a boat, which would make communication easier, and would also allow them to ascend the Mercy at the time of the grand exploration of the southwest of the island, which was put off till the first fine days.

The seals were numerous, and the hunters, armed with their iron-tipped spears, easily killed half a dozen. Neb and Pencroff skinned them, and brought back to Granite House only their fat and their skins, the skins being meant for making strong shoes.

The result of this hunt was about three hundred pounds of fat, which was all to be used in making candles.

The operation was extremely simple, and if it did not give perfect products, they were at least fit for use. Had Smith had nothing but sulphuric acid at his disposal, he could have isolated the glycerine by heating this acid with the neutral fatty bodies, in this case the seal fat; then, from the new compound, he could easily have separated the oleine, the margarine, and the stearine by means of boiling water. But, to simplify the operation, he preferred to saponify the fat with lime. In this way he obtained a calcareous soap, easily decomposed by the sulphuric acid, which precipitated the lime as a sulphate and set free the fatty acids.

Of these three acids, the oleic, the margaric, and the stearic, the first, being liquid, was driven out by sufficient pressure. The other two formed the very substance from which the candles were to be moulded.

The operation took no more than twenty-four hours. The wicks, after several trials, were made of vegetable fibre, and, dipped in the melted substance, they made true stearine candles, moulded by hand, which lacked nothing but bleaching and polishing. They had not, of course, the advantage of wicks soaked in boracic acid, which vitrify as they burn and are entirely consumed; but as Smith had made a fine pair of snuffers, these candles were greatly prized during the evenings at Granite House.

All this month there was no lack of work inside the new dwelling. The joiners had plenty to do. They improved the tools, which were very rude, and they added to them.

Among other things, scissors were made, and the colonists could at last cut their hair, and if not shave their beards, at least trim them as they pleased. Herbert had none, and Neb had little; but their companions were bristling with them in a way that fully justified the making of the said scissors.

The making of a hand-saw, of the kind called a panel-saw, cost no end of trouble; but at last they had a tool which, worked vigorously, would cut through the fibres of wood. So they made tables, seats, and cupboards to furnish the principal rooms, and bedsteads, whose bedding consisted wholly of mattresses of sea-wrack. The kitchen, with its shelves holding the earthenware utensils, its brick stove, and its sink-stone, looked very well, and Neb went about his work there gravely, as if he were in a chemist's laboratory.

But the joiners had soon to give place to the carpenters. The new outlet, made by blasting, required the building of two small bridges, one on Prospect Plateau, the other on the shore itself. For now the plateau and the shore were cut across by a stream, which had to be crossed whenever they wished to reach the north of the island. To avoid it, the colonists would have been forced to make a long circuit, and go up westward beyond the sources of Red Creek. The simplest plan, then, was to build on the plateau and on the shore two small bridges, from twenty to twenty-five feet long, whose whole framework was made of a few trees, merely squared with the axe. This was the work of a few days. When the bridges were finished, Neb and Pencroff took the opportunity to go to the oyster-bed which had been discovered off the downs. They took with them a sort of rough cart, which replaced the old hurdle, now found too inconvenient, and they brought back some thousands of oysters, which quickly became acclimated among the rocks at the mouth of the Mercy, which made so many natural beds. These mollusks were of excellent quality, and the colonists ate them almost daily.

It will be seen that Lincoln Island, though its inhabitants had explored only a very small portion of it, already supplied nearly all their wants. And it was likely that, when searched into its most secret corners, over all that wooded tract which stretched from the Mercy to Reptile Promontory, it would yield new treasures.

There was but one privation which the colonists of Lincoln Island still felt. They had no lack of nitrogenous food, nor of the vegetable products which should temper its use. The woody roots of the dragon trees, fermented, gave them an acid drink, a kind of beer much better than plain water. They had even made sugar, without cane or beet, by gathering the liquor which flows from the "acer saccharinum," a kind of maple of the family Acerineæ, which thrives in all the temperate zones, and of which the island had a great number. They made a very pleasant tea from the Oswego tea brought from the warren. And lastly, they had an abundance of salt, the only mineral product which enters into food. But bread was wanting.

Perhaps, later, the colonists might replace this food by some equivalent, sago flour or bread-fruit starch; and it was possible, in fact, that the forests of the south might number these precious trees among their species; but so far none had been met with.

But Providence was, on this occasion, to come directly to the aid of the colonists, in an infinitesimal proportion, it is true; yet Smith, with all his intelligence and all his ingenuity, could never have produced what Herbert, by the merest chance, found one day in the lining of his jacket, which he was mending.

That day it was raining in torrents, and the colonists were gathered in the great hall of Granite House, when the lad cried out all at once:

"Look, Mr. Smith! A grain of wheat!"

And he showed his companions a grain, one single grain, which had worked its way out of his torn pocket into the lining of his jacket.

The presence of this grain was explained by Herbert's habit, while at Richmond, of feeding some wood-pigeons which Pencroff had given him.

"A grain of wheat?" said the engineer quickly.

"Yes, Mr. Smith; but only one, just one!"

"Well, my boy," cried Pencroff, smiling, "we are a great deal better off, upon my word! What could we possibly do with one grain of wheat?"

"We will make bread of it," answered Smith.

"Bread, cakes, and tarts!" replied the sailor. "Come! The bread this grain of wheat will furnish won't choke us very soon!"

Herbert, attaching little importance to his discovery, was about to throw the grain away; but Smith took it, examined it, found that it was in good condition, and, looking the sailor full in the face, said quietly:

"Pencroff, do you know how many ears one grain of wheat can produce?"

"One, I suppose!" answered the sailor, surprised at the question.

"Ten, Pencroff. And do you know how many grains an ear bears?"

"Upon my word, no."

"Eighty on an average," said Smith. "So, if we plant this grain, at the first harvest we shall reap eight hundred grains, which at the second will produce six hundred and forty thousand, at the third five hundred and twelve millions, at the fourth more than four hundred thousand millions of grains. That is the proportion."

Smith's companions listened without answering. These figures astounded them. They were correct, nevertheless.

"Yes, my friends," continued the engineer. "Such are the arithmetical progressions of fruitful Nature. And yet, what is this multiplication of the grain of wheat, whose ear bears but eight hundred grains, compared with the poppy plant, which bears thirty-two thousand seeds, or the tobacco plant, which produces three hundred and sixty thousand? In a few years, but for the many causes of destruction which check their fertility, these plants would overrun the whole earth."

But the engineer had not finished his little catechism.

"And now, Pencroff," he went on, "do you know how many bushels four hundred thousand millions of grains would make?"

"No," answered the sailor; "but what I do know is, that I am nothing but a blockhead!"

"Well, it would make more than three millions, at a hundred and thirty thousand to the bushel, Pencroff."

"Three millions!" cried Pencroff.

"Three millions."

"In four years?"

"In four years," answered Smith, "and even in two, if, as I hope, we can get two harvests a year in this latitude."

To this, as was his custom, Pencroff thought he could make no other answer than a tremendous hurrah.

"So, Herbert," added the engineer, "you have made a discovery of the utmost importance to us. Everything, my friends, everything may be of use to us in our present situation. I beg you not to forget it."

"No, Mr. Smith, no, we will not forget it," answered Pencroff; "and if ever I find one of those tobacco seeds which multiply by three hundred and sixty thousand, I promise you I won't throw it to the winds! And now, do you know what we have to do?"

"We have to plant this grain," said Herbert.

"Yes," added Spilett, "and with all the respect due to it, for it carries our future harvests in it."

"If only it grows!" cried the sailor.

"It will grow," answered Smith.

It was the 20th of June. The time was therefore favorable for sowing this one precious grain of wheat. At first they talked of planting it in a pot; but, on reflection, they decided to trust Nature more frankly, and to commit it to the earth. This was done that very day, and it is needless to add that every precaution was taken to make the operation succeed.

The weather having cleared a little, the colonists climbed the heights above Granite House. There, on the plateau, they chose a spot well sheltered from the wind, and on which the noonday sun would pour all its heat. The spot was cleared, carefully weeded, and even dug over, to drive out insects and worms; a layer of good soil, dressed with a little lime, was spread upon it; it was surrounded with a paling; and then the grain was pressed into the moist bed.

Did it not seem as if the colonists were laying the corner-stone of an edifice? It reminded Pencroff of the day when he had lighted his one match, and of all the care he had given to that operation. But this time the matter was more serious. For the castaways would always have managed to get fire, by one means or another; but no human power could give them back this grain of wheat if, by ill luck, it should perish!
