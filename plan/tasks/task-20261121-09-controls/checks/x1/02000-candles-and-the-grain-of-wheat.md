CHAPTER XX.

THE RAINY SEASON—THE QUESTION OF CLOTHING—A SEAL HUNT—CANDLE-MAKING—INTERIOR WORK IN GRANITE HOUSE—THE TWO BRIDGES—RETURN FROM A VISIT TO THE OYSTER-BED—WHAT HERBERT FOUND IN HIS POCKET.

The winter season set in earnestly with the month of June, which corresponds to December in the Northern Hemisphere. It began with showers and squalls which succeeded each other without intermission. The guests of Granite House were able to appreciate the advantages of a dwelling which the inclemency of the weather could not reach. The shelter of the Chimneys would have been truly insufficient against the rigors of a winter, and it was to be feared that the high tides, driven by the winds from the open sea, would again make an irruption there. Cyrus Smith even took some precautions, in anticipation of this contingency, in order to preserve, as far as possible, the forge and the furnaces which had been set up there.

During this whole month of June, the time was employed in various labors, which did not exclude either hunting or fishing, and the stores of the larder were abundantly kept up. Pencroff, as soon as he should have the leisure, proposed to set up traps from which he expected the greatest benefit. He had made snares of woody fibres, and not a day passed that the warren did not furnish its contingent of rodents. Neb spent almost all his time in salting or smoking meats, which secured him excellent preserves.

The question of clothing was then very seriously discussed. The colonists had no other garments than those they wore when the balloon cast them upon the island. These clothes were warm and strong; they had taken extreme care of them as well as of their linen, and kept them in a perfect state of cleanliness, but all these would soon need to be replaced. Besides, if the winter proved severe, the colonists would suffer greatly from the cold.

On this point the ingenuity of Cyrus Smith was at fault. He had been obliged to provide for the most pressing needs—to create a dwelling, to secure food—and the cold might overtake him before the question of clothing had been solved. They must therefore resign themselves to passing this first winter without too much complaint. When the fine season came, they would organize a serious hunt after those moufflons whose presence had been noted during the exploration of Mount Franklin, and, once the wool was gathered, the engineer would certainly know how to manufacture warm and strong stuffs—how, he would think over.

“Well, we shall get off with roasting our shins in Granite House!” said Pencroff. “Fuel abounds, and there is no reason to spare it.”

“Besides,” answered Gideon Spilett, “Lincoln Island is not situated in a very high latitude, and it is probable that the winters here are not severe. Did you not tell us, Cyrus, that this thirty-fifth parallel corresponded to that of Spain in the other hemisphere?”

“Doubtless,” replied the engineer, “but some winters are very cold in Spain! Snow and ice—nothing is wanting there, and Lincoln Island may be just as rigorously tried. However, it is an island, and, as such, I hope the temperature will be more moderate.”

“And why, Mr. Cyrus?” asked Herbert.

“Because the sea, my boy, may be considered as an immense reservoir in which are stored the heats of summer. When winter comes, it restores these heats, which secures to regions bordering on the oceans an average temperature less high in summer, but less low in winter.”

“We shall see,” answered Pencroff. “For my part, I ask not to trouble myself further about the cold that will or will not come. What is certain is that the days are already short and the evenings long. Suppose we take up the question of lighting a little.”

“Nothing is easier,” answered Cyrus Smith.

“To take up?” asked the sailor.

“To solve.”

“And when shall we begin?”

“To-morrow, by organizing a seal hunt.”

“To make tallow candles?”

“Fie, Pencroff! wax candles.”

Such, in fact, was the engineer’s project—a practicable project, since he had lime and sulphuric acid, and the amphibians of the islet would furnish him the fat necessary for their manufacture.

It was the 4th of June. It was Whit-Sunday, and there was a unanimous agreement to observe this festival. All work was suspended, and prayers ascended to heaven. But these prayers were now thanksgivings. The colonists of Lincoln Island were no longer the wretched castaways thrown upon the islet. They no longer petitioned; they gave thanks.

The next day, June 5, in rather uncertain weather, they set out for the islet. They were still obliged to take advantage of low tide to ford the channel, and, in this connection, it was agreed that they would build, after a fashion, a boat which would make communication easier, and would also allow them to ascend the Mercy at the time of the grand exploration of the southwest of the island, which was put off until the first fine days.

The seals were numerous, and the hunters, armed with their iron-tipped spears, easily killed half a dozen. Neb and Pencroff skinned them, and carried back to Granite House only their fat and their hides, these hides being intended for the manufacture of strong foot-gear.

The result of this hunt was about three hundred pounds of fat, which was to be entirely used in the manufacture of candles.

The operation was extremely simple, and, if it did not yield absolutely perfect products, at least they were serviceable. Had Cyrus Smith had nothing at his disposal but sulphuric acid, by heating this acid with neutral fatty bodies—in this case, seal fat—he could have isolated the glycerine; then, from the new combination, he would easily have separated the oleine, the margarine, and the stearine by employing boiling water. But, in order to simplify the operation, he preferred to saponify the fat by means of lime. In this way he obtained a calcareous soap, easy to decompose by sulphuric acid, which precipitated the lime in the state of sulphate and set free the fatty acids.

Of these three acids—oleic, margaric, and stearic—the first, being liquid, was expelled by sufficient pressure. As to the other two, they formed the very substance which was to serve for molding the candles.

The operation did not last more than twenty-four hours. The wicks, after several trials, were made of vegetable fibres, and, dipped in the liquefied substance, they formed genuine stearic candles, molded by hand, which lacked nothing but bleaching and polishing. They did not, doubtless, offer that advantage which wicks impregnated with boric acid have of vitrifying in proportion as they burn, and of consuming themselves entirely; but Cyrus Smith having made a fine pair of snuffers, these candles were greatly appreciated during the evenings at Granite House.

Throughout this month, work was not lacking in the interior of the new abode. The joiners had plenty to do. They improved the tools, which were very rudimentary. They also added to their number.

Scissors, among other things, were made, and the colonists were at last able to cut their hair, and, if not to shave their beards, at least to trim them to their fancy. Herbert had none, Neb had scarcely any, but their companions were so bristling with them as to justify the making of the said scissors.

The manufacture of a handsaw, of the kind called compass-saws, cost infinite pains, but at last they obtained an instrument which, vigorously handled, could divide the woody fibres of timber. They therefore made tables, seats, cupboards, which furnished the principal chambers, and bedsteads, all the bedding of which consisted of mattresses of sea-wrack. The kitchen, with its shelves on which rested the earthenware utensils, its brick stove, its sink, had a very good appearance, and Neb worked there gravely, as if he had been in a chemist’s laboratory.

But the joiners had soon to be replaced by the carpenters. In fact, the new outlet, created by blasting, rendered necessary the construction of two small bridges, one on Prospect Plateau, the other on the beach itself. Now, indeed, the plateau and the beach were cut transversely by a watercourse which had necessarily to be crossed when they wished to reach the north of the island. To avoid it, the colonists would have been obliged to make a considerable detour and go up westward beyond the sources of Red Creek. The simplest way, therefore, was to throw across the plateau and the beach two bridges, from twenty to twenty-five feet long, of which a few trees, merely squared with the axe, formed the entire framework. It was the work of a few days. The bridges built, Neb and Pencroff took advantage of them to go as far as the oyster-bed which had been discovered off the downs. They dragged with them a sort of rude cart, which replaced the old hurdle, really too inconvenient, and they brought back some thousands of oysters, whose acclimatization took place rapidly amid those rocks which formed so many natural preserves at the mouth of the Mercy. These mollusks were of excellent quality, and the colonists made an almost daily consumption of them.

As we see, Lincoln Island, although its inhabitants had explored only a very small portion of it, already supplied almost all their wants. And it was probable that, searched to its most secret recesses, throughout all that wooded part which extended from the Mercy to Reptile Promontory, it would lavish new treasures upon them.

A single privation still tried the colonists of Lincoln Island. Azotized food was not wanting to them, nor the vegetable products which should temper its use; the woody roots of the dragon-trees, subjected to fermentation, gave them an acidulated beverage, a kind of beer far preferable to pure water; they had even made sugar, without cane or beets, by collecting that liquor which the *acer saccharinum* distills—a species of maple of the family of *acerineæ*, which thrives in all temperate zones, and of which the island possessed a great number; they made a very agreeable tea by using the monardas brought from the warren; finally, they had in abundance salt, the only mineral product which enters into food—but bread was wanting.

Perhaps, later on, the colonists might replace this food by some equivalent, sago-flour or bread-fruit starch, and it was possible, indeed, that the southern forests counted these precious trees among their species, but hitherto they had not been met with.

However, Providence was, in this circumstance, to come directly to the aid of the colonists, in an infinitesimal proportion, it is true, but, after all, Cyrus Smith, with all his intelligence, all his ingenuity, could never have produced what, by the greatest chance, Herbert found one day in the lining of his jacket, which he was engaged in mending.

That day—it was raining in torrents—the colonists were assembled in the great hall of Granite House, when the boy suddenly cried out:—

“Look, Mr. Cyrus! A grain of wheat!”

And he showed his companions a grain—a single grain—which, from a hole in his pocket, had slipped into the lining of his jacket.

The presence of this grain was explained by the habit Herbert had, when at Richmond, of feeding some ring-doves of which Pencroff had made him a present.

“A grain of wheat?” answered the engineer quickly.

“Yes, Mr. Cyrus, but only one—nothing but one!”

“Well, my boy,” cried Pencroff, smiling, “we are nicely advanced, upon my word! What could we possibly do with a single grain of wheat?”

“We will make bread of it,” answered Cyrus Smith.

“Bread, cakes, tarts!” replied the sailor. “Come! the bread that grain of wheat will furnish will not choke us very soon!”

Herbert, attaching but little importance to his discovery, was about to throw away the grain in question, but Cyrus Smith took it, examined it, saw that it was in good condition, and, looking the sailor full in the face:—

“Pencroff,” he asked him quietly, “do you know how many ears a single grain of wheat can produce?”

“One, I suppose!” answered the sailor, surprised at the question.

“Ten, Pencroff. And do you know how many grains an ear bears?”

“Faith, no.”

“Eighty on an average,” said Cyrus Smith. “Therefore, if we plant this grain, at the first harvest we shall reap eight hundred grains, which at the second will produce six hundred and forty thousand; at the third, five hundred and twelve millions; at the fourth, more than four hundred billions of grains. That is the proportion.”

Cyrus Smith’s companions listened to him without answering. These figures stupefied them. They were exact, however.

“Yes, my friends,” resumed the engineer. “Such are the arithmetical progressions of fruitful nature. And yet, what is this multiplication of the grain of wheat, whose ear bears only eight hundred grains, compared to those poppy plants which bear thirty-two thousand seeds, or those tobacco plants which produce three hundred and sixty thousand? In a few years, without the numerous causes of destruction which arrest their fecundity, these plants would overrun the whole earth.”

But the engineer had not finished his little examination.

“And now, Pencroff,” he resumed, “do you know how many bushels four hundred billions of grains represent?”

“No,” answered the sailor, “but what I do know is that I am only a blockhead!”

“Well, it would make more than three millions, at one hundred and thirty thousand to the bushel, Pencroff.”

“Three millions!” cried Pencroff.

“Three millions.”

“In four years?”

“In four years,” answered Cyrus Smith, “and even in two years, if, as I hope, we can, in this latitude, obtain two harvests a year.”

To this, according to his custom, Pencroff could think of no other reply than a tremendous hurrah.

“Thus, Herbert,” added the engineer, “you have made a discovery of extreme importance to us. Everything, my friends, everything can serve us in the condition in which we are. I beg you not to forget it.”

“No, Mr. Cyrus, no, we will not forget it,” answered Pencroff, “and if ever I find one of those tobacco seeds which multiply by three hundred and sixty thousand, I assure you I will not throw it to the winds! And now, do you know what remains for us to do?”

“It remains for us to plant this grain,” answered Herbert.

“Yes,” added Gideon Spilett, “and with all the respect due to it, for it bears within it our future harvests.”

“Provided it grows!” cried the sailor.

“It will grow,” answered Cyrus Smith.

It was the 20th of June. The time was therefore favorable for sowing this single and precious grain of wheat. At first there was some talk of planting it in a pot; but, upon reflection, they resolved to trust more frankly to nature, and to commit it to the earth. This was done the very same day, and it is needless to add that every precaution was taken that the operation might succeed.

The weather having cleared slightly, the colonists climbed the heights of Granite House. There, on the plateau, they chose a spot well sheltered from the wind, and upon which the midday sun would pour all its heat. The spot was cleared, carefully weeded, even dug over, to drive out insects or worms; they put down a layer of good soil enriched with a little lime; they surrounded it with a palisade; then the grain was pressed into the moist bed.

Did it not seem as if these colonists were laying the first stone of an edifice? It reminded Pencroff of the day when he had lighted his single match, and all the care he had brought to that operation. But this time the matter was more serious. In fact, the castaways would always have succeeded in procuring fire, by one process or another, but no human power could ever have remade that grain of wheat for them, if, by misfortune, it had perished!
