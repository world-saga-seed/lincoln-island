# The Bell, the Wire and the Nautilus (draft 1)

CHAPTER LVII.

THE VOLCANO AWAKES—THE FINE SEASON—WORK RESUMED—THE EVENING OF THE 15TH OF OCTOBER—A TELEGRAM—A QUESTION—AN ANSWER—DEPARTURE FOR THE CORRAL—THE NOTICE—THE ADDITIONAL WIRE—THE BASALT COAST—AT HIGH TIDE—AT LOW TIDE—THE CAVERN—A DAZZLING LIGHT.

The colonists, warned by the engineer, had left off their work, and were gazing in silence at the summit of Mount Franklin.

The volcano, then, had awoke, and the vapor had pierced the mineral layer heaped up at the bottom of the crater. But would the subterranean fires provoke some violent eruption? That was a possibility which no one could prevent.

However, even admitting the hypothesis of an eruption, it was probable that Lincoln Island would not suffer from it as a whole. Outpourings of volcanic matter are not always disastrous. The island had already undergone this trial, as was shown by the streams of lava which streaked the northern slopes of the mountain. Besides, the shape of the crater, and the breach hollowed out in its upper edge, must throw the matter vomited forth away from the fertile parts of the island.

Nevertheless, the past was not necessarily a pledge for the future. Often, at the summit of volcanoes, old craters close and new ones open. This has happened in both worlds, at Aetna, at Popocatepelt, at Orizaba; and on the eve of an eruption everything may be feared. It needed, in short, only an earthquake—a phenomenon which sometimes accompanies volcanic outpourings—for the interior arrangement of the mountain to be altered, and for new ways to be opened to the incandescent lava.

Smith explained these things to his companions, and, without exaggerating the situation, he made known to them all that could be said for and against.

After all, nothing could be done. Granite House, unless there were an earthquake to shake the ground, did not seem likely to be threatened. But the corral would have everything to fear, if some new crater should open in the southern sides of Mount Franklin.

From that day the vapors never ceased to crown the summit of the mountain with a plume, and it could even be seen that they were gaining in height and in thickness, without any flame mingling with their heavy volumes. The phenomenon was still concentrated in the lower part of the central chimney.

Meanwhile, with the fine days, work had been resumed. The building of the vessel was pushed on as fast as possible, and, by means of the fall on the shore, Smith succeeded in setting up a hydraulic sawmill, which cut up the trunks of trees into planks and joists more rapidly. The mechanism of this apparatus was as simple as those which work in the rustic sawmills of Norway. A first, horizontal movement to be given to the piece of wood, a second, vertical movement to be given to the saw, was all that had to be obtained; and the engineer succeeded in it by means of a wheel, two cylinders, and pulleys, suitably arranged.

Towards the end of the month of September the hull of the vessel, which was to be rigged as a schooner, stood upon the building-yard. The ribs were almost entirely finished, and, all the frames being held together by a temporary band, the lines of the craft could already be judged. This schooner, sharp in the bows and very slender in her run aft, would evidently be fit for a tolerably long voyage, should occasion arise; but the laying of the planking, of the inner lining, and of the deck must still require a considerable time. Very fortunately, the iron-work of the old brig had been saved after the submarine explosion. From the mutilated planks and knees Pencroff and Ayrton had drawn out the bolts and a great quantity of copper nails. It was so much labor saved for the smiths, but the carpenters had much to do.

The building had to be interrupted for a week for the harvest, the haymaking, and the gathering in of the various crops which abounded on Prospect Plateau. This task finished, every moment was thenceforth given to the completion of the schooner.

When night came, the workers were thoroughly worn out. So as to lose no time, they had changed the hours of their meals; they dined at noon, and did not sup until the light of day failed them. They then went up to Granite House, and made haste to go to bed.

Sometimes, however, when the conversation turned upon some interesting subject, it put off the hour of sleep a little. The colonists let themselves talk of the future, and they spoke willingly of the changes which a voyage of the schooner to the nearest lands would bring to their situation. But in the midst of these plans the thought of a later return to Lincoln Island always prevailed. Never would they abandon this colony, founded with so much toil and so much success, and to which communications with America would give a fresh development.

Pencroff and Neb especially hoped to end their days there.

“Herbert,” said the sailor, “you will never abandon Lincoln Island?”

“Never, Pencroff, and especially if you make up your mind to stay here!”

“It is quite made up, my boy,” answered Pencroff; “I shall wait for you! You will bring me your wife and your children, and I shall make famous young fellows of your little ones!”

“That is agreed,” replied Herbert, laughing and blushing at the same time.

“And you, Mr. Smith,” resumed Pencroff, enthusiastically, “you shall always be the governor of the island! Ah, now! how many inhabitants will it be able to feed? Ten thousand, at least!”

They talked in this way; they let Pencroff run on; and, from one thing to another, the reporter ended by founding a newspaper, the New Lincoln Herald!

So it is with the heart of man. The need of doing a work which shall endure, which shall survive him, is the sign of his superiority over everything that lives here below. It is this which has founded his dominion, and it is this which justifies it throughout the whole world.

After all, who knows whether Jup and Top had not, they too, their little dream of the future?

Ayrton, silent, said to himself that he would like to see Lord Glenarvan again, and to show himself to all, rehabilitated.

One evening, the 15th of October, the conversation, carried on among these hypotheses, had been prolonged later than usual. It was nine o’clock in the evening. Already long yawns, ill concealed, were sounding the hour of rest, and Pencroff had just made for his bed, when the electric bell, placed in the hall, suddenly rang.

All were there, Smith, Spilett, Herbert, Ayrton, Pencroff, Neb. Not one of the colonists, then, was at the corral.

Smith had risen. His companions looked at one another, thinking that they had heard wrong.

“What does that mean?” cried Neb. “Is it the devil who is ringing?”

No one answered.

“The weather is stormy,” observed Herbert. “Might not the influence of the electricity—”

Herbert did not finish his sentence. The engineer, towards whom all eyes were turned, was shaking his head.

“Let us wait,” then said Spilett. “If it is a signal, whoever it may be who makes it, he will repeat it.”

“But who do you suppose it can be?” cried Neb.

“Why,” answered Pencroff, “he who—”

The sailor’s sentence was cut short by a fresh quiver of the hammer upon the bell.

Smith went to the apparatus, and, sending the current through the wire, he dispatched this question to the corral:—

“What do you want?”

A few moments later the needle, moving over the alphabetical dial, gave this answer to the inmates of Granite House:—

“Come to the corral with all speed.”

“At last!” cried Smith.

Yes! At last! The mystery was about to be unveiled! Before this immense interest which was to urge them to the corral, all the colonists’ fatigue had vanished, all need of rest had ceased. Without having uttered a word, in a few moments they had left Granite House and were upon the beach. Jup and Top alone had been left behind; they could do without them.

The night was dark. The moon, new that very day, had disappeared at the same time as the sun. As Herbert had remarked, great storm-clouds formed a low and heavy vault, which shut out all light of the stars. A few flashes of heat lightning, the reflections of a distant storm, lit up the horizon.

It was possible that, a few hours later, the thunder would roll over the island itself, and it was a threatening night.

But the darkness, however deep it was, could not stop men accustomed to this road to the corral. They went up the left bank of the Mercy, reached the plateau, crossed the bridge over Glycerine Creek, and advanced through the forest.

They walked at a good pace, a prey to very lively emotion. For them there was no doubt of it; they were at last about to learn the long-sought answer to the riddle, the name of this mysterious being, who had entered so deeply into their life, so generous in his influence, so powerful in his action! Must not this unknown, in fact, have been mixed up with their existence, have known its smallest details, have heard all that was said in Granite House, to have been able always to act at the right moment?

Each, absorbed in his own reflections, hastened his steps. Under this vault of trees the darkness was such that even the edge of the road was hidden. Not a sound, besides, in the forest. Beasts and birds, affected by the heaviness of the atmosphere, were motionless and silent. Not a breath stirred the leaves. The footsteps of the colonists alone sounded, in the darkness, upon the hardened ground.

The silence, during the first quarter of an hour’s march, was broken only by this remark of Pencroff’s:—

“We ought to have brought a lantern.”

And by this reply from the engineer:—

“We shall find one at the corral.”

Smith and his companions had left Granite House at twelve minutes past nine. At forty-seven minutes past nine they had gone three miles of the five which separated the mouth of the Mercy from the corral.

At that moment great whitish flashes spread out above the island, and drew in black the outlines of the foliage. These intense glares dazzled and blinded. The storm, evidently, could not be long in breaking. The flashes became by degrees quicker and more brilliant. Distant growls rolled through the depths of the sky. The atmosphere was stifling.

The colonists went on as if they had been driven forward by some irresistible force.

At a quarter past nine a vivid flash showed them the palisaded enclosure, and they had not passed the gate when the thunder burst with tremendous violence.

In an instant the corral was crossed, and Smith stood before the dwelling.

It was possible that the house was occupied by the unknown, since it was from the house itself that the telegram must have been sent. However, no light shone from its window.

The engineer knocked at the door.

No answer.

Smith opened the door, and the colonists entered the room, which was in utter darkness.

Neb struck the steel, and an instant afterwards the lantern was lighted and carried into every corner of the room....

There was no one there. Things were in the state in which they had been left.

“Have we been the dupes of an illusion?” murmured Smith.

No! It was not possible! The telegram had said plainly:—

“Come to the corral with all speed.”

They went up to the table which was specially set apart for the service of the wire. Everything was in its place, the battery and the box which contained it, as well as the receiving and transmitting apparatus.

“Who was here the last time?” asked the engineer.

“I, Mr. Smith,” answered Ayrton.

“And that was—?”

“Four days ago.”

“Ah! a notice!” cried Herbert, pointing to a paper laid upon the table.

On this paper these words were written, in English:—

“Follow the new wire.”

“Let us go!” cried Smith, who understood that the dispatch had not been sent from the corral, but from the mysterious retreat, which an additional wire, joined to the old one, connected directly with Granite House.

Neb took the lighted lantern, and all left the corral.

The storm was then raging with extreme violence. The interval between each flash and each peal of thunder was sensibly diminishing. The meteor would soon be over Mount Franklin and the whole island. By the gleam of the intermittent flashes the summit of the volcano could be seen, plumed with vapor.

In all that part of the corral which lay between the house and the palisaded enclosure there was no telegraphic communication. But, after passing the gate, the engineer, running straight to the first post, saw by the light of a flash that a new wire hung down from the insulator to the ground.

“There it is!” said he.

This wire trailed along the ground, but along its whole length it was surrounded with an insulating substance, as a submarine cable is, which ensured the free passage of the currents. By its direction it appeared to pass through the woods and the southern spurs of the mountain, and consequently it ran towards the west.

“Let us follow it!” said Smith.

And, now by the light of the lantern, now amid the flashes of the lightning, the colonists dashed along the way traced by the wire.

The peals of thunder were continuous then, and their violence such that no word could have been heard. Besides, there was no question of talking; the thing was to go forward.

Smith and his companions first climbed the spur which rose between the valley of the corral and that of Fall River, which they crossed at its narrowest part. The wire, now stretched over the low branches of the trees, now running along the ground, guided them surely.

The engineer had supposed that the wire would perhaps stop at the bottom of the valley, and that there the unknown retreat would be.

It was nothing of the sort. They had to climb the south-western spur, and to go down again upon that arid plateau which ended in the wall of basalt so strangely heaped up. From time to time one or other of the colonists stooped, felt the wire with his hand, and corrected the direction if need were. But there was no longer any doubt that this wire ran directly to the sea. There, no doubt, in some depth of the igneous rocks, lay hollowed out the dwelling so vainly sought until then.

The sky was on fire. One flash did not wait for another. Several struck the summit of the volcano and plunged into the crater amid the thick smoke. One might have thought, at moments, that the mountain was throwing out flames.

A few minutes before ten o’clock the colonists had arrived upon the high edge which overlooked the ocean on the west. The wind had risen. The surf was roaring five hundred feet below.

Smith reckoned that his companions and he had gone a distance of a mile and a half from the corral.

At this point the wire entered among the rocks, following the rather steep slope of a narrow and winding ravine.

The colonists entered it, at the risk of bringing down some fall of ill-balanced rocks and of being hurled into the sea. The descent was extremely perilous, but they took no account of the danger; they were no longer masters of themselves, and an irresistible attraction drew them towards this mysterious point, as the magnet draws iron.

Thus they went down, almost unconsciously, this ravine, which, even in broad daylight, would have been, so to speak, impracticable. The stones rolled down, and shone like fiery meteors when they crossed the zones of light. Smith was at the head. Ayrton brought up the rear. Here they went step by step; there they slid upon the polished rock; then they picked themselves up and continued their way.

At last the wire, making a sharp turn, touched the rocks of the shore, a regular scattering of reefs which the spring tides must beat upon. The colonists had reached the lower limit of the basalt wall.

There ran a narrow ledge, which went horizontally and parallel with the sea. The wire followed it, and the colonists entered upon it. They had not gone a hundred paces when the ledge, sloping by a moderate incline, came down to the very level of the waves.

The engineer seized the wire, and saw that it went down into the sea.

His companions, who had stopped near him, were stupefied.

A cry of disappointment, almost a cry of despair, escaped them! Must they, then, throw themselves under these waters and seek there some submarine cavern? In the state of moral and physical excitement in which they were, they would not have hesitated to do it.

A remark from the engineer stopped them.

Smith led his companions under a hollow in the rocks, and there:—

“Let us wait,” said he. “The tide is high. At low water the way will be open.”

“But what can make you believe—?” asked Pencroff.

“He would not have called us, if the means of reaching him were to fail!”

Smith had spoken in a tone of such conviction that no objection was raised; his remark, besides, was logical. It had to be admitted that an opening, practicable at low water, which the flood was blocking at that moment, opened at the foot of the wall.

There were some hours to wait. The colonists therefore remained silently crouching under a sort of deep portico hollowed out in the rock. The rain was then beginning to fall, and soon the clouds, torn by the lightning, were condensed in torrents. The echoes repeated the crash of the thunder and gave it a grand resonance.

The emotion of the colonists was extreme. A thousand strange, supernatural thoughts crossed their brains, and they called up some grand and superhuman apparition, which alone could have answered to the idea they had formed of the mysterious genius of the island.

At midnight Smith, taking the lantern, went down to the level of the beach, so as to observe the disposition of the rocks. The tide had already been ebbing for two hours.

The engineer had not been mistaken. The arch of a vast excavation was beginning to show itself above the water. There the wire, bending at a right angle, entered this yawning mouth.

Smith returned to his companions, and said to them simply:—

“In an hour the opening will be practicable.”

“It is there, then?” asked Pencroff.

“Did you doubt it?” answered Smith.

“But this cavern will be filled with water to a certain height,” observed Herbert.

“Either the cavern dries completely,” answered Smith, “and in that case we shall go through it on foot; or it does not dry, and some means of transport will be put at our disposal.”

An hour passed. All went down through the rain to the level of the sea. In three hours the tide had fallen fifteen feet. The top of the arch traced by the vault stood at least eight feet above its level. It was like the arch of a bridge, under which the water passed, mingled with foam.

Leaning forward, the engineer saw a black object floating on the surface of the sea. He drew it towards him.

It was a boat, moored by a rope to some projection on the inside of the wall. This boat was made of bolted sheet-iron. Two oars lay at the bottom, under the seats.

“Let us embark,” said Smith.

A moment later the colonists were in the boat. Neb and Ayrton had taken the oars, Pencroff the rudder. Smith, in the bow, the lantern placed upon the stem, lighted the way.

The vault, very low, under which the boat first passed, rose suddenly; but the darkness was too deep, and the light of the lantern too feeble, for the extent of this cavern, its breadth, its height, its depth, to be made out. In the midst of this basaltic substructure reigned an imposing silence. No sound from without penetrated into it, and the peals of the thunder could not pierce its thick walls.

There exist in some parts of the globe such immense caverns, a sort of natural crypts, which date from its geological epoch. Some are invaded by the waters of the sea; others hold entire lakes within their sides. Such is Fingal’s Cave, in the Island of Staffa, one of the Hebrides; such are the caves of Morgat, on the Bay of Douarnenez, in Brittany, the caves of Bonifacio, in Corsica, those of the Lyse-Fjord, in Norway; such is the immense Mammoth Cave, in Kentucky, five hundred feet high and more than twenty miles long! In several parts of the globe nature has hollowed out these crypts, and has preserved them for the admiration of man.

As to this cavern which the colonists were then exploring, did it, then, extend to the centre of the island? For a quarter of an hour the boat had been advancing, making windings which the engineer pointed out to Pencroff in a curt voice, when, at a certain moment:—

“More to the right!” he commanded.

The boat, changing its direction, came at once alongside the right-hand wall. The engineer wished, and with reason, to make sure whether the wire still ran along this wall.

The wire was there, hooked to the projections of the rock.

“Forward!” said Smith.

And the two oars, plunging into the black waters, drove the boat on.

The boat went on for a quarter of an hour more, and, since the opening of the cavern, it must have gone a distance of half a mile, when the voice of Smith was heard again.

“Stop!” said he.

The boat stopped, and the colonists perceived a brilliant light which illuminated the enormous crypt, hollowed out so deep in the bowels of the island.

It was then possible to examine this cavern, the existence of which nothing could have led them to suspect.

At a height of a hundred feet rose a rounded vault, supported upon shafts of basalt, which seemed all to have been cast in the same mould. Irregular springings and capricious ribs rested upon these columns, which nature had set up by thousands in the first epochs of the formation of the globe. The basaltic pieces, fitted one into another, measured from forty to fifty feet in height, and the water, calm in spite of the tumult without, washed their base. The brilliance of the focus of light which the engineer had noticed seized every prismatic edge and tipped it with points of fire. It penetrated, so to speak, the walls, as if they had been transparent, and changed the least projections of this substructure into so many sparkling cabochons.

By a phenomenon of reflection the water reproduced these various brilliances upon its surface, so that the boat seemed to float between two sparkling zones.

There could be no mistake as to the nature of the radiation thrown out by the luminous centre, whose rays, sharp and straight, were broken at every angle, at every rib of the crypt. This light came from an electric source, and its white color betrayed its origin. It was the sun of this cavern, and it filled the whole of it.

At a sign from Smith the oars fell again, raising a regular shower of carbuncles, and the boat made for the luminous focus, from which it was soon no more than half a cable’s length.

At this place the breadth of the sheet of water measured about three hundred and fifty feet, and beyond the dazzling centre an enormous basaltic wall could be seen, which closed every outlet on that side. The cavern, then, had widened considerably, and the sea formed there a little lake. But the vault, the side walls, the wall of the apse, all these prisms, all these cylinders, all these cones, were bathed in the electric fluid to such a degree that the brilliance seemed to be their own; and one might have said of these stones, cut in facets like diamonds of great price, that they sweated light!

In the centre of the lake a long spindle-shaped object floated on the surface of the water, silent, motionless. The brilliance which came from it escaped from its sides, as from two furnace mouths heated to a white welding heat. This apparatus, like the body of an enormous cetacean, was about two hundred and fifty feet long, and rose from ten to twelve feet above the level of the sea.

The boat approached it slowly. In the bow Smith had risen. He was gazing, a prey to violent agitation. Then, all at once, seizing the reporter’s arm:—

“But it is he! It can be no one but he!” he cried, “he!—”

Then he fell back upon his seat, murmuring a name which Spilett alone heard.

No doubt the reporter knew this name, for it had a prodigious effect upon him, and he answered in a hollow voice:—

“He! a man outside the law!”

“He!” said Smith.

At the engineer’s order the boat approached this singular floating apparatus. The boat came alongside the left quarter, from which a sheaf of light escaped through a thick pane of glass.

Smith and his companions mounted upon the platform. An open hatchway was there. All rushed through the opening.

At the foot of the ladder an inner gangway could be made out, lighted by electricity. At the end of this gangway was a door, which Smith pushed open.

A richly decorated room, which the colonists crossed rapidly, adjoined a library, in which a luminous ceiling poured down a torrent of light.

At the end of the library a large door, likewise closed, was opened by the engineer.

A vast saloon, a sort of museum, in which were heaped up, with all the treasures of mineral nature, works of art and marvels of industry, appeared before the eyes of the colonists, who must have believed themselves transported by enchantment into the world of dreams.

Stretched upon a rich divan they saw a man, who did not appear to perceive their presence.

Then Smith raised his voice, and, to the extreme surprise of his companions, he uttered these words:—

“Captain Nemo, you asked for us? Here we are.”
