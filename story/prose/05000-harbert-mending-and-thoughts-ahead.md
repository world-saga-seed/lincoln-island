# Herbert Mending and Thoughts Ahead (final)

CHAPTER L.

THE CONVICTS IN THE NEIGHBORHOOD OF THE CORRAL—A PROVISIONAL INSTALLATION—CONTINUATION OF HERBERT'S TREATMENT—PENCROFF'S FIRST JUBILATIONS—A GLANCE AT THE PAST—WHAT THE FUTURE HOLDS—SMITH'S IDEAS UPON THE SUBJECT.

Thus the convicts were still there, watching the corral, and resolved to kill the colonists one after the other! There was nothing left but to treat them as wild beasts. But great precautions must be taken, for these wretches had, at that moment, the advantage of the situation: they saw and were not seen, and they could surprise by the suddenness of their attack without being surprised.

Smith therefore arranged matters so as to live at the corral, the stores of which, besides, would suffice for a tolerably long time. Ayrton's house had been provided with all that was necessary to life, and the convicts, frightened by the arrival of the colonists, had not had time to pillage it. It was probable, as Spilett observed, that things had passed as follows: the six convicts, having landed on the island, had followed its southern shore; and after going over the double shore of Serpentine Peninsula, being in no humor to venture under the woods of the Far West, they had reached the mouth of Fall River. Once at that point, by going up the right bank of the stream, they had arrived at the spurs of Mount Franklin, among which it was natural that they should seek some retreat, and they could not have been long in discovering the corral, then uninhabited. There they had probably established themselves, while waiting for the moment to put their abominable projects into execution. Ayrton's arrival had surprised them, but they had succeeded in seizing the unfortunate man, and—the rest could easily be guessed!

Now the convicts,—reduced to five, it is true, but well armed,—were prowling in the woods, and to venture there was to expose oneself to their shots, without any possibility either of warding them off or of forestalling them.

"Wait! There is nothing else to be done!" repeated Smith. "When Herbert is cured, we can organize a general beat of the island, and get the better of these convicts. That will be the object of our grand expedition, at the same time——"

"As the search for our mysterious protector," added Spilett, finishing the engineer's sentence. "Ah! it must be owned, my dear Cyrus, that this time his protection failed us, and at the very moment when it would have been most necessary to us!"

"Who knows!" replied the engineer.

"What do you mean?" asked the reporter.

"That we are not at the end of our troubles, my dear Spilett, and that the powerful intervention will perhaps yet have occasion to exert itself. But that is not the question now. Herbert's life before everything."

This was the most painful anxiety of the colonists. Some days passed, and the poor boy's state had happily grown no worse. And time gained upon the illness was much. The cold water, always kept at the proper temperature, had absolutely prevented the inflammation of the wounds. It even seemed to the reporter that this water, being a little sulphurous,—which the neighborhood of the volcano explained,—had a more direct action upon the healing. The suppuration was much less abundant, and, thanks to the incessant care with which he was surrounded, Herbert was returning to life, and his fever was tending to fall. He was, besides, kept to a severe diet, and consequently his weakness was, and must be, extreme; but he did not want for tisanes, and absolute rest did him the greatest good.

Smith, Spilett, and Pencroff had become very skilful in dressing the young wounded lad. All the linen of the dwelling had been sacrificed. Herbert's wounds, covered with compresses and lint, were bound neither too tight nor too loose, so as to bring about their healing without causing any inflammatory reaction. The reporter brought extreme care to these dressings, knowing well how important they were, and he repeated to his companions what most physicians readily acknowledge: that it is perhaps rarer to see a dressing well made than an operation well performed.

At the end of ten days, on the 22nd of November, Herbert was perceptibly better. He had begun to take some nourishment. The color was returning to his cheeks, and his kind eyes smiled at his nurses. He talked a little, in spite of the efforts of Pencroff, who, for his part, talked the whole time to prevent him from speaking, and told the most improbable stories. Herbert had questioned him about Ayrton, whom he was astonished not to see near him, as he thought that he must be at the corral. But the sailor, not wishing to grieve Herbert, had contented himself with answering that Ayrton had joined Neb, in order to defend Granite House.

"Ha!" he would say, "these pirates! Here are gentlemen who have no longer any right to consideration! And Mr. Smith, who wanted to take them by sentiment! I will send them sentiment, I will, but in good lead of the proper calibre!"

"And they have not been seen again?" asked Herbert.

"No, my boy," answered the sailor, "but we shall find them again, and when you are cured we shall see if these cowards, who strike from behind, will dare to attack us face to face!"

"I am still very weak, my poor Pencroff!"

"Eh! your strength will come back little by little! What is a ball through the chest? A mere joke! I have seen plenty of others, and I am none the worse for them!"

In short, things appeared to be going for the best, and, so long as no complication arose, Herbert's recovery might be regarded as assured. But what would have been the situation of the colonists if his state had grown worse; if, for example, the ball had remained in his body, or if his arm or his leg had had to be amputated!

"No," said Spilett more than once, "I have never thought of such a possibility without shuddering!"

"And yet, if it had been necessary to act," Smith answered him one day, "you would not have hesitated?"

"No, Cyrus!" said Spilett; "but God be blessed for having spared us that complication!"

As in so many other conjunctures, the colonists had appealed to that logic of simple good sense which had so often served them, and once more, thanks to their general knowledge, they had succeeded! But would not the moment come when all their science would be at fault? They were alone upon this island. Now, men complete one another in the state of society; they are necessary to one another. Smith knew it well, and sometimes he asked himself whether some circumstance might not arise which they would be powerless to surmount!

It seemed to him, besides, that his companions and he, hitherto so fortunate, had entered upon an unlucky period. In the more than two years and a half since they had escaped from Richmond, it might be said that everything had gone according to their wishes. The island had abundantly furnished them with minerals, vegetables, and animals. If nature had constantly loaded them with her bounty, their science had known how to turn to account what she offered them. The material well-being of the colony was, so to speak, complete. Moreover, in certain circumstances, an inexplicable influence had come to their aid!... but all that could only last for a time!

In short, Smith believed that he perceived their luck to be turning against them.

In fact, the convicts' ship had appeared in the waters of the island, and if these pirates had been, so to speak, miraculously destroyed, six of them, at least, had escaped the catastrophe. They had landed on the island, and the five who survived were almost impossible to seize. Ayrton had been, without any doubt, massacred by these wretches, who possessed firearms; and at the first use they had made of them, Herbert had fallen, struck almost mortally. Were these, then, the first blows which adverse fortune was dealing the colonists? That was what Smith asked himself! That was what he often repeated to the reporter; and it seemed to them also that this intervention, so strange but so efficacious, which had served them so well until then, now failed them. Had this mysterious being, whoever he might be, whose existence they could not deny, abandoned the island, then? Had he in his turn succumbed?

To these questions no answer was possible. But let no one imagine that Smith and his companion, because they talked of these things, were men to despair! Far from it. They looked the situation in the face, they analyzed the chances, they prepared themselves for every event, they stood firm and upright before the future. If adversity was at last to strike them, it would find in them men prepared to combat it.
