# The Foxes, the Launch and the Bottle (draft 1)

CHAPTER XXXIV.

THE RIGGING OF THE BOAT—AN ATTACK OF CULPEUX—JUP WOUNDED—JUP NURSED—JUP CURED—COMPLETION OF THE BOAT—PENCROFF'S TRIUMPH—THE BONADVENTURE—FIRST TRIAL TO THE SOUTH OF THE ISLAND—AN UNEXPECTED DOCUMENT.

That same evening the hunters returned. They had had good sport, and, literally loaded with game, they carried all that four men could carry.

Top wore a string of pintails round his neck, and Jup belts of snipe round his body.

"Here, master," cried Neb, "here is something to employ our time! Preserves, pies, we shall have a pleasant reserve there! But some one must help me. I count on you, Pencroff."

"No, Neb," replied the sailor. "The rigging of the boat claims me, and you will have to do without me."

"And you, Master Herbert?"

"I, Neb, must go to the corral to-morrow," replied the lad.

"Then it will be you, Mr. Spilett, who will help me?"

"To oblige you, Neb," replied the reporter; "but I warn you that if you reveal your recipes to me, I shall publish them."

"As you please, Mr. Spilett," replied Neb, "as you please!"

And that is how, the next day, Spilett, become Neb's assistant, was installed in his culinary laboratory. But before this the engineer had told him the result of the exploration which he had made the day before; and on this point the reporter shared the opinion of Smith, that, although he had found nothing, there still remained a secret to discover!

The cold lasted a week longer, and the colonists did not leave Granite House, except to attend to the poultry-yard. The dwelling was perfumed with the good odors given out by the learned manipulations of Neb and the reporter. But not all the produce of the hunt in the marshes was turned into preserves; and as the game kept perfectly in this intense cold, wild ducks and others were eaten fresh, and declared superior to all other aquatic creatures of the known world.

During this week Pencroff, aided by Herbert, who handled the sailmaker's needle skilfully, worked with such ardor that the sails of the boat were finished. Hemp cordage was not wanting, thanks to the rigging which had been recovered with the envelope of the balloon. The cables, the cords of the net, were all made of excellent rope, of which the sailor made good use. The sails were edged with strong bolt-ropes, and there still remained enough to make the halyards, the shrouds, the sheets, etc. As to the blocks, by Pencroff's advice, and by means of the lathe which he had set up, Smith made the pulleys required. It thus happened that the rigging was entirely ready long before the boat was finished. Pencroff even made a flag, blue, red and white, the colors of which had been furnished by certain dye-plants, very abundant in the island. Only, to the thirty-seven stars representing the thirty-seven States of the Union, which shine in the canton of the American flags, the sailor had added a thirty-eighth, the star of "the State of Lincoln"; for he considered his island as already joined to the great republic.

"And," said he, "it is so in heart, if it is not yet in fact!"

In the meantime this flag was hoisted at the central window of Granite House, and the colonists saluted it with three cheers.

The cold season was now drawing to its end, and it seemed that this second winter was to pass without serious incident, when, on the night of the 11th of August, Prospect Plateau was threatened with complete devastation.

After a well-filled day the colonists were sleeping soundly, when, towards four o'clock in the morning, they were suddenly awakened by the barking of Top.

The dog was not barking this time near the mouth of the well, but at the threshold of the door, and he threw himself against it as if he wished to break it in. Jup, on his side, was uttering sharp cries.

"Well, Top!" cried Neb, who was the first awake.

But the dog went on barking with more fury.

"What is the matter?" asked Smith.

And all, hastily dressed, rushed to the windows of the room and opened them.

Beneath their eyes spread a sheet of snow, which looked scarcely white in this very dark night. The colonists saw nothing, but they heard a strange barking which broke out in the darkness. It was evident that the beach had been invaded by a number of animals which could not be distinguished.

"What is it?" cried Pencroff.

"Wolves, jaguars, or monkeys!" replied Neb.

"The devil! But they can reach the top of the plateau!" said the reporter.

"And our poultry-yard," cried Herbert, "and our plantations?..."

"Which way can they have come?" asked Pencroff.

"They must have crossed the little bridge on the beach," replied the engineer, "which one of us must have forgotten to close."

"Indeed," said Spilett, "I remember having left it open..."

"A fine thing you have done there, Mr. Spilett!" cried the sailor.

"What is done is done," replied Smith. "Let us consider what must be done!"

Such were the questions and answers rapidly exchanged between Smith and his companions. It was certain that the little bridge had been crossed, that the beach was invaded by animals, and that these, whatever they might be, could, by going up the left bank of the Mercy, reach Prospect Plateau. They must therefore outstrip them, and fight them if need be.

"But what are these beasts?" was asked a second time, at the moment when the barking rang out more loudly.

This barking made Herbert start, and he remembered having heard it already during his first visit to the sources of Red Creek.

"They are culpeux, they are foxes!" said he.

"Forward!" cried the sailor.

And all, arming themselves with hatchets, carbines and revolvers, rushed into the basket of the elevator and set foot on the beach.

These culpeux are dangerous animals, when they are in great numbers and hunger has made them furious. Nevertheless the colonists did not hesitate to throw themselves into the midst of the troop, and their first revolver-shots, darting rapid flashes into the darkness, drove back the foremost assailants.

What mattered above all was to prevent these plunderers from reaching Prospect Plateau; for the plantations and the poultry-yard would have been at their mercy, and immense damage, perhaps irreparable, especially as regarded the wheat-field, would inevitably have been done. But as the plateau could be invaded only by the left bank of the Mercy, it was enough to oppose to the culpeux an insurmountable barrier on that narrow strip of the bank which lay between the river and the granite wall.

This was understood by all, and, at an order from Smith, they reached the appointed place, while the troop of culpeux bounded about in the darkness.

Smith, Spilett, Herbert, Pencroff and Neb therefore placed themselves so as to form a line that could not be crossed. Top, his formidable jaws open, went before the colonists, and he was followed by Jup, armed with a knotty cudgel, which he brandished like a club.

The night was extremely dark. It was only by the flash of the discharges, each of which had to tell, that the assailants could be seen. They must have been at least a hundred, and their eyes shone like embers.

"They must not pass!" cried Pencroff.

"They shall not pass!" replied the engineer.

But if they did not pass, it was not for want of trying. The rear ranks pushed on the foremost, and it was an incessant struggle with revolver and with hatchet. Many carcasses of culpeux must already have strewn the ground, but the troop seemed undiminished, and it might have been said that it was ceaselessly renewed by way of the little bridge on the beach.

Soon the colonists were obliged to fight hand to hand, and they had received some wounds, very fortunately slight ones. Herbert, with a shot from his revolver, had rid Neb of a culpeux which had just sprung upon his back like a tiger-cat. Top fought with real fury, leaping at the throats of the foxes and strangling them outright. Jup, armed with his stick, laid about him with all his might, and it was in vain that they tried to keep him in the rear. Gifted, no doubt, with a sight which allowed him to pierce this darkness, he was always in the thick of the fight, and from time to time he uttered a shrill whistle, which was with him the mark of extreme jubilation. At one moment he even advanced so far that, by the flash of a revolver, he could be seen surrounded by five or six large culpeux, against whom he held his ground with rare coolness.

However, the struggle was to end to the advantage of the colonists, but only after they had held out for two long hours! The first glimmer of dawn, no doubt, decided the retreat of the assailants, who scampered off towards the north, so as to recross the little bridge, which Neb ran to raise immediately.

When the day had lighted the field of battle sufficiently, the colonists could count some fifty carcasses scattered over the beach.

"And Jup!" cried Pencroff. "Where is Jup?"

Jup had disappeared. His friend Neb called him, and, for the first time, Jup did not answer the call of his friend.

Each one set out in search of Jup, trembling lest he should be counted among the dead. The place was cleared of the carcasses, which stained the snow with their blood, and Jup was found in the midst of a perfect heap of culpeux, whose shattered jaws and broken backs showed that they had had to do with the terrible cudgel of the intrepid animal. Poor Jup still held in his hand the stump of his broken stick; but, deprived of his weapon, he had been overpowered by numbers, and deep wounds furrowed his breast.

"He is alive!" cried Neb, who bent over him.

"And we will save him," replied the sailor; "we will nurse him like one of ourselves!"

It seemed that Jup understood, for he leaned his head on the shoulder of Pencroff, as if to thank him. The sailor was wounded himself, but his wounds, like those of his companions, were insignificant; for, thanks to their fire-arms, they had nearly always been able to keep the assailants at a distance. Only the orang, therefore, was in a serious state.

Jup, carried by Neb and Pencroff, was brought to the elevator, and scarcely a feeble moan escaped his lips. He was gently raised to Granite House. There he was laid upon one of the mattresses taken from one of the beds, and his wounds were washed with the greatest care. It did not appear that they had reached any vital organ; but Jup had been much weakened by the loss of his blood, and a fever set in of some severity.

He was put to bed, then, after his wounds were dressed; a strict diet was imposed on him, "just like a natural person," said Neb; and he was made to drink a few cups of a cooling tisane, the ingredients of which were furnished by the vegetable pharmacy of Granite House.

Jup fell asleep, his sleep at first restless; but by degrees his breathing became more regular, and he was left to rest in the greatest quiet. From time to time Top, walking, one may say, "on tiptoe," came to visit his friend, and seemed to approve of all the care that was taken of him. One of Jup's hands hung out of the bed, and Top licked it with a contrite air.

That same morning they proceeded to the burial of the dead, which were dragged as far as the forest of the Far West and buried deep.

This attack, which might have had such grave consequences, was a lesson to the colonists; and from that time they never went to bed until one of them had made sure that all the bridges were raised, and that no invasion was possible.

Meanwhile Jup, after causing serious fears for some days, rallied vigorously against the illness. His constitution carried the day, the fever abated little by little, and Spilett, who was something of a doctor, soon considered him out of danger. On the 16th of August Jup began to eat. Neb made him nice little sweet dishes, which the invalid relished with gusto; for, if he had one pet failing, it was that of being a trifle greedy, and Neb had never done anything to cure him of that fault.

"What would you have?" he would say to Spilett, who sometimes reproached him with spoiling him. "He has no other pleasure but that of his mouth, poor Jup, and I am only too happy to be able to repay his services in this way!"

Ten days after taking to his bed, on the 21st of August, Master Jup got up. His wounds were healed, and it was plain that he would soon recover his usual suppleness and vigor. Like all convalescents, he was then seized with a devouring hunger, and the reporter let him eat as he pleased; for he trusted to that instinct which is too often wanting in reasoning beings, and which was to preserve the orang from all excess. Neb was delighted to see the appetite of his pupil return.

"Eat, my Jup," he would say to him, "and want for nothing! You have shed your blood for us, and the least I can do is to help you make it again!"

At last, on the 25th of August, the voice of Neb was heard calling his companions.

"Mr. Smith, Mr. Gideon, Master Herbert, Pencroff, come! come!"

The colonists, who were gathered in the great hall, rose at the call of Neb, who was then in the room set apart for Jup.

"What is the matter?" asked the reporter.

"Look!" replied Neb, with a great burst of laughter.

And what did they see? Master Jup, smoking, quietly and seriously, squatting like a Turk at the door of Granite House!

"My pipe!" cried Pencroff. "He has taken my pipe! Ah! my good Jup, I make you a present of it! Smoke, my friend, smoke!"

And Jup gravely puffed out thick clouds of tobacco, which seemed to give him unequalled enjoyment.

Smith showed no great astonishment at the incident, and he cited several examples of tamed monkeys to whom the use of tobacco had become familiar.

But from that day Master Jup had his own pipe, the sailor's ex-pipe, which was hung up in his room, near his store of tobacco. He filled it himself, he lighted it at a live coal, and appeared to be the happiest of quadrumana. It may well be supposed that this community of taste only drew closer the bonds of friendship which already united the worthy monkey and the honest sailor.

"Perhaps he is a man," Pencroff would sometimes say to Neb. "Would it astonish you if one day he began to speak to us?"

"My word, no," Neb would answer. "What astonishes me is rather that he does not speak, for, after all, he wants nothing but speech!"

"It would amuse me all the same," the sailor would go on, "if one fine day he said to me: 'Suppose we change pipes, Pencroff!'"

"Yes," Neb would reply. "What a pity that he is dumb from his birth!"

With the month of September the winter was entirely over, and the works were resumed with ardor.

The building of the boat advanced rapidly. She was already entirely planked, and she was framed inside, so as to bind together all the parts of the hull, with timbers softened by steam, which lent themselves to all the requirements of the mould.

As wood was not wanting, Pencroff proposed to the engineer to line the hull inside with a water-tight ceiling, which would make the boat entirely secure.

Smith, not knowing what the future had in store, approved of the sailor's idea of making his boat as strong as possible.

The ceiling and the deck of the boat were entirely finished towards the 15th of September. To caulk the seams, oakum was made of dry zostera, which was driven in with a mallet between the planks of the hull, of the ceiling and of the deck; then these seams were covered with boiling tar, which the pines of the forest furnished in abundance.

The fitting of the boat was of the simplest. She had first been ballasted with heavy pieces of granite set in a bed of lime, of which about twelve thousand pounds were stowed. A flooring was laid over this ballast, and the interior was divided into two cabins, along which ran two benches that served as lockers. The foot of the mast was to prop the bulkhead which separated the two cabins; these were reached by two hatchways, opening on the deck and fitted with covers.

Pencroff had no trouble in finding a tree suitable for the mast. He chose a young fir, very straight, without knots, which he had only to square at the step and to round at the head. The iron-work of the mast, that of the rudder and that of the hull, had been roughly but solidly made at the forge of the Chimneys. Lastly, yards, topmast, boom, spars, oars, etc., all were finished in the first week of October; and it was agreed that the boat should be tried in the waters near the island, in order to learn how she behaved at sea, and how far she might be trusted.

During all this time the necessary work had not been neglected. The corral was refitted, for the flock of mouflons and goats counted a certain number of young ones, which had to be housed and fed. The colonists had not failed to visit the oyster-bed, the warren, the beds of coal and iron, and some parts, till then unexplored, of the forests of the Far West, which abounded in game.

Some more native plants were discovered; and, if they were of no immediate use, they helped to vary the vegetable stores of Granite House. They were kinds of ficoides, some like those of the Cape, with fleshy leaves which could be eaten, others bearing seeds which held a sort of flour.

On the 10th of October the boat was launched. Pencroff was radiant. The operation succeeded perfectly. The boat, completely rigged, having been pushed on rollers to the edge of the shore, was taken up by the rising tide, and floated amid the applause of the colonists, and particularly of Pencroff, who showed no modesty on this occasion. Besides, his vanity was to outlive the completion of the boat, since, after having built her, he was to be called upon to command her. The rank of captain was bestowed upon him with the consent of all.

To satisfy Captain Pencroff, it was first of all necessary to give the boat a name; and, after several proposals had been discussed at length, the votes were united upon that of Bonadventure, which was the Christian name of the honest sailor.

As soon as the Bonadventure had been lifted by the rising tide, it could be seen that she sat perfectly upon her water-lines, and that she would sail well on every point.

Besides, the trial was to be made that very day, in an excursion off the coast. The weather was fine, the breeze fresh, and the sea easy, above all along the southern shore, for the wind had been blowing from the north-west for an hour already.

"All aboard! all aboard!" cried Captain Pencroff.

But breakfast had to be eaten before starting, and it even seemed well to take provisions on board, in case the excursion should be prolonged till the evening.

Smith was equally eager to try this boat, the plans of which had come from him, although, on the advice of the sailor, he had often altered some parts of them. But he had not in her the confidence which Pencroff showed; and as the latter no longer spoke of the voyage to Tabor Island, Smith even hoped that the sailor had given it up. It would have been repugnant to him, indeed, to see two or three of his companions venture far on this bark, so small after all, which measured no more than fifteen tons.

At half-past ten every one was on board, even Jup, even Top. Neb and Herbert weighed the anchor, which was biting the sand near the mouth of the Mercy, the mainsail was hoisted, the Lincolnian flag floated from the masthead, and the Bonadventure, steered by Pencroff, stood out to sea.

To get out of Union Bay they had first to run before the wind, and it could be seen that on this point of sailing the speed of the boat was satisfactory.

After rounding Jetsam Point and Claw Cape, Pencroff was obliged to keep close-hauled, in order to follow the southern coast of the island; and, after making a few boards, he observed that the Bonadventure could sail about five points from the wind, and that she held up well against leeway. She went about very well in stays, having "way," as sailors say, and even gaining in her turning.

The passengers of the Bonadventure were truly delighted. They had there a good boat, which, should occasion arise, might render them great services; and in this fine weather, with this steady breeze, the sail was charming.

Pencroff stood out to sea, three or four miles from the coast, abreast of Balloon Harbor. The island then appeared in its whole extent and under a new aspect. There was the varied panorama of its shore from Claw Cape to Reptile End; there were its foregrounds of forest, in which the conifers still stood out against the young foliage of the other trees, scarcely in bud; and there was Mount Franklin, which overlooked the whole, its head whitened by a little snow.

"How beautiful it is!" cried Herbert.

"Yes, our island is beautiful and good," replied Pencroff. "I love it as I loved my poor mother! It received us, poor and in want of everything; and what is wanting now to these five children who fell to it from the sky?"

"Nothing!" replied Neb; "nothing, captain!"

And the two worthy fellows gave three tremendous cheers in honor of their island!

During this time Spilett, leaning against the foot of the mast, was sketching the panorama which spread before his eyes.

Smith looked on in silence.

"Well, Mr. Smith," asked Pencroff, "what do you say of our boat?"

"She seems to behave well," replied the engineer.

"Good! And do you think now that she could undertake a voyage of some length?"

"What voyage, Pencroff?"

"That to Tabor Island, for instance?"

"My friend," replied Smith, "I think that in a pressing case we ought not to hesitate to trust ourselves to the Bonadventure, even for a longer passage. But, you know, I should see you start for Tabor Island with pain, since nothing obliges you to go there."

"One likes to know one's neighbors," replied Pencroff, who was obstinate in his idea. "Tabor Island is our neighbor, and the only one! Politeness requires that we should go, at least, and pay it a visit!"

"The devil!" said Spilett; "our friend Pencroff is a stickler for the proprieties!"

"I am a stickler for nothing at all," retorted the sailor, whom the opposition of the engineer vexed a little, but who would not have wished to cause him any pain.

"Consider, Pencroff," replied Smith, "that you cannot go to Tabor Island alone."

"One companion will be enough for me."

"So be it," replied the engineer. "Then you risk depriving the colony of Lincoln Island of two colonists out of five?"

"Out of six!" replied Pencroff. "You forget Jup."

"Out of seven!" added Neb. "Top is quite worth another!"

"There is no risk, Mr. Smith," resumed Pencroff.

"That is possible, Pencroff; but, I repeat, it is exposing oneself without necessity!"

The obstinate sailor made no answer, and let the conversation drop, quite determined to take it up again. But he little suspected that an incident was about to come to his aid, and to change into a work of humanity what was only a whim, open to question after all.

In fact, after standing out to sea, the Bonadventure had just drawn near the coast again, steering towards Balloon Harbor. It was important to examine the passes left between the sand-banks and the reefs, in order to buoy them if need be, since this little creek was to be the boat's home port.

They were now only half a mile from the coast, and it had been necessary to tack in order to make way against the wind. The speed of the Bonadventure was then only very moderate, because the breeze, partly stopped by the high land, scarcely swelled her sails. And the sea, smooth as glass, was rippled only by the breath of the gusts which passed capriciously over it.

Herbert was standing in the bows, so as to point out the course to follow among the passes, when all at once he cried:—

"Luff, Pencroff, luff!"

"What is the matter?" replied the sailor, rising. "A rock?"

"No ... wait," said Herbert ... "I cannot see well ... luff again ... right ... bear away a little ..."

And so saying, Herbert, lying along the side, quickly plunged his arm into the water, and rose again, saying:—

"A bottle!"

He held in his hand a closed bottle, which he had just seized a few cables' lengths from the coast.

Smith took the bottle. Without saying a single word he drew the cork, and took out a damp paper, on which were read these words:—

"Castaway ... Tabor Island: 153° W. long.—37° 11′ S. lat."
