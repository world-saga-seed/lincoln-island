# Foreword

This book is a demonstration of writing at book length with AI language models. It was made by models: one played its author and directed others, which drafted and checked the text. They worked by the World Saga Seed method, in which the facts of the story are kept as notes and every chapter is checked against them. A human read the book as a reader, and also gave it these things:

- the reading aloud of XIX bis, the chapter added after Verne’s XIX (see below), which changed it in thirteen places;
- two later turns of its plot, the net snag and the false hope;
- in the same chapter, the fact its glass float turns on, that no fishery of the Pacific then buoyed its nets with glass;
- the idea of the additions described below, which we call vandalism;
- the invented scholar: how he came by the papers (he treats the novel as the colonists’ own record), that he notes only where those papers disagree with themselves, and the note on the title of XIX bis, which is the human’s sentence word for word.

Everything else here that is not Verne’s or White’s is the models’ work.

It was made in two stages.

**First, the book was restored.** Jules Verne’s *L’Île mystérieuse* (Hetzel, 1875) came into English in 1876 as *The Mysterious Island*, in a translation usually credited to Stephen W. White, though the edition names no translator. White cut as he went, and changed things. Here each of Verne’s sixty-two chapters was set against the French, paragraph by paragraph. Where White left words out, they are put back in his manner. Where he changed what Verne said, it is set right. Where his chapter stood too far from the French to mend, the chapter was written anew from the French in his English. Those chapters lean on the other English translation of the time, W. H. G. Kingston’s of 1875, which the models evidently remember: about three words in ten are in stretches of eight or more words identical with his, where White’s own text shares fewer than one in ten. White’s names, spellings and forms of address are kept, except where he changed Verne’s: the boat is Verne’s *Bonadventure*, not White’s *Good Luck*. Verne’s own slips in print are listed in the errata kept with the files (`texts/errata.md`), with how each was taken: most as printed, or as White took them. White’s glossary of 1876 follows the last chapter.

The map after the forewords shows what White did to Verne, and so what the restoration had to mend. It has one band for each chapter, as long as Verne’s French. The band darkens where White condensed or cut, and a mark stands for each place he changed, softened or added to what Verne said. A star marks a chapter written anew. The table after the map counts the restoration’s patches (each one correction to White’s text), about three thousand two hundred of them.

**Then we vandalized it.** That is our word for it; “we” is the models and the human together. We added a scholar, a member of an Iowa county historical society, who writes the second foreword and footnotes the places where the story disagrees with itself and explains them away where the papers let him, leaving the rest open. And we added a chapter, XIX bis, “The Float in the Bar”, which Verne never wrote. It is set between his chapters XIX and XX, in the first days of June 1865.

**Taking the vandalism out.** The book is kept as plain text files in a public repository, and anyone may copy it and change the copy. Every addition lives in its own files, so deleting them is all it takes, and the book builds without them:

- the scholar’s preface, `story/front/scholar-preface.md`;
- his notes, `story/scholar/`;
- the chapter XIX bis, `story/prose/01950-the-float-in-the-bar.md`.

What is left is Verne’s novel, unabridged, in White’s English, with this foreword and the map. Delete those too if you like.
