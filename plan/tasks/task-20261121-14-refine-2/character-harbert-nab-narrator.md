# Character check: Harbert, Nab and the narrator (R1121-138)

Rows C2, C7, C8, C10, C11, C12, C13, C15. All quotes were checked with grep -F against texts/fr-1875/. References are part.chapter ¶paragraph, with the file in brackets. The draft uses White's names (Herbert, Neb); the French and this report use Harbert and Nab.

## C2. Nab calls the two "madmen" for going out in the rain, and goes back to his pots

1. Like cases.
   - I.9 ¶89 [09.md]: « Eh ! Pencroff, dit Nab d’un ton un peu sarcastique, si c’est là tout le gibier que vous avez promis de rapporter à mon maître »
   - III.5 ¶65 [47.md]: « Allons, Pencroff, dit Nab, ne te fais pas si méchant que cela ! » (He chides Pencroff, in the tu of friends, I.19 ¶26.)
   - III.14 ¶46 [56.md]: « ce qui m’occupe, moi, c’est la besogne quotidienne ! » He laughs first, then turns to his work, as in I.20 ¶28 [20.md]: « Nab y fonctionnait gravement, comme s’il eût été dans un laboratoire de chimiste ».
2. Verdict: **plausible**, leaning in character for the second half. Nab does tease Pencroff and does go back to his work. Nothing in Verne shows him scolding Harbert, whom he spoils (III.11 ¶16 [53.md]: « Dieu sait quels bons petits plats, légers et savoureux, lui préparait Nab »). "Two madmen" lumps the boy in with the sailor. Nab's one rude word to the boy would be new; Verne's Nab is gentler (I.19 ¶26 [19.md]: « Mais rien n’est plus naturel. »), and his teasing is aimed at Pencroff.
3. If a change is wanted: let Nab call Pencroff the madman and say only a word of the boy ("and you drag the boy after you"), or have him say it to Pencroff alone. This rests on I.9 ¶89 and III.5 ¶65, where his gibes are for the sailor.

## C7. The narrator's two paragraphs on glass floats and the South Pacific's currents, closing "The sea is patient..."

1. Like cases.
   - I.16 ¶57 [16.md]: the narrator steps in to correct the boy's naming, « Ce n’était pas un lamantin », and goes on to explain the animal.
   - I.20 ¶30 [20.md]: the narrator reasons about the island's resources and says « il était probable que ».
   - I.2 ¶45 [02.md]: « On le voit, c’étaient cinq hommes déterminés »; also II.6 ¶46 [28.md]: « l’on sait que le jeune garçon se connaissait en zoologie ».
2. Verdict: **plausible**. Verne's narrator often stops to teach (glass, saltpetre, the dugong), speaks to the reader ("on le voit", "on sait"), and puts a fact before a character's guess. The aside on the glass float and the gyre is that habit. Two soft spots, neither fatal:
   - The closing maxim, "The sea is patient, and sooner or later it brings in everything that it does not sink", is more lyrical than the nearest Verne maxims, which are short and about a man ("Nab était Nab", I.19 ¶26). I found no sentence in Verne's narrator that personifies the sea's patience.
   - "had very likely never seen one" is a hedge. Where Verne's narrator hedges, as in I.20 ¶30, it is about the island, not about a character's knowledge.
3. Not strained enough to change. If trimmed, the plainest change is to end on the currents sentence (drop the maxim) and state the sailor's ignorance outright: "An American sailor had never seen one."

## C8. Herbert: "Latin, I think; but a word in Latin may be read a dozen ways, and here there are not letters enough to choose among them."

1. Like cases.
   - II.13 ¶12 [35.md]: « rien ne prouve que cette bouteille flotte depuis longtemps sur la mer ? »
   - II.13 ¶76 [35.md]: Harbert reads the document closely and remarks that it « ne parle que d’un seul naufragé ».
   - II.3 ¶42 [25.md] and the card: he gives a "petite leçon" with much spirit; and I.4 ¶16, the best professors in Boston.
2. Verdict: **in character**. Verne's boy is the one who holds back from a conclusion the evidence does not carry, and he reads a text sharply. The caution is his. "Latin, I think" fits a boy who studied under Boston professors. The length of the second clause is a little more pointed than his usual plain statement, but the card shows him lecturing with a will.
3. Nothing to change.

## C10. Herbert's foot goes through the old net under the sand; Pencroff digs, a sea breaks over them, he cuts the foot free; then takes the lad by the arm

1. Like cases (Harbert in danger, and the sea).
   - II.14 ¶50, ¶51 [36.md]: « Tu n’as pas de mal, Harbert ? » ... « Non ! non ! » He is thrown down by the savage and answers at once that he is whole.
   - I.3 ¶60 [03.md]: Harbert « nageait comme un poisson ». He is no helpless boy in the water.
   - II.13 ¶31 [35.md]: at the helm, Pencroff's trust « justifiée par le sang-froid et la raison du jeune garçon ».
2. Verdict: **in character**. A mishap that puts Harbert in danger and Pencroff over him is Verne's regular pattern (the savage, the jaguar, the ball at the corral). The boy falls without a cry in the draft, which suits the cool head Verne gives him. Nothing here makes him foolish or fearful.
3. Nothing to change. (Pencroff's half is not in my rows.)

## C11. Under the wall Herbert coughs up the sea water and laughs; Pencroff does not laugh

1. Like cases.
   - II.14 ¶51 [36.md]: « Non ! non ! » (he makes light of the hurt).
   - III.15 ¶17 [57.md]: « riant et rougissant à la fois » (he laughs under feeling).
   - II.7 ¶43 [29.md]: « disait plaisamment Harbert »  (he jokes about Jup and the masons' name for apprentices).
2. Verdict: **in character**. Harbert makes light of his own hurt and laughs readily; the sailor not laughing is Pencroff's side of Verne's pair (I.2 ¶24, his love for the boy).
3. Nothing to change.

## C12. The silence. Herbert says they must tell Mr. Smith; Pencroff asks what they would give but a hope; Herbert agrees. Try A adds Herbert's "Then no one need know that I took a ducking, either"

1. Like cases.
   - II.1 ¶80, ¶81 [23.md]: « Bon, fit Nab, qui voulait cacher sa mésaventure, nous n’en parlerons pas. » ... Harbert: « Au contraire, Nab, il faut en parler »
   - II.10 ¶30 [32.md]: « Ne disons rien à Pencroff, prenons le temps de préparer ces feuilles » (he proposes a secret himself, a gift for a friend).
   - III.18 ¶60 [60.md]: « il ne voulut pas contrarier Pencroff en s’absentant » (he yields to Pencroff). Also III.9 ¶3 [51.md]: he is the boy « qui comprenait bien les embarras dont il était la cause ».
2. Verdict, in two parts.
   - Herbert saying "we must tell Mr. Smith": **in character** (II.1 ¶81; I.11 ¶45 [11.md]: « Je vous obéirai en tout, monsieur Cyrus »; he asks Smith « mille questions », II.9 ¶44 [31.md]).
   - Herbert giving way to Pencroff's reason (a hope with nothing to show): **plausible**. He yields to the sailor in III.18 ¶60, and Verne's Harbert is the one who weighs evidence (II.13 ¶12). But in the one like case where a mishap is to be hidden (II.1), he is the boy who refuses to hide it, and the secret in II.10 is a surprise that ends in a gift, not a hope kept from the engineer.
   - Try A's line, hiding his own ducking: **plausible**. It fits his "Non ! non !" and his dislike of being a trouble (III.9 ¶3), but it is the only line in the set where he asks for silence about himself.
3. No change needed for the yielding. If the writer wants it nearer to Verne, give the agreement a term, as the tobacco has: "until we have something to show" (II.10 ¶30). This rests on the tobacco and on his answer to Nab.

## C13. At home they tell only Pencroff's joke and keep the float from Smith, Spilett and Nab; afterward they never bring it up

1. Like cases.
   - II.1 ¶81 [23.md]: « Au contraire, Nab, il faut en parler »
   - II.10 ¶30 [32.md]: the tobacco secret kept from Pencroff until a pipe can be offered.
   - III.14 ¶46 [56.md] and III.18 ¶18 [60.md] for Nab: he laughs, and sometimes jokes with a straight face.
2. Verdict, per man.
   - Nab laughing loudly at the joke: **in character**. He is « toujours souriant » (I.2 ¶14), he laughs in III.14 ¶45 ("Nab s’était mis à rire"), and a joke on the sailor is his sort. He knows nothing, so he cannot be false.
   - Herbert keeping the secret for a while: **plausible** (as C12).
   - Herbert "never bringing it up": **strained**. The boy who « faisait mille questions à l’ingénieur » (II.9 ¶44) and who is « aimant à s’instruire de toutes choses » (I.14 ¶6 [14.md]) has a puzzle with three letters on it and does not once ask Smith about glass, letters or seals. Verne's secrets, the tobacco, have an end the boy plans for. Keeping a secret without a term leans against his rule in II.1 ¶81.
3. Plainest change: have Herbert (not Pencroff) set a term, "until we have something to show", and let the later silence be the sailor's doing alone. It rests on II.10 ¶30 and II.1 ¶81.

## C15. Herbert, at the smooth sand: "Yes; and it lay under our hands the whole time the sea was out."

1. Like cases.
   - III.13 ¶59 [55.md]: after the loss of the boat, Harbert takes Pencroff's hand: « nous ferons un autre Bonadventure, un plus grand ! » (he comforts the sailor.)
   - II.1 ¶81 [23.md]: he owns the mishap and tells it.
   - I.9 ¶99 [09.md]: « Il ne faut pas se plaindre » (the boy who does not complain at a bad meal).
2. Verdict: **plausible**. The line is true and plain, and owning that the float lay under their hands is the honesty he shows in II.1. But it is a rueful, rather pointed remark, and in the like case (the loss of the Bonadventure) Verne's Harbert is the one who comforts the sailor, not the one who agrees with his regret and sharpens it. The gentleness of the draft's last paragraph (Pencroff's "Bah!") comes from the sailor, which suits Verne better than Harbert's dryness does.
3. If a change is wanted: let Herbert answer in the comforting key, as in III.13 ¶59, with something forward ("Then we shall know the place next time"), and keep the sharpness for Pencroff. It rests on III.13 ¶59.

## The beats as a whole (Harbert, Nab, narrator)

None makes any of the three a man Verne's reader would not know. The drift is small and runs one way, **more secretive** for Harbert: the boy who says "il faut en parler" (II.1 ¶81) is made to keep a find from the engineer, and to keep it for good (C12, C13). The second drift is **wittier and drier**: Harbert's rueful line (C15), and a narrator who ends on a maxim (C7). Nab is the least moved; his teasing and his return to the pots are his own, and only "two madmen" is wide of his target (C2).

Counts: in character 3 (C8, C10, C11); plausible 4 (C2, C7, C12, C15); strained 1 (C13); out of character 0.
