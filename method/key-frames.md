# Key frames and tweens: whose story it is

## The division of labor

In animation, the lead artist draws the key frames, the poses that carry the story, and assistants draw the frames between them, the tweens. The kit splits the work the same way.

- **The writer gives the key frames:** the scenes, beats, rules and pictures they see. These are the book.
- **Claude gives the tweens:** it places the frames on a timeline, proposes the passages between them, and sets the pacing. Every tween is marked *(Proposal.)* so it never blurs with the writer's frames.

A crowded stretch is raw material for a pacing pitch, not a problem to report.

## The writer's list is not an order

Frames often come unordered and scattered. A recurring beat (a slip, a drill, a visit) is scattered through an arc, not placed once. The order and spacing are Claude's pacing proposal, marked as such.

## Where there is no frame, ask

Chapters with several of the writer's frames come out well. Chapters with none, built only from tweens and a thin set of notes, come out flat. So:

- **Claude never fills a chapter that has no key frame by invention.** A writer with a long-developed story already has most answers; a gap in the notes is usually something they know and have not written down. So Claude reads back, asks, and marks its readings *(My reading, to confirm.)*. A recommendation is most likely to miss exactly where the notes are silent, which is why that is where to ask.
- Instead it writes a **brief**: where the chapter sits, what is settled and what is only proposed, and a few questions aimed at a character's goal in the scene and how far they push. These give the writer a handle; "what happens in this chapter?" does not.
- **The writer dictates** the scene as they see it, and the dictation is carded as new key frames, in the writer's words.

The brief's form and the loop are in [dictation](../workflows/dictation.md).

## Ask when the writer is present

When the writer is at the keyboard, collect the open questions and present them, a few at a time, each with:

- the context (where in the book, what it touches);
- the canon lines it rests on, quoted;
- Claude's recommendation, with the plain option first.

Do not launch agents to resolve by judgment what the writer could answer in a minute. Resolving by judgment is for when the writer asks for it or is away. When Claude does resolve, it marks the result overturnable and records the runner-up.

## Ask by structure, not by recall

Ask which cause makes two pieces fit, not what a detail was called ([the interview's rule](../interview/README.md#the-rules-of-the-interview)).

## The obvious solution first

When proposing how a child, or anyone, does something, or how an adult reads what they did, offer the obvious, ordinary explanation first. A child late home was dawdling. A capable adult caught out made an ordinary slip. Reach for a clever mechanism only when the obvious one breaks a settled rule.

## When settled lines dead-end

The writer dictates as a collaborator, so two settled lines can conflict, or one can lead nowhere. When the writer asks, Claude resolves these by story judgment: it prefers a reconciliation that keeps both lines true, and where one must give, it changes it and marks the change *(Resolved by judgment, overturnable.)* with the reason.
