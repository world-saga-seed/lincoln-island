# The Float in the Bar (draft 3)

CHAPTER XIX bis.

PENCROFF'S LINES—A ROUND THING ON THE BAR—HERBERT TAKEN INTO CONFIDENCE—A DAY OF RAIN—THE BALL OF GLASS—THE GOOSE BARNACLES—A FEW LETTERS—THE SQUALL—A FLASH ON THE ROCKS OF THE ISLET—THE SMOOTH SAND.

On the 1st of June, Pencroff was out at first light. The night had been one of squalls, and, though the wind was dropping, the sea still ran high in Union Bay and broke in long rolls upon the islet, under a sky that was grey and low. The sailor went down the ladder, crossed the strip of shore at the foot of the wall, and took his way along the beach toward the mouth of the Mercy, where, near the Chimneys, on the left bank of the river, he had set his lines.

These lines were his own affair; he had armed them with iron hooks from the forge, and he went every morning to visit them, as a captain goes round his ship.

He walked with his eyes on the sea, for a man who has passed his life on board reads a shore at the waterline, where the last tide has left its mark, and he looks first for what is changed. Much was changed that morning, for the squalls of the last days had worked upon this coast as the great storm of March had worked upon it. Here the sand had been cut away from the foot of a rock, there a bank showed where there had been none before. The tide was near its lowest, and the greater part of the sands which formed the bed of the channel were uncovered; between the islet and the coast there remained only the narrow gut through which the current ran.

Off the mouth of the Mercy, on the coast side of this gut, a bar of sand lay bare, a cable or two from the beach. Pencroff's eyes, which were two veritable telescopes, ran along it, and stopped.

Something lay on the bar, half buried, and it was round.

The sailor stood still. He knew stones, as every man knows them who has walked a beach at low water: the sea rounds them, it is true, but it does not make them so regular as that, nor leave them sitting up in the sand like the top of a buoy on a mud-bank. The thing might measure a foot across, and what it was, at that distance, was more than he could say; but it was the wrong shape for a stone, and that was enough to hold him there a full minute.

He would have gone out to it then and there; but the flood was already making. He could hear the current quicken in the gut, and he had crossed that channel on the first day, and knew what it was worth. A man alone on the bar, with the sea coming in on either hand, would have no one to give him a rope. Pencroff was bold, but he was a seaman, and he did not go out alone.

He went on to his lines, lifted them and set them again, and all the while he looked over his shoulder at the bar. Before he had come back to the foot of the ladder, the sea had covered it.

At breakfast he said nothing; but later in the morning, finding Herbert alone on the beach, he went up to him.

"My boy," said he, "will you lend me a hand with my lines to-morrow, at the morning ebb? I mean to lift them and set them afresh."

"Willingly, Pencroff."

"Good." The sailor lowered his voice, though there was no one within a quarter of a mile but the gulls. "And there is something else. On the bar yonder, off the mouth of the river, I saw a thing this morning that has no business to be there."

"What thing?"

"A round thing, half in the sand. It may be a stone, after all."

"But you do not think so."

"I do not think so. If it is what I think, it was made by men's hands; and if it was made by men's hands—"

"Then a ship—!" cried Herbert.

"Gently, gently. It may have come a long way. That is why I say nothing to the others. I'll not have Mr. Smith and Mr. Spilett and Neb all hoping, and tumbling down the ladder, for a thing that may turn out to be a stone with a coat of weed on it. We will go and see, the two of us. If it is worth telling, I'll tell it, and to-morrow night at latest."

Herbert promised; and no one would wonder to see the lad go down with the sailor to the lines, for it was the errand of every day.

The rest of that day passed at the work of Granite House. The evening ebb fell after dark, when nothing could be done on the sands, and the sailor, who was not a patient man, had to wait the night through for the low water of the next morning.

The 2nd of June dawned in rain. A cold rain, driven by a wind which had gone round to the east-north-east and blew straight into the bay, beat upon the facade, and from first light the thick shutters were kept closed. Granite House was lit only by the fire of the kitchen, and the chimney smoked. The engineer, the reporter and Neb were at work within, upon the furnishing of the rooms, and Top lay at his master's feet. No one looked out at the bar.

There were picks and shovels in the storeroom, and Pencroff thought of them. But a man does not carry a pick down a ladder to lift his lines, and the engineer would have asked what it was for. The tools stayed behind, and the sailor had his knife, one of the two blades made from Top's collar; and that was all.

Toward eight o'clock the ebb began to near its lowest. Pencroff turned up the collar of his jacket, and said aloud that the fish did not mind the rain, if he did, and went out by the door, Herbert after him. Neb called to them that they were two madmen, and went back to his pots.

At the foot of the wall the two companions took off their shoes, which were much worn, set them under the rock, and rolled their trousers to the knee. The sand was hard under their feet, and the water in the pools was cold enough to numb them. They went first to the lines at the mouth of the Mercy, and Pencroff hauled them in, one after the other; the hooks came up with their bait untouched.

"Empty," said he. "The fish have more sense than we have, my boy, and keep indoors."

The tide was out, and from the river's mouth the bared sands of the channel stretched toward the islet, ribbed by the ebb and shining with water. And on the coast side of the gut, where the current still ran fast, the bar rose a little above them. There was no need to cross the gut; the two walked out over the wet sand, with the rain in their faces, and in a few minutes they stood beside the thing.

A tangle of wrack, torn loose by the squalls, lay over it, and Pencroff dragged it away. It was a ball of glass, almost hidden under the shells.

Pencroff knelt down beside it in the sand. About a third of it stood above the bar, and the rest was buried, so that it showed above the sand as the back of a turtle shows above the sea. Over all its upper part lay a crust of grey-white shells, dead and empty, packed one against another, thickest on the top, where it had faced the sky. Here and there a shell had broken away, and there the glass showed through, dark green, like the glass of a bottle. From under the crust, on the side toward the gut, a dark cord came out of the sand, rotten and swollen with water, with the meshes of a net choked with sand along it; and it ran down again into the bar.

"A net," said Pencroff.

They tried to turn the ball, but it was heavy with the sand that held it, and stuck as fast as a rock. The sailor felt round it with his fingers as far as he could reach. A cord of the same kind as the other passed under it, on the hidden side, and went down into the sand. Pencroff worked the blade of his knife down beside the glass, until he felt the cord with its point, and sawed at it blindly a long while; but the sand closed upon the blade at every stroke, and when at last he drew it out, he could not tell whether he had cut the cord half through or not cut it at all. To free the ball they must dig; and they had nothing to dig with but their hands, and the sand, as fast as they scooped it out, filled again with water.

Pencroff sat back on his heels and looked at the thing.

"It's a float," said he at last. "A float for a net. But it's no cork, and it was never made in America."

He rapped the glass with his knuckle, where the shells had broken away.

"Glass! I have put in at many good ports, my boy, and I have never seen the like."

The sailor might well say so.

Only in late years had the fishermen of the north of Europe, in the cod fisheries of Norway, begun to buoy their great nets with globes of blown glass, which do not drink the water as cork does, and neither rot nor sink. They were still a novelty of the northern seas, and an American sailor had very likely never seen one.

That such a float should lie upon Lincoln Island would astonish no one who knows the roads which the sea opens to floating bodies. In the South Pacific the waters turn in a great round: up the coasts of Chile and Peru, westward below the line, then south, and back to the east under the westerly winds; and whatever falls into that round, a spar, a cask, a float, it carries for months and for years. The sea is patient, and sooner or later it brings in everything that it does not sink.

Herbert picked up a stone from the bar and began to knock away the crust, a little at a time, holding his hand very lightly, for fear of cracking the glass. Pencroff, on his knees, held the ball steady with both hands, and kept one eye upon the water.

And while the lad worked, the sailor made his reckoning. A foot of good glass! At that moment Granite House sat behind its shutters, in the light of the fire, because the engineer had not yet had the time to make glass for its windows. Here was glass already made. What Mr. Smith could do with it Pencroff could only guess; but he had seen the lens he made of two watch glasses, and he was sure that he could make a window of a ball. And failing a window, it would be the finest float for his lines that a fisherman ever had; but he kept all this to himself.

The shells came away more easily than Herbert had feared, and he looked at them closely as they fell.

"Barnacles," said he. "And look here, among them: these are goose barnacles."

"Barnacles, I know well enough," answered Pencroff. "I have scraped them off many a ship's bottom."

"Yes, but these few are of another kind, that hang by a stalk. They fasten upon anything that floats—driftwood, a buoy, a ship—and they live only near the surface, where the sea brings them their food. All of them are dead, and long dead; of these there is nothing left but the shells, and a stump of the stalk, dried hard among the others."

"And what does that tell you?"

"That this ball floated a long while before ever the sand took it. It has been in the sea a long time, Pencroff; and it must have come from far off."

The sailor looked out over the bay, where the rain hid the horizon.

"From far off," he repeated. "So there is no ship to look for in the offing."

"Not near, perhaps."

"Far off is a long word, my boy," said Pencroff, after a moment. "Ships go a long way."

Herbert went on with his stone. He was working now lower down, close to the sand, on the side toward the gut, and there, under the crust, his fingers felt a ridge in the glass. He cleared it with care; it was a raised edge, curving, the rim of a round seal about a palm across, which had been moulded in the glass itself. But the greater part of the seal lay below, toward the sand, and only a piece of it could be seen. Along the inside of this rim there ran letters, capitals, standing out from the glass.

"Letters!" cried Pencroff.

Herbert scraped again. The first was a B. After it came a place where the crust held fast and would not come away under the stone. Then an L; then an I. Beyond the I, the ring went down into the sand.

"Can you read them, my boy?"

Herbert looked at them a long while, his stone in his hand.

"Latin, I think," said he; "but a word in Latin may be read a dozen ways, and here there are not letters enough to choose among them, and Mr. Smith would know better than I."

He said no more. The two bent over these three letters, the lad trying the crust again between the B and the L, the sailor with his eyes upon them, and for a while even Pencroff forgot to watch the water. The hour of low water went by in this way.

A sound made them look up.

The squall was on them. It came out of the east, across the bay, a grey wall of rain and wind. The sea rose with it. The flood had turned while they knelt there, and now the flood and the squall came in together. A wave ran up the bar and broke over Pencroff's knees.

"Back!" cried the sailor.

They could not stay. Already the sea was running over the sands they had crossed dry an hour before. The water was at their knees, then above them. They waded, ran, waded again. But the ebb had scoured the sands into hollows that ran down toward the gut, and in one of these the bottom went from under Herbert's feet. The current took him, turned him over, and drew him seaward. Pencroff threw himself after him, caught him by the collar, lost him, caught him again by the arm, and, planting himself against the stream, hauled him up on firmer sand. Then he took the lad under the shoulder, and carried rather than led him. The wind tore at them. Behind them the waves broke upon the bar, and when Pencroff turned his head he could still see the float, dark in the white of the surf.

They reached the beach. They went up it to the granite wall and stood close under it, near the foot of the ladder, out of the worst of the wind. Under the wall Herbert coughed up the sea water he had swallowed, and laughed, and said that it was nothing. Pencroff did not laugh. From there they could still see the bar, or the place where the bar had been, and the float upon it, now hidden, now showing between two seas.

The squall did not slacken. The sea came in heavier with every minute, and the rain drove along the face of the wall. The sand they had dug from about the ball no longer held it, and it rocked under each sea; and the cord upon which the sailor's knife had worked, if it was not cut through, had been weakened. At last it gave way; the backwash drew the freed ball off the bar into the gut, and the current of the channel carried it toward the point of the islet.

Then, for a moment, the clouds broke under the squall. A pale light fell across the channel. Out on the rocks of the islet, in the spray, something flashed, once, and flew apart.

Then nothing.

Pencroff looked at the bar. The float was gone from it. He looked again at the islet, and searched its rocks from one end to the other, and he saw only the spray. Neither of them spoke. They both knew that glass thrown upon those rocks by such a sea was in pieces. Where the bar had been there was only broken water, and the flood ran over it.

Toward one o'clock the squall eased; the flood had long since covered the bar. The two companions went back for their shoes, and climbed the ladder, which streamed with water, and came into Granite House wet to the skin.

"Well, Pencroff," said the reporter, "what has the sea given you this morning?"

"The lines took nothing, and the sea took the pair of us."

Neb laughed loudly, and set about making up the fire, and the engineer smiled. The two wet men stood before the hearth and dried their clothes upon their backs, as sailors do. That was all they told, and it was true, as far as it went.

Pencroff might have told the rest then, by the fire. He did not. He meant to tell it, he had promised it; but the rest could not be told by halves. To speak of the float was to speak of the bar, of the hour of low water let slip, of the flood which he, a seaman, had not watched, and of the lad turned over in the current like a piece of weed. He had let the boy stay out on the bar with the flood making, against a rule he had kept all his life.

The 3rd of June was grey and still, for in the night the wind had gone round to the south-west, and the sea was down. Toward the middle of the morning, at low water, the two went down once more to the lines, and from the lines out over the sands. This time Pencroff kept the lad on his landward side, and did not take his eyes off the gut.

The bar had moved.

Where the ball had lain the day before, the sand ran smooth and level to the gut, and of the net not a thread showed.

Pencroff stood a long time and looked at it.

"Five of us and the picks, and we'd have had it," said he.

Then he shook himself, as Top does coming out of the water. "Bah!" he cried. "The sea has taken, and the sea will give us something else. She owes us that much, my boy." And he put his great hand on the lad's shoulder, and they went back across the sand to the lines.
