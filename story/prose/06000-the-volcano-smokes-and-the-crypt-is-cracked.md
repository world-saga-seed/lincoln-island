# The Volcano Smokes and the Crypt Is Cracked (final)

CHAPTER LX.

THE REFLECTIONS OF EACH ONE—RENEWAL OF WORK—THE 1ST OF JANUARY, 1869—A PLUME AT THE SUMMIT OF THE VOLCANO—FIRST SYMPTOMS OF AN ERUPTION—AYRTON AND SMITH AT THE CORRAL—EXPLORATION OF THE CRYPT DAKKAR—WHAT CAPTAIN NEMO HAD SAID TO THE ENGINEER.

In the early morning the colonists had silently regained the entrance of the cavern, which they called Crypt Dakkar, in remembrance of Captain Nemo. The tide was low, and they easily passed under the archway, whose basaltic pier was beaten by the waves.

The iron boat remained in this place, and so placed as to be sheltered from the waves; but as additional precaution Pencroff, Neb, and Ayrton drew it up on a little beach on one side of the crypt, in a spot where it ran no danger.

The storm had ceased with the night. The last mutterings of the thunder were dying away in the west. It was not raining, although the sky was still clouded. In short, this month of October, the beginning of the southern spring, did not come in good fashion, and the wind had a tendency to shift from one point of the compass to another, so that it was impossible to say what the weather would be.

Smith and his companions, on leaving Crypt Dakkar, went towards the corral. On the way Neb and Herbert took care to take up the wire which had been stretched by Captain Nemo between the corral and the crypt, as it might be useful in the future.

While walking the colonists spoke but little. The various incidents of this night of the 15th to the 16th of October had made a very vivid impression upon them. This unknown, whose influence had protected them so well, this man whom they imagined a genii, Captain Nemo, was no more. His Nautilus and himself were buried in the depths of the abyss. It seemed to each one of them that they were more isolated than before. They were, so to speak, accustomed to count upon this powerful intervention which to-day was wanting, and Spilett, and even Smith, did not escape this feeling. So they all kept a profound silence as they followed the road to the corral.

About 9 o’clock in the morning the colonists were in Granite House again.

It had been agreed that the construction of the ship should be pushed forward as rapidly as possible, and Smith gave the work more of his time and care than ever before. They did not know what the future might bring forth, and it would be a guarantee of safety for them to have a strong vessel, able to stand rough weather, and large enough to carry them, if need be, a voyage of some length. If, when it was finished, the colonists did not yet decide to leave Lincoln Island and make for either a Polynesian archipelago of the Pacific or the coasts of New Zealand, they must at least make the voyage to Tabor Island as soon as possible and leave there the notice relating to Ayrton. This was an indispensable precaution in case the Scotch yacht returned to these seas, and it must on no account be neglected.

The work was therefore resumed. Smith, Pencroff, and Ayrton, aided by Neb, Spilett, and Herbert whenever some other pressing work did not call them away, worked at it without ceasing. It was important to have the new ship finished in five months, that is to say, by the beginning of March, if they wished to make the voyage to Tabor Island before the equinoxial storms would render it impracticable. So the carpenters did not lose a moment. Besides, they need not trouble themselves about making rigging, since that of the Speedy had been saved entire. It was, therefore, above all, the hull of the ship which must be finished.

The end of the year 1868 passed in the midst of this important work, almost to the exclusion of all other. At the end of two months and a half the ribs had been put in place and the planking began, so that they were already able to see that Smith’s plans were excellent, and that the ship would behave well at sea. Pencroff worked with ardor, and always grumbled when any of the others left off work to go hunting. It was, nevertheless, necessary to lay in a stock of provisions for the approaching winter. But that made no difference. The honest sailor was unhappy unless every one was at work in the ship-yard. At these times he grumbled and did—he was so put out—the work of half a dozen men.

All this summer season was bad. For some days together the heat was overpowering, and the atmosphere, charged with electricity, then discharged itself only in violent storms, which deeply disturbed the strata of the air. It was seldom that the distant muttering of the thunder was unheard. It was like a dull, but permanent murmur, such as is produced in the equatorial regions of the globe.

On the 1st of January, 1869, a terrific storm burst over the island, and the lightning struck the island several times. Tall trees were shattered, and among them was one of the enormous micocouliers which shaded the poultry-yard at the southern extremity of the lake. Had this meteoric storm any relation to the phenomena which were occurring in the bowels of the earth? Was there a sort of connection between the disturbances in the air and those in the interior of the globe? Smith was inclined to believe so, since the development of these storms was marked by a recrudescence of the volcanic symptoms.

On the 3d of January, Herbert, who had gone at daybreak to Prospect Plateau to saddle one of the onaggas, saw an enormous plume rolling out from the summit of the volcano.

Herbert hastened to inform the others, who came at once to look at the summit of Mount Franklin.

“Ah!” said Pencroff, “it is not vapor this time! It seems to me that the giant is not content to breathe, he must smoke!”

The image employed by the sailor expressed with exactness the change which had taken place at the mouth of the volcano. For three months the crater had been emitting vapors more or less intense, but which as yet came only from an interior ebullition of mineral matters. This time, instead of vapors, a thick smoke rose in the form of a greyish column, more than three hundred feet wide at its base, which spread out like an immense mushroom at a height of from seven to eight hundred feet above the summit of the mountain.

“The chimney is on fire!” said Spilett.

“And we cannot put it out!” answered Herbert.

“It would be well to sweep the volcanoes,” said Neb, who seemed to speak in perfect earnest.

“All right, Neb,” cried Pencroff. “Will you undertake the job?”

And Pencroff burst into a loud laugh.

Smith looked attentively at the thick smoke thrown out by Mount Franklin, and at the same time he listened as if he expected to detect some distant rumbling. Then, turning towards his companions, who were at some little distance, he said:—

“In truth, my friends, it cannot be denied that an important change has taken place. The volcanic matters are not only in a state of ebullition, they have taken fire, and, without doubt, we are threatened with an approaching eruption!”

“Very well, sir; we will witness this eruption,” cried Pencroff, “and we will applaud it if it is a success! I don’t think that anything over there need worry us!”

“No, Pencroff,” answered Smith, “for the old course of the lava is still open, and, thanks to its position, the crater has heretofore discharged towards the north. Nevertheless—”

“Nevertheless, since there is nothing to be gained by an eruption, it would be better not to have it,” said the reporter.

“Who knows?” replied the sailor. “There may be some useful and precious matter in the volcano, which it will be good enough to throw up, which will be advantageous for us!”

Smith shook his head, as a man who anticipated nothing good from this phenomenon, whose development had been so sudden. He did not think so lightly of the consequences of an eruption. If the lava, on account of the position of the crater, did not directly menace the wooded and cultivated portions of the island, other complications might arise. Eruptions are often accompanied by earthquakes, and an island formed, like Lincoln Island, of such different materials: basalt on one side, granite on another, lavas to the north, a loose soil to the south, material which, therefore, could not be solidly bound together, ran the risk of being torn asunder. If, therefore, the outpouring of volcanic substances did not constitute a very serious danger, any movement in the framework of the earth which shook the island might be followed by the gravest consequences.

“It seems to me,” said Ayrton, who had lain down, with his ear to the ground, “it seems to me that I hear dull rumblings, like the rattling of a wagon, loaded with iron bars.”

The colonists listened with extreme attention, and were convinced that Ayrton was not mistaken. With the rumbling mingled at times a subterranean roaring, making a sort of “rinfordzando,” which died away slowly, as if some violent breeze had passed through the depths of the globe. But no detonation, properly so called, was yet heard, and it was fair to conclude that the smoke and vapor found a free passage through the central chimney, and that, the escape-pipe being sufficiently large, no dislocation would be produced, and no explosion need be feared.

“Come,” said Pencroff at length, “shall we not go back to work? Let Mount Franklin smoke, brawl, moan, and vomit fire and flames as much as it chooses, but that is no excuse for us to quit work! Come, Ayrton, Neb, Herbert, Mr. Smith, Mr. Spilett, we want all hands to-day! We are going to fit the wales, and a dozen arms will not be too many. I want our new Bonadventure—for we will keep the name, will we not?—to be afloat in Balloon Harbor before two months are passed! So there is not an hour to be lost!”

All the colonists, whose arms were demanded by Pencroff, went down to the shipyard and set about placing the wales, those thick planks which form the belt of a ship and bind the ribs of her frame solidly together. It was heavy and toilsome work, in which all had to take part.

They worked steadily, therefore, all that day, the 3d of January, without troubling themselves about the volcano, which could not be seen from the beach before Granite House. But once or twice heavy shadows obscured the sunlight, and, as the day was perfectly clear, it was evident that thick clouds of smoke were passing between the sun’s disc and the island. The wind, blowing from the sea, carried all these vapors to the west. Smith and Spilett noticed these passing darkenings, and talked more than once of the progress that the volcanic phenomenon was evidently making, but they did not cease work. It was, moreover, of great importance, in every sense, that the ship should be finished with as little delay as possible. In the presence of events which might happen, the security of the colonists would be better assured. Who could say but that this ship might not, some day, be their sole refuge?

That evening, after supper, Smith, Spilett, and Herbert climbed to the plateau. It was already dark, and they would be able to distinguish whether flames or incandescent matter projected by the volcano, was mingled with the smoke and vapor accumulated at the mouth of the crater.

“The crater is on fire!” cried Herbert, who, more active than his companions, had reached the plateau the first.

Mount Franklin, about six miles distant, appeared like a gigantic torch, with fuliginous flames twisting about its summit. So much smoke, such quantities of scoriæ and cinders, perhaps, were mingled with the flames, that their light, much dimmed, did not glare upon the shades of night. But a sort of dull yellow glow spread over the island, making dimly visible the masses of forest in the foreground. Enormous clouds obscured the heavens, between which glittered a few stars.

“The progress is rapid,” said the engineer.

“It is not astonishing,” answered the reporter. “The volcano has been awake for some time already. You remember, Cyrus, that the first vapors appeared about the time we were searching the spurs of the mountain for the retreat of Captain Nemo. That was, if I am not mistaken, about the 15th of October?”

“Yes!” replied Herbert, “two months and a half ago.”

“The subterranean fires have been brooding for ten weeks,” continued Spilett, “and it is not astonishing that they develop now with this violence.”

“Do not you feel certain vibrations in the ground?” asked Smith.

“Indeed,” replied Spilett, “but an earthquake—”

“I do not say that we are menaced by an earthquake,” said Smith, “and God preserve us from one! No. These vibrations are due to the effervescence of the central fire. The crust of the earth is nothing more than the covering of a boiler, and you know how the covering of a boiler, under the pressure of the gases, vibrates like a sonorous plate. That is what is happening at this moment.”

“What magnificent sheaves of fire!” cried Herbert, as a sort of bouquet of fireworks, whose brilliance the vapors had not been able to dim, shot up from the crater. From its midst thousands of luminous fragments and bright scintillations were thrown in contrary directions. Some of them pierced the dome of smoke in a rapid jet, leaving behind them a perfect cloud of incandescent dust. This outpouring was accompanied by successive detonations like the discharge of a battery of mitrailleuses.

Smith, the reporter, and the lad, after having passed an hour on Prospect Plateau, went down again to the beach and returned to Granite House. The engineer was pensive, and so much preoccupied that Spilett asked him if he anticipated any near danger, of which the eruption would be the direct or indirect cause.

“Yes and no,” responded Smith.

“But,” said the reporter, “would not the worst that could happen to us be an earthquake, which would overthrow the island? And I don’t think that is to be feared, since the vapors and lava have a free passage of escape.”

“I do not fear an earthquake,” answered Smith, “of the ordinary kind, such as are brought about by the expansion of subterranean vapors. But other causes may bring about great disaster.”

“What are they, my dear Cyrus?”

“I do not know exactly—I must see—I must visit the mountain. In a few days I shall have made up my mind.”

Spilett asked no further questions, and soon, notwithstanding the detonations of the volcano, which increased in violence and were repeated by the echoes of the island, the inhabitants of Granite House slept soundly.

Three days passed, the 4th, 5th, and 6th of January, during which they worked on the ship, and, without explaining himself further, the engineer hastened the work as much as possible. Mount Franklin was covered with a sinister cloud, and with the flames vomited forth incandescent rocks, some of which fell back into the crater. This made Pencroff, who would only look upon the phenomenon from its amusing side, say—

“Look! The giant plays at cup and ball! He is a juggler.”

And, indeed, the matters vomited forth fell back into the abyss, and it seemed as if the lavas, swollen by the interior pressure, had not yet risen to the mouth of the crater. At least, the fracture on the northeast, which was partly visible, did not pour forth any torrent on the northern slope of the mountain.

Meanwhile, however pressing the ship-building, other cares required the attention of the colonists in different parts of the island. First of all, they must go to the corral, where the moufflons and goats were enclosed, and renew the store of fodder for these animals. It was, therefore, agreed that Ayrton should go there the next day, the 7th of January, and, as he was able to do this work alone, being accustomed to it, Pencroff and the others showed some surprise to hear the engineer say to Ayrton:——

“As you are going to the corral to-morrow, I will go with you.”

“Oh! Mr. Smith!” cried the sailor, “our time is very limited, and, if you go off too, that will be four arms the fewer!”

“We will return the next day,” answered Smith, “but I must go to the corral—I wish to see about this eruption.”

“Eruption! Eruption!” answered Pencroff, with a dissatisfied air. “What is there important about this eruption? It don’t bother me!”

Notwithstanding the sailor’s protest, the exploration was decided upon for the next day. Herbert wanted to go with Smith, but he did not wish to annoy Pencroff by absenting himself.

The next day, at daybreak, Smith and Ayrton, mounted on the wagon drawn by the two onaggas, took the road to the corral, and went there at a brisk trot.

Over the forest hung huge clouds constantly supplied from Mount Franklin with fuliginous matter. These clouds, which rolled heavily through the air, were evidently composed of heterogeneous substances. It was not altogether the smoke from the volcano that made them so strangely heavy and opaque. Scoriæ in a state of powder, pulverized puzzolan and grey cinder as fine as the finest fecula, were held in suspension in their thick folds. These cinders are so fine that they have sometimes been seen to remain in the air for months at a time. After the eruption of 1783, in Iceland, for more than a year the atmosphere was so charged with volcanic powder that the sun’s rays were scarcely visible.

Usually, however, these pulverized matters fall to the earth, and it was so in this instance. Smith and Ayrton had hardly reached the corral, when a sort of blackish snow, like fine gunpowder, fell, and instantly modified the whole aspect of the ground. Trees, fields, everything was covered with a coating several inches deep. But, most fortunately, the wind was from the northeast, and the greater part of the cloud was carried off to sea.

“That is very curious, Mr. Smith,” said Ayrton.

“It is very serious,” answered Smith. “This puzzolan, this pulverized pumice stone, all this mineral dust in short, shows how deep-seated is the commotion in the lower strata of the volcano.”

“But is there nothing to be done?”

“Nothing, but to observe the progress of the phenomenon. Employ yourself, Ayrton, at the corral, and meanwhile I will go up beyond the sources of Red Creek and examine the state of the mountain on its northern slope. Then——”

“Then, sir?”

“Then we will make a visit to Crypt Dakkar—I wish to see—Well, I will come back for you in a couple of hours.”

Ayrton went into the corral, and while waiting for the return of the engineer occupied himself with the moufflons and goats, which showed a certain uneasiness before these first symptoms of an eruption.

Meantime Smith had ventured along the crest of the eastern spurs, had gone round Red Creek, and arrived at the place where his companions and he had discovered the sulphur spring on their first exploration.

How everything was changed! Instead of a single column of smoke, he counted thirteen escaping from the ground as if violently thrust upward by a piston. It was evident that the crust of earth was subjected in this place to a frightful pressure. The atmosphere was saturated with sulphurous gas, hydrogen, and carbonic acid, mingled with aqueous vapors. Smith felt the volcanic tufa with which the plain was strewn, the pulverulent cinders hardened by time, trembling beneath him, but he did not yet see any traces of fresh lava.

This the engineer was able to verify more completely when he observed the whole northern slope of Mount Franklin. Whirlwinds of smoke and flames escaped from the crater; a hail of scoriæ fell upon the soil; but no lava flowed from the gullet of the crater, which proved that the level of the volcanic matter had not yet attained the upper orifice of the central chimney.

“And I would be better satisfied if they had!” said Smith to himself. “At least I would be certain that the lavas had taken their accustomed route. Who knows if they may not burst forth from some new mouth? But that is not the danger! Captain Nemo has well foreseen it! No! the danger is not there!”

Smith went forward as far as the enormous causeway, whose prolongation enframed the narrow Shark Gulf. Here, then, he was able to examine the ancient lava marks well enough. There could be no doubt that the last eruption had been at a far distant epoch.

Then he returned, listening to the subterranean rumblings, which sounded like continuous thunder, broken by loud detonations, and at 9 o’clock in the morning he was at the corral.

Ayrton was waiting for him.

“The animals are attended to, sir,” said he.

“All right, Ayrton.”

“They seem to be restless, Mr. Smith.”

“Yes, instinct speaks in them, and instinct does not mislead.”

“When you are ready—”

“Take a lantern and tinder, Ayrton,” answered the engineer, “and let us go.”

Ayrton did as he was told. The onaggas, unharnessed, were wandering about the corral. The gate was closed from without, and Smith, leading, took the narrow path westward to the coast.

They walked over a soil covered with the pulverulent matter which had fallen from the clouds. No quadruped appeared in the woods. Even the birds had flown away. Sometimes a passing breeze raised the coating of cinders, and the two colonists, caught in an opaque whirlwind, could no longer see each other. They had to place handkerchiefs over their eyes and mouths or they would have been blinded and suffocated.

Under these circumstances they could not march rapidly. The air was heavy, as if its oxygen had been partly burned out of it, making it unfit to breathe. Every hundred paces they had to stop and take breath, and it was after 10 o’clock when the engineer and his companion reached the summit of the enormous heap of basaltic and porphyrytic rocks which formed the northwest coast of the island.

They began to go down this abrupt descent, following very nearly the detestable road, which, during that stormy night had led them to Crypt Dakkar. By daylight this descent was less perilous, and, moreover, the covering of cinders gave a firmer foothold to the slippery rocks.

The projection which bordered the shore, at a height of about forty feet, was soon attained. Smith remembered that this projection sloped gently down to the level of the sea. Though the tide was low at this moment, no beach was uncovered, and the waves, fouled by the volcanic dust, beat directly upon the basalt of the shore.

Smith and Ayrton found the opening to Crypt Dakkar without any difficulty, and stopped under the last rock, which formed the lowest landing of the projection.

“The iron boat should be there?” asked the engineer.

“Yes, sir,” answered Ayrton, drawing towards him the light boat, which was sheltered under the vault of the archway.

“Let us get in, then, Ayrton,” said the engineer.

The two embarked in the boat. A slight undulation of the waves carried it farther in under the very low arch of the crypt, and there Ayrton, having struck a light, lit the lantern. Then he took the two oars, and, the lantern having been placed in the bow of the boat so as to throw its rays forward, Smith took the tiller and steered into the darkness of the crypt.

The Nautilus was no longer here to illuminate this sombre cavern. Perhaps the electric irradiation, ever fed by its powerful source, still shone under the waters, but no light came from the abyss where Captain Nemo reposed.

The light of the lantern was barely sufficient to permit the engineer to advance, following the right hand wall of the crypt. A sepulchral silence reigned under this vault, at least in its forward portion, but soon Smith heard distinctly the mutterings which came from the bowels of the mountain.

“It is the volcano,” he said.

Soon, with this noise, the chemical combinations betrayed themselves by a strong odor, and sulphurous vapors choked the engineer and his companion.

“It is as Captain Nemo feared,” murmured Smith, growing slightly pale. “Yet we must go on to the end.”

“Come!” answered Ayrton, who bent to his oars and pushed the boat towards the head of the crypt.

Twenty-five minutes after having left the opening the two reached the terminal wall and stopped.

Smith, standing on the seat, moved the lantern about over this wall, which separated the crypt from the central chimney of the volcano. How thick was it? Whether 100 feet or but 10 could not be determined. But the subterranean noises were too plainly heard for it to be very thick.

The engineer, after having explored the wall along a horizontal line, fixed the lantern to the end of an oar and went over the basaltic wall again at a greater height.

There, through scarcely visible cracks between the ill-joined prisms, came a pungent smoke, which infected the air of the cavern. The wall was striped with these fractures, and some of the larger ones came to within only two or three feet of the waters of the crypt.

At first, Smith rested thoughtful. Then he murmured these words again:—

“Yes! Captain Nemo was right! There is the danger, and it is terrible!”

Ayrton said nothing, but, on a sign from the engineer, he took up the oars, and, a half hour later, he and Smith came out of Crypt Dakkar.
