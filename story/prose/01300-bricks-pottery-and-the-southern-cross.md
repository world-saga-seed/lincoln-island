# Bricks, Pottery and the Southern Cross (final)

CHAPTER XIII.

TOP’S CONTRIBUTION—MAKING BOWS AND ARROWS—A BRICK-KILN—THE POTTERY KILN—DIFFERENT COOKING UTENSILS—THE FIRST BOILED MEAT—MUGWORT—THE SOUTHERN CROSS—AN IMPORTANT ASTRONOMICAL OBSERVATION.

“Now then, Mr. Smith, where shall we begin?” asked Pencroff of the engineer the next morning.

“At the beginning,” answered the engineer.

And indeed it was by the “beginning” that these colonists were forced to start, as they did not even possess implements with which to make implements. Neither were they in that condition of nature which, “having time, economizes effort.” Time failed them, since the necessities of life must be provided for at once, and, if profiting by experience they had nothing to invent, at least they had everything to make. Their iron and steel was in the ore, their pottery was in the clay, their linen and clothes were still in the raw fibre.

It must be remembered, however, that these colonists were “men,” in the fine and powerful sense of the word. The engineer Smith could not have been aided by comrades more intelligent, or more devoted and zealous. He had questioned them, and knew their ability.

The reporter, a man of great talent, having learned everything so as to be able to speak of everything, would contribute largely from his knowledge and skill towards the settlement of the island. He would shirk no task; and, an ardent sportsman, he would follow as a business what he had formerly indulged in as a pastime. Herbert, a manly lad, already remarkably well versed in natural science, would contribute no small share to the common cause. Neb was devotion personified. Adroit, intelligent, indefatigable, robust, of iron constitution, knowing something of the work in a smithy, his assistance would be considerable. As to Pencroff, he had sailed every sea, had been a carpenter in the Brooklyn yards, an assistant tailor on board the ships of the State, and, during his leaves of absence, a gardener, farmer, etc.; in short, like every sailor, he was a Jack-of-all-trades.

Indeed, it would have been hard to bring together five men, more able to struggle against fate, and more certain to triumph in the end.

“At the beginning,” Smith had said. And this beginning was the construction of an apparatus which would serve to transform the natural substances. Every one knows what an important part heat plays in these transformations. Therefore, as the fuel, wood or coal, could be used at once, it was only necessary to make an oven to utilize them.

“What good is an oven?” asked Pencroff.

“To make the pottery that we want,” replied Smith.

“And how will we make an oven?”

“With bricks.”

“And how will we make the bricks?”

“With the clay. Come, friends. We will set up our factory at the place of production, so as to avoid carriage. Neb will bring the provisions, and we shall not lack fire to cook food.”

“No,” replied the reporter, “but suppose we lack food, since we have no hunting implements?”

“If we only had a knife!” cried the sailor.

“What, then?” asked Smith.

“Why, I would soon make a bow and arrows. And game would be plenty in the larder.”

“Yes, a knife. Something that will cut,” said the engineer, as if talking to himself.

At this moment his eyes turned towards Top, who was going to and fro upon the shore.

Suddenly his face brightened:

“Come here, Top,” he called.

The dog bounded to his master at the call, and Smith, having taken Top’s head between his hands and taken off the collar which the animal had around his neck, broke it into halves, saying:—

“Here are two knives, Pencroff.”

For all response, the sailor gave a couple of cheers. Top’s collar was made from a thin piece of tempered steel. All that was therefore necessary was to rub it to an edge upon a sand-stone, and then to take off the wire-edge upon one of finer grain. These kind of stones were readily procurable upon the beach, and in a couple of hours the implements of the colony consisted of two sharp blades, which it was easy to fasten into solid handles. The making of this first tool was greeted as a triumph: a precious conquest, and one that came at the right moment.

On setting out, it was the intention of the engineer to return to the western bank of the lake, where, the day before, he had noticed the clay, of which he had secured a specimen. Following the bank of the Mercy they crossed Prospect Plateau, and after a walk of five miles at most, they arrived at a glade some 200 paces distant from Lake Grant.

On the way, Herbert had discovered a tree from which the South American Indians make bows. It was the “crejimba,” of the palm family, which bears no edible fruit. From it they cut long straight branches, which they stripped of their leaves and shaped, thicker in the middle and thinner at the ends; and it only remained to find a plant fit to make the string of the bow. For cords they took the fibres of the “hibiscus heterophyllus”, a malvaceous plant, the fibres of which have a remarkable tenacity, and might have been compared to the tendons of an animal. Pencroff, having thus provided bows of considerable power, only needed arrows. Those were easily made from straight, stiff branches, free from knots, but it was not so easy to arm them with a substitute for iron. But Pencroff said to himself that, as he had done his share of the work, chance would do the rest.

The party had reached the place discovered the day before. The ground was composed of that clay which is used in making bricks and tiles, and was therefore just the thing for their purpose. The labor was not difficult. It was only necessary to temper the clay with sand, mould the bricks, and then bake them before a wood fire.

Usually, bricks are pressed in moulds, but the engineer contented himself with making these by hand. All this day and the next was employed in this work. The clay, soaked in water, was kneaded by the hands and feet of the manipulators, and then divided into blocks of equal size. A skilled workman can make, without machinery, as many as 10,000 bricks in twelve hours; but in the two days the five brickmakers of Lincoln Island had made but 3,000, which were ranged side by side to await the time when they would be dry enough throughout to bake, which would be in three or four days.

On the 2d of April, Smith occupied himself in fixing the orientation of the island.

The day before he had noted the precise minute at which the sun had set, allowing for the refraction. On this morning, he ascertained with equal precision the time of its rising. The intervening time was twelve hours and twenty-four minutes. Therefore six hours and twelve minutes after rising the sun would, on that day, pass exactly over the meridian, and the point in the sky which it would occupy at that instant would be north(1).

(1) In fact, at this season of the year and in this latitude, the sun rose at 5 h. 33 m. in the morning, and set at 6 h. 17 m. in the evening.

At the proper hour Smith marked this point, and by getting two trees, which were to serve him as landmarks, in line with the sun, obtained a fixed meridian for his future operations.

During the two days preceding the baking they occupied themselves by laying in a supply of firewood. Branches were cut from the edge of the clearing, and all the dead wood under the trees was picked up. And now and then they hunted in the neighborhood, the more successfully, as Pencroff had some dozens of arrows with very sharp points. It was Top who had provided these points by bringing in a porcupine, poor game enough, but of an undeniable value, thanks to the quills with which it bristled. These quills were firmly fastened to the ends of the arrows, and their flight was guided by feathering them with the cockatoo’s feathers. The reporter and Herbert soon became expert marksmen, and all kinds of game, such as cabiais, pigeons, agoutis, heath-cock, etc., abounded at the Chimneys. Most of these were killed in that part of the forest upon the left bank of the Mercy, which they had called Jacamar Wood, after the jacamar which Pencroff and Herbert had pursued there during their first exploration.

The meat was eaten fresh, but they preserved the hams of the cabiai by smoking them over a fire of green wood, having made them aromatic with odorous leaves. Still, nourishing as this food was, it was nothing but roast after roast, and they would have been glad of a simple pot of boiled meat upon the hearth; but first they must have the pot, and for this they must have the oven.

During these excursions, which were made only within a very narrow circle round the brick-field, the hunters noticed the recent tracks of large animals, armed with strong claws, but they could not tell their species; and Smith therefore cautioned them to use extreme prudence, as it was probable that there were some dangerous beasts in the forest.

He was right. For one day Spilett and Herbert saw an animal resembling a jaguar. But, fortunately, the beast did not attack them, as they might perhaps not have escaped without some serious wound. But, Spilett promised himself, as soon as he should obtain a proper weapon, such as one of the guns Pencroff begged for, that he would wage relentless war on all ferocious beasts and rid the island of their presence.

They did not do anything to the Chimneys during these few days, as the engineer hoped to discover, or to build, if need be, a more convenient habitation, but contented themselves by spreading fresh quantities of moss and dry leaves upon the sand in the corridors, and upon these primitive beds the tired workmen slept soundly. They also reckoned the days already passed on Lincoln Island, and began keeping a calendar. On the 5th of April, which was a Wednesday, it was twelve days since the wind had cast the castaways upon this shore.

On the 6th, at dawn, the engineer with his companions met at the place where the bricks were to be baked. Of course the operation was to be conducted in the open air, and not in an oven, or, rather, the pile of bricks would in itself form an enormous bake-oven which would bake itself. Carefully-prepared faggots were laid upon the ground, and surrounded with several tiers of dry bricks, which soon formed a great cube, on the outside of which air-holes had been left. The work occupied the whole day, and it was not until evening that they lit the fire.

That night nobody went to bed, and all night long they kept the fire supplied with fuel, taking care that it should not slacken.

The work lasted forty-eight hours, and succeeded perfectly. Then, as it was necessary to let the smoking mass cool, Neb and Pencroff, directed by Mr. Smith, brought, on a hurdle made of branches, several loads of carbonate of lime, a very common stone, which they found scattered in abundance to the north of the lake. These stones, decomposed by heat, furnished a very fat quick-lime, which increased greatly in bulk by slacking, and was fully as pure as if it had been produced by the calcination of chalk or marble. Mixed with sand in order to diminish its shrinkage while drying, this lime made an excellent mortar.

By the 9th of April the engineer had at his disposal a quantity of lime, all prepared, and some thousands of bricks. They, therefore, began at once the construction of an oven, in which to bake the various pottery indispensable for domestic use. This was accomplished without much difficulty; and, five days later, the oven was supplied with coal from the open vein, which the engineer had discovered near the mouth of Red Creek, and the first smoke escaped from a chimney some twenty feet high. The glade was transformed into a manufactory, and Pencroff was ready to believe that all the products of modern industry would be produced from this oven.

Meantime, what the colonists made first was a common pottery, but well fitted for cooking food. The material was the clay of the soil itself, to which Smith had them add a little lime and quartz, thus forming a true pipe-clay, from which they made pots, mugs which had been moulded upon pebbles of a suitable shape, plates, large jars, tubs to hold water, etc. Their form was rude and defective, but after they had been baked at a high temperature, the kitchen of the Chimneys found itself provided with a number of utensils as precious as if they were composed of the finest kaolin.

We must add that Pencroff, desirous of knowing whether this material deserved its name of pipe-clay, made himself some rather coarse pipes, which he found charming, but for which, alas! there was no tobacco. And, indeed, this was a great privation to the sailor.

“But the tobacco will come like everything else,” he would repeat in his moments of absolute confidence.

The work lasted until the 15th of April, and the time was well spent. The colonists having become potters, made nothing but pottery. When it would suit the engineer to make them smiths they would be smiths. But as the morrow would be Sunday, and moreover Easter Sunday, all agreed to sanctify the day by rest. These Americans were religious men, scrupulous observers of the precepts of the Bible, and their situation could only develop their trust in the Author of all things.

On the evening of the 15th they returned permanently to the Chimneys, bringing the rest of the pottery back with them, and putting out the oven fire until there should be use for it again. This return was marked by the fortunate discovery by the engineer of a substance that would answer for tinder, which, we know, is the spongy, velvety pulp of a certain mushroom of the polypore genus. Properly prepared it is extremely inflammable, especially when previously saturated with gunpowder, or boiled in a solution of nitrate or chlorate of potash. But until then they had found no polypores, nor even any of the morels which can take their place. Now, the engineer, having found a certain plant belonging to the mugwort genus, which counts among its principal species wormwood, southernwood, tarragon, genipi, etc., broke off some tufts, and, handing them to the sailor, said:—

“Here, Pencroff, is something that will please you.”

Pencroff examined the plant, with its long silky threads and leaves covered with a cotton-like down.

“What is it, Mr. Smith?” he asked. “Good heavens! Is it tobacco?”

“No,” answered Smith; “it is artemisia, known to science as Chinese mugwort, but to us it will be tinder.”

This mugwort, properly dried, furnished a very inflammable substance, especially when, later on, the engineer had impregnated it with that nitrate of potash of which the island possessed several beds, and which is the same as saltpetre.

This evening the colonists, seated in the central chamber, supped with comfort. Neb had prepared some boiled agouti, a spiced cabiai ham, and the boiled corms of the “caladium macrorhizum,” an herbaceous plant of the arad family, which under the tropics takes a tree form. These corms, which are very nutritious, had an excellent flavor, something like that of the substance sold in England under the name of Portland sago, and measurably supplied the place of bread, which the colonists were still without.

Supper finished, before going to sleep the party took a stroll upon the beach. It was 8 o’clock, and the night was magnificent. The moon, which had been full five days before, was not yet risen, but the horizon was already silvered with those soft, pale tints which may be called the lunar dawn; and in the southern zenith the circumpolar constellations shone resplendent, and among them all the Southern Cross, which the engineer, a few days before, had saluted from the summit of Mount Franklin. For some moments the engineer gazed attentively at this splendid constellation. At its summit and base were two stars of the first magnitude, and on the left arm and the right, stars, respectively, of the second magnitude and the third. Then, after some reflection, he said:—

“Herbert, is not to-day the 15th of April?”

“Yes, sir,” answered the lad.

“Then, if I am not mistaken, to-morrow will be one of the four days in the year when the mean and real time are the same; that is to say, my boy, that to-morrow, within some seconds of noon by the clocks, the sun will pass the meridian. If, therefore, the weather is clear, I think I will be able to obtain the longitude of the island within a few degrees.”

“Without a sextant or instruments?” asked Spilett.

“Yes,” replied the engineer. “And since it is so clear, I will try to-night to find our latitude by calculating the height of the Cross, that is, of the Southern Pole, above the horizon. You see, my friends, before settling down, it will not do to be content with determining this land to be an island; we must find out, as nearly as possible, at what distance it lies from the American continent, or the Australian, or the principal archipelagoes of the Pacific.”

“Indeed,” said the reporter, “instead of building a house, it may be better to build a ship, if by chance we are only some hundred miles from an inhabited coast.”

“That is why,” resumed Smith, “I am now going to try to get the latitude of Lincoln Island, and to-morrow noon to calculate the longitude.”

If the engineer had possessed a sextant, an instrument which measures with great precision the angular distance of objects by reflection, the work would have been easy, as this evening, by taking the height of the pole, and to-morrow by the sun’s passage of the meridian, he would have the co-ordinates of the island. But, having no instruments he must devise something. So returning to the Chimneys, he made, by the light of the fire, two little flat sticks which he joined together at one end with a strong acacia thorn, taken from the dead wood of the wood-pile, in a way that they could be opened and shut like compasses, and returned with them to the beach. But as he must take the height of the pole above a sharply defined horizon, that is to say, a sea horizon, and as the southern horizon was hidden from this point by Claw Cape, he was obliged to seek a more suitable station. The best would evidently have been the shore facing directly south; but it would have been necessary to cross the Mercy, which was then deep, and this was a difficulty.

The engineer therefore determined to make his observation from Prospect Plateau, intending to allow for its height above the level of the sea, and leaving, until the next day, the computation of that height, which could easily be done by elementary geometry.

The colonists, therefore, went up to the plateau by the left bank of the Mercy, and placed themselves on the edge which ran north-west and south-east, that is to say, upon that line of fantastic rocks which bordered the river. This part of the plateau rose some fifty feet above the heights of the right bank, which descended, by a double slope, to the end of Claw Cape and to the southern boundary of the island. Nothing obstructed the vision, which extended over half the horizon from the Cape to Reptile Promontory. To the south, this horizon, lit from below by the first light of the moon, was sharply defined against the sky, and could be sighted with some precision. The Cross was at this time reversed, the star Alpha, which marks its base, being nearest the southern pole. This constellation is not situated as near to the southern as the polar star is to the northern pole; Alpha is about 27° from it, but Smith knew this and could calculate accordingly. He took care also to observe it at the instant when it passed the meridian under the pole, thus simplifying the operation.

The engineer opened the arms of his compass so that one pointed to the sea horizon and the other to the star Alpha, as he would have done the telescopes of a repeating circle, and thus obtained the angle of distance which separated them. And in order to fix this distance immovably, he fastened these arms, respectively, by means of thorns, to a third piece of wood placed crosswise, so that their opening was firmly kept. This done, it was only necessary to calculate the angle obtained, bringing the observation to the level of the sea so as to allow for the depression of the horizon, which made it necessary to measure the height of the plateau. The measurement of this angle would thus give the height of Alpha, and consequently that of the pole, above the horizon; or, since the latitude of a point on the globe is always equal to the height of the pole above the horizon at that point, the latitude of the island.

This calculation was postponed until the next day, and by 10 o’clock everybody slept profoundly.
