# The Peninsula and the Open Corral (final)

CHAPTER LIV.

EXPLORATION OF SERPENTINE PENINSULA—CAMP AT THE MOUTH OF FALL RIVER—SIX HUNDRED PACES FROM THE CORRAL—RECONNOISSANCE MADE BY SPILETT AND PENCROFF—THEIR RETURN—ALL FORWARD!—AN OPEN DOOR—A LIGHTED WINDOW—BY THE LIGHT OF THE MOON.

The next day, the 18th of February, was devoted to the exploration of all that wooded part which formed the shore from Reptile End to Fall River. The colonists were able to search this forest thoroughly, for its breadth varied from three to four miles, as it was enclosed between the two shores of Serpentine Peninsula. The trees, by their lofty height and their thick branches, bore witness to the vegetative power of the soil, more astonishing here than in any other part of the island. One would have said it was a corner of those virgin forests of America or of Central Africa, carried into this middle zone. This led one to admit that these superb trees found in this soil, damp in its upper layer, but heated within by volcanic fires, a warmth which could not belong to a temperate climate. The prevailing kinds were precisely those kauris and eucalyptus which here reached gigantic dimensions.

But the object of the colonists was not to admire these vegetable splendors. They knew already that, in this respect, Lincoln Island would have deserved to take rank in that group of the Canaries, of which the first name was that of the Fortunate Isles. Now, alas! their island no longer belonged wholly to them; others had taken possession of it, villains trod its soil, and they must be destroyed to the last.

On the western coast no further traces were found, whatever care was taken in seeking them. No more footprints, no more broken branches, no more cold ashes, no more deserted encampments.

“This does not surprise me,” said Smith to his companions. “The convicts landed on the island in the neighborhood of Jetsam Point, and they at once threw themselves into the forests of the Far West, after having crossed Tadorn’s Fens. They thus followed nearly the road which we took on leaving Granite House. That explains the traces which we found in the woods. But, having arrived on the shore, the convicts understood well that they would find no suitable retreat there, and it was then that, having gone up again towards the north, they discovered the corral—”

“Where they have perhaps returned—” said Pencroff.

“I do not think so,” replied the engineer, “for they must suppose that our search would be directed that way. The corral is for them only a place of supply, and not a settled encampment.”

“I am of Cyrus’s opinion,” said the reporter, “and, in my view, it must be among the spurs of Mount Franklin that the convicts will have sought a lair.”

“Then, Mr. Smith, straight to the corral!” cried Pencroff. “We must put an end to it, and till now we have lost our time!”

“No, my friend,” replied the engineer. “You forget that it was to our interest to know whether the forests of the Far West did not contain some dwelling. Our exploration has a double object, Pencroff. If, on the one hand, we must punish crime, on the other, we have an act of gratitude to perform!”

“That is well spoken, Mr. Smith,” replied the sailor. “My opinion is, all the same, that we shall find that gentleman only if he pleases!”

And, truly, Pencroff only expressed the opinion of all. It was probable that the retreat of the unknown would be no less mysterious than he was himself!

That evening the wagon stopped at the mouth of Fall River. The camp was arranged according to custom, and the usual precautions were taken for the night. Herbert, become again the vigorous and healthy lad that he had been before his illness, profited largely by this life in the open air, between the breezes of the ocean and the invigorating atmosphere of the forests. His place was no longer on the wagon, but at the head of the caravan.

The next day, the 19th of February, the colonists left the shore, on which, beyond the mouth, basalts of every shape were so picturesquely heaped, and ascended the course of the river by its left bank. The road was partly cleared, in consequence of the former excursions which had been made from the corral to the west coast. The colonists were then at a distance of six miles from Mount Franklin.

The engineer’s plan was this: to observe minutely all the valley of which the thalweg formed the bed of the river, and to approach the neighborhood of the corral with caution; if the corral was occupied, to take it by force; if it was not, to entrench themselves there and make it the centre of the operations which should have for their object the exploration of Mount Franklin.

This plan was unanimously approved by the colonists, and they were impatient indeed to have regained entire possession of their island!

They proceeded, then, along the narrow valley which separated two of the most powerful spurs of Mount Franklin. The trees, crowded on the banks of the river, became scarce towards the upper zones of the volcano. It was hilly ground, rather broken, very favorable for ambushes, and over which they ventured only with extreme precaution. Top and Jup went ahead as scouts, and, springing right and left into the thick coppices, rivalled each other in intelligence and skill. But nothing showed that the banks of the stream had been recently frequented; nothing announced either the presence or the proximity of the convicts.

Towards five o’clock in the evening the wagon stopped about six hundred paces from the palisaded enclosure. A semicircular curtain of tall trees still hid it.

It was necessary, therefore, to reconnoitre the corral, in order to know whether it was occupied. To go there openly, in broad daylight, if the convicts were in the least in ambush there, was to expose themselves to some evil blow, as had happened to Herbert. It was better, then, to wait until night had come.

However, Spilett wished, without further delay, to reconnoitre the approaches to the corral, and Pencroff, at the end of his patience, offered to accompany him.

“No, my friends,” replied the engineer. “Wait for night. I will not let one of you expose himself in open day.”

“But, Mr. Smith—” replied the sailor, little disposed to obey.

“I beg you, Pencroff,” said the engineer.

“So be it!” replied the sailor, who gave another course to his anger by bestowing on the convicts the roughest epithets in the seaman’s vocabulary.

The colonists remained, therefore, around the wagon, and they carefully watched the neighboring parts of the forest.

Three hours passed thus. The wind had fallen, and an absolute silence reigned under the great trees. The snapping of the slightest branch, a sound of steps on the dry leaves, the gliding of a body among the grass, would have been heard without difficulty. All was quiet. Besides, Top, lying on the ground, his head stretched out on his paws, gave no sign of uneasiness.

At eight o’clock the evening appeared far enough advanced for the reconnoissance to be made under good conditions. Spilett declared himself ready to set out, in company with Pencroff. Smith consented. Top and Jup were to remain with the engineer, Herbert, and Neb, for a bark or a cry, uttered at the wrong moment, must not give the alarm.

“Do not engage yourselves imprudently,” Smith recommended to the sailor and the reporter. “You have not to take possession of the corral, but only to find out whether it is occupied or not.”

“Agreed,” replied Pencroff.

And both set out.

Under the trees, thanks to the thickness of their foliage, a certain obscurity already rendered objects invisible beyond a radius of thirty to forty feet. The reporter and Pencroff, stopping whenever any sound seemed to them suspicious, advanced only with the most extreme precautions.

They walked apart from each other, so as to offer less mark to a shot. And, to tell all, they expected every instant that a report would ring out.

Five minutes after having left the wagon, Spilett and Pencroff had arrived at the edge of the wood, before the clearing at the end of which rose the palisaded enclosure.

They stopped. A few vague gleams still bathed the meadow, which was bare of trees. Thirty paces off stood the gate of the corral, which appeared to be shut. These thirty paces, which had to be crossed between the edge of the wood and the enclosure, constituted the dangerous zone, to employ an expression borrowed from ballistics. In fact, one or more balls, fired from the top of the palisade, would have struck to the ground whoever had ventured upon this zone.

Spilett and the sailor were not men to draw back, but they knew that an imprudence on their part, of which they would be the first victims, would fall afterwards on their companions. If they were killed, what would become of Smith, Neb, and Herbert?

But Pencroff, excited beyond measure at feeling himself so near the corral, where he supposed that the convicts had taken refuge, was about to move forward, when the reporter held him back with a vigorous hand.

“In a few moments it will be quite dark,” whispered Spilett in Pencroff’s ear, “and that will be the time to act.”

Pencroff, convulsively clutching the stock of his gun, restrained himself and waited, grumbling.

Soon the last gleams of twilight faded completely. The shadow, which seemed to come out of the thick forest, invaded the clearing. Mount Franklin rose like an enormous screen before the western horizon, and darkness came on rapidly, as happens in regions already low in latitude. It was the moment.

The reporter and Pencroff, since they had posted themselves on the edge of the wood, had not lost sight of the palisaded enclosure. The corral seemed to be absolutely abandoned. The top of the palisade formed a line a little blacker than the surrounding shadow, and nothing broke its sharpness. Nevertheless, if the convicts were there, they must have posted one of their number, so as to guard against any surprise.

Spilett pressed his companion’s hand, and both crept forward towards the corral, their guns ready to fire.

They reached the gate of the enclosure without the darkness having been crossed by a single ray of light.

Pencroff tried to push the gate, which, as the reporter and he had supposed, was shut. However, the sailor was able to ascertain that the outer bars had not been put up.

It might be concluded, then, that the convicts were at that time in the corral, and that, very likely, they had fastened the gate so that it could not be forced.

Spilett and Pencroff listened.

Not a sound within the enclosure. The mouflons and the goats, asleep no doubt in their stables, in no way disturbed the calm of the night.

The reporter and the sailor, hearing nothing, asked themselves whether they ought to scale the palisade and penetrate into the corral. This was contrary to Smith’s instructions.

It is true that the attempt might succeed, but it might also fail. Now, if the convicts suspected nothing, if they had no knowledge of the expedition undertaken against them, if, in short, there existed at that moment a chance of surprising them, ought they to compromise this chance by venturing heedlessly to cross the palisade?

That was not the reporter’s opinion. He thought it reasonable to wait until the colonists were all together before trying to penetrate into the corral. What was certain was that they could reach the palisade without being seen, and that the enclosure did not appear to be guarded. This point settled, there was nothing more to do but to return to the wagon, and then they would consider.

Pencroff probably shared this view, for he made no difficulty about following the reporter, when the latter fell back under the wood.

A few minutes after, the engineer was made acquainted with the state of affairs.

“Well,” said he, after having reflected, “I have now reason to believe that the convicts are not in the corral.”

“We shall know well enough,” replied Pencroff, “when we have scaled the enclosure.”

“To the corral, my friends!” said Smith.

“Shall we leave the wagon in the wood?” asked Neb.

“No,” replied the engineer, “it is our wagon of ammunition and provisions, and, if need be, it will serve us as an entrenchment.”

“Forward, then!” said Spilett.

The wagon came out of the wood and began to roll noiselessly towards the palisade. The darkness was then profound, the silence as complete as at the moment when Pencroff and the reporter had gone away, creeping along the ground. The thick grass completely smothered the sound of their steps.

The colonists were ready to fire. Jup, by Pencroff’s order, kept behind. Neb led Top in a leash, so that he should not spring forward.

The clearing soon appeared, and it was deserted. Without hesitating, the little band moved towards the enclosure. In a short space of time the dangerous zone was crossed, and not a shot had been fired. When the wagon had reached the palisade, it stopped. Neb remained at the onaggas’ heads to hold them in. The engineer, the reporter, Herbert, and Pencroff then went towards the gate, in order to see whether it was barricaded within—

One of the leaves was open!

“But what did you tell me?” asked the engineer, turning towards the sailor and Spilett.

Both were stupefied.

“On my salvation,” said Pencroff, “this gate was shut just now!”

The colonists then hesitated. Had the convicts, then, been in the corral at the moment when Pencroff and the reporter were reconnoitring it? That could not be doubted, since the gate, then shut, could have been opened only by them! Were they there still, or had one of their number just gone out?

All these questions presented themselves instantly to the mind of each, but how were they to be answered?

At this moment Herbert, who had advanced a few steps within the enclosure, drew back hastily and seized Smith’s hand.

“What is the matter?” asked the engineer.

“A light!”

“In the house?”

“Yes!”

All five advanced to the gate, and, in fact, through the panes of the window which faced them, they saw a feeble glimmer trembling.

Smith rapidly made up his mind.

“It is a unique chance,” said he to his companions, “to find the convicts shut up in this house, expecting nothing! They are ours! Forward!”

The colonists then slipped into the enclosure, their guns ready to be raised to the shoulder. The wagon had been left outside in the charge of Jup and Top, who had been tied to it for prudence.

Smith, Pencroff, and Spilett on one side, Herbert and Neb on the other, going along the palisade, examined that part of the corral, which was absolutely dark and deserted.

In a few moments all were near the house, before the door, which was shut.

Smith made his companions a sign with his hand which bade them not to stir, and he approached the window, then faintly lit by the light within.

His gaze plunged into the single room which formed the ground floor of the house.

On the table shone a lighted lantern. Near the table was the bed which had formerly served Ayrton.

On the bed lay the body of a man.

Suddenly Smith drew back, and in a stifled voice,—

“Ayrton!” he cried.

Immediately the door was burst in rather than opened, and the colonists rushed into the room.

Ayrton appeared to be asleep. His face showed that he had suffered long and cruelly. On his wrists and his ankles large bruises could be seen.

Smith bent over him.

“Ayrton!” cried the engineer, seizing the arm of him whom he had just found again in circumstances so unexpected.

At this call Ayrton opened his eyes, and, looking Smith full in the face, then the others,—

“You,” he cried, “you?”

“Ayrton! Ayrton!” repeated Smith.

“Where am I?”

“In the house at the corral!”

“Alone?”

“Yes!”

“But they will come!” cried Ayrton. “Defend yourselves! defend yourselves!”

And Ayrton fell back exhausted.

“Spilett,” then said the engineer, “we may be attacked from one moment to another. Bring the wagon into the corral. Then barricade the gate, and all of you come back here.”

Pencroff, Neb, and the reporter hastened to execute the engineer’s orders. There was not an instant to lose. Perhaps even the wagon was already in the hands of the convicts!

In a moment the reporter and his two companions had crossed the corral and regained the gate of the palisade, behind which Top could be heard growling low.

The engineer, leaving Ayrton for an instant, came out of the house, ready to fire. Herbert was at his side. Both watched the crest of the spur which overlooked the corral. If the convicts were in ambush in that place, they could strike down the colonists one after the other.

At this moment the moon appeared in the east above the black curtain of the forest, and a white sheet of light spread over the interior of the enclosure. The whole corral was lighted up, with its clumps of trees, the little stream which watered it, and its wide carpet of grass. On the side of the mountain the house and a part of the palisade stood out white. On the opposite side, towards the gate, the enclosure remained dark.

A black mass soon showed itself. It was the wagon entering the circle of light, and Smith could hear the noise of the gate, which his companions were shutting again, and the leaves of which they were firmly securing on the inside.

But at this moment Top, violently breaking his leash, began to bark furiously and rushed towards the back of the corral, on the right of the house.

“Look out, my friends, and take aim!—” cried Smith.

The colonists had raised their guns to the shoulder and were waiting the moment to fire. Top still barked, and Jup, running towards the dog, uttered shrill whistles.

The colonists followed him and arrived on the bank of the little brook, which was shaded by tall trees.

And there, in the full light, what did they see?

Five bodies, stretched upon the bank!

They were those of the convicts who, four months before, had landed on Lincoln Island!
