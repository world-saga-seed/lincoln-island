CHAPTER XIX.

CYRUS SMITH’S PLAN—THE FRONT OF GRANITE HOUSE—THE ROPE LADDER—PENCROFF’S DREAMS—AROMATIC HERBS—A NATURAL WARREN—DIVERSION OF THE WATERS FOR THE NEEDS OF THE NEW DWELLING—VIEW FROM THE WINDOWS OF GRANITE HOUSE.

On the morrow, May 22, were commenced the works destined for the special fitting-up of the new dwelling. The colonists were impatient, indeed, to exchange their insufficient shelter of the Chimneys for this vast and healthful retreat, hollowed out of the solid rock, sheltered from the waters of the sea and of the sky. The Chimneys were not to be entirely abandoned, however, and the engineer’s project was to make of them a workshop for heavy labors.

Cyrus Smith’s first care was to ascertain at what precise point the front of Granite House was situated. He repaired to the beach, at the foot of the enormous wall, and, as the pick which had slipped from the reporter’s hands must have fallen perpendicularly, it was enough to find this pick again in order to recognize the spot where the hole had been pierced in the granite.

The pick was easily found, and, indeed, a hole opened in a perpendicular line above the point where it had stuck into the sand, about eighty feet above the beach. Some rock-pigeons were already entering and leaving by this narrow opening. It truly seemed as if it were for them that Granite House had been discovered!

The engineer’s intention was to divide the right-hand portion of the cavern into several chambers preceded by an entrance passage, and to light it by means of five windows and a door pierced in the front. Pencroff readily admitted the five windows, but he did not understand the utility of the door, since the old outlet offered a natural staircase, by which it would always be easy to gain access to Granite House.

“My friend,” answered Cyrus Smith, “if it is easy for us to reach our dwelling by the outlet, it will be equally easy for others than ourselves. I intend, on the contrary, to obstruct this outlet at its orifice, to stop it up hermetically, and, if need be, even to conceal its entrance completely by causing, by means of a dam, a rise in the waters of the lake.”

“And how shall we enter?” asked the sailor.

“By an exterior ladder,” answered Cyrus Smith, “a rope ladder, which, once drawn up, will render access to our dwelling impossible.”

“But why so many precautions?” said Pencroff. “Hitherto the animals have not seemed to us to be very formidable. As for being inhabited by natives, our island is not!”

“Are you quite sure of that, Pencroff?” asked the engineer, looking at the sailor.

“We shall only be sure of it, evidently, when we have explored it in all its parts,” answered Pencroff.

“Yes,” said Cyrus Smith, “for we know as yet only a small portion of it. But, in any case, if we have no enemies within, they may come from without, for these regions of the Pacific are dangerous waters. Let us therefore take our precautions against every eventuality.”

Cyrus Smith spoke wisely, and, without making any further objection, Pencroff prepared to execute his orders.

The front of Granite House was thus to be lighted by means of five windows and a door, serving what constituted “the apartment” properly so called, and by means of a wide bay and bull’s-eyes which would allow light to enter in profusion into that marvelous nave which was to serve as the large hall. This front, situated at a height of eighty feet above the ground, faced the east, and the rising sun saluted it with its first rays. It extended along that portion of the curtain wall comprised between the projection forming an angle at the mouth of the Mercy, and a line drawn perpendicularly above the heap of rocks which formed the Chimneys. Thus the bad winds, that is to say those from the northeast, struck it only obliquely, for it was protected by the very orientation of the projection. Moreover, while waiting for the window-frames to be made, the engineer intended to close the openings with thick shutters, which would let through neither wind nor rain, and which he could conceal in case of need.

The first work consisted, therefore, in hollowing out these openings. The working of the pick upon this hard rock would have been too slow, and it is well known that Cyrus Smith was a man of great resources. He still had a certain quantity of nitro-glycerine at his disposal, and he employed it usefully. The effect of the explosive substance was suitably localized, and, under its force, the granite gave way at the very places chosen by the engineer. Then the pick and the mattock finished the ogive design of the five windows, of the vast bay, of the bull’s-eyes, and of the door; they dressed their casings, the profiles of which were somewhat capriciously shaped, and, a few days after the commencement of the works, Granite House was broadly lighted by that eastern light, which penetrated even into its most secret depths.

According to the plan decided upon by Cyrus Smith, the apartment was to be divided into five compartments looking out upon the sea: on the right, an entrance served by a door to which the ladder would lead, then a first room-kitchen, thirty feet wide, a dining-room, measuring forty feet, a sleeping-chamber, of equal width, and finally a “guest-chamber,” demanded by Pencroff, and which adjoined the large hall.

These rooms, or rather this suite of rooms, which formed the apartment of Granite House, were not to occupy the entire depth of the cavity. They were to be served by a corridor contrived between them and a long store-room, in which the utensils, provisions, and reserves would find ample space. All the products gathered in the island, those of the flora as well as those of the fauna, would be there in excellent conditions of preservation, and completely sheltered from dampness. Space was not wanting, and each object could be methodically arranged. Besides, the colonists still had at their disposal the small grotto situated above the great cavern, which would be like the garret of the new dwelling.

This plan decided upon, nothing remained but to put it into execution. The miners therefore became brick-makers again; then the bricks were brought and deposited at the foot of Granite House.

Hitherto Cyrus Smith and his companions had had access to the cavern only by the old outlet. This mode of communication obliged them first to ascend to Prospect Plateau by making a detour along the bank of the river, to descend two hundred feet by the passage, and then to climb up as much again when they wished to return to the plateau. Hence, loss of time and considerable fatigue. Cyrus Smith resolved, therefore, to proceed without delay to the fabrication of a stout rope ladder, which, once hauled up, would render the entrance to Granite House absolutely inaccessible.

This ladder was made with extreme care, and its side-ropes, formed of the fibers of the “curry-rush” braided by means of a reel, had the strength of a thick cable. As for the rungs, a species of red cedar, with light and tough branches, furnished them, and the apparatus was wrought with a master-hand by Pencroff.

Other ropes were likewise manufactured from vegetable fibers, and a sort of rude tackle was installed at the door. In this way the bricks could be easily hoisted up to the level of Granite House. The transport of materials was thus greatly simplified, and the interior fitting-up, properly so called, began immediately. Lime was not lacking, and several thousand bricks were there, ready to be used. The framework of the partitions, very rudimentary moreover, was easily erected, and, in a very short time, the apartment was divided into rooms and store-room, according to the agreed plan.

These various works were carried on rapidly, under the direction of the engineer, who himself handled the hammer and the trowel. No manual labor was foreign to Cyrus Smith, who thus set the example to intelligent and zealous companions. They worked with confidence, gaily even, Pencroff always having a word to make them laugh, now carpenter, now rope-maker, now mason, and communicating his good humor to all this little world. His faith in the engineer was absolute. Nothing could have shaken it. He believed him capable of undertaking everything and succeeding in everything. The question of clothes and shoes—a serious question, assuredly—that of lighting during the winter nights, the cultivation of the fertile portions of the island, the transformation of this wild flora into a civilized flora, all seemed easy to him with Cyrus Smith’s aid, and all would be done in its own good time. He dreamed of canalized rivers, facilitating the transport of the riches of the soil, of the working of quarries and mines to be undertaken, of machines suited to all industrial practices, of railroads, yes, of railroads! whose network would certainly cover Lincoln Island one day.

The engineer let Pencroff talk on. He checked none of the exaggerations of this brave heart. He knew how contagious confidence is; he even smiled to hear him speak, and said nothing of the anxieties which the future sometimes inspired in him. Indeed, in this part of the Pacific, out of the track of vessels, he might fear never to be rescued. It was upon themselves, therefore, upon themselves alone, that the colonists had to count, for the distance from Lincoln Island to any other land was such that to venture forth in a boat, of necessarily mediocre construction, would be a serious and perilous thing.

“But,” as the sailor said, “they surpassed by a hundred cubits the Robinsons of old, for whom everything had to be done by miracle.”

And indeed, they “knew,” and the man who “knows” succeeds where others would vegetate and inevitably perish.

During these works, Herbert distinguished himself. He was intelligent and active; he understood quickly, executed well, and Cyrus Smith became more and more attached to this boy. Herbert felt for the engineer a lively and respectful friendship. Pencroff clearly saw the close sympathy which was forming between these two beings, but he was not at all jealous of it.

Neb was Neb. He was what he would always be—courage, zeal, devotion, self-abnegation personified. He had the same faith in his master as Pencroff, but he manifested it less noisily. When the sailor grew enthusiastic, Neb always seemed to answer him: “Why, nothing is more natural.” Pencroff and he loved each other dearly, and had not been long in addressing each other like old comrades.

As for Gideon Spilett, he took his share of the common labor, and was not the most awkward—at which the sailor was always a little astonished. A “journalist” clever, not only at understanding everything, but at executing everything!

The ladder was finally installed on May 28. Not fewer than a hundred rungs were counted on this perpendicular height of eighty feet which it measured. Cyrus Smith had fortunately been able to divide it into two parts, by taking advantage of a projection of the wall which jutted out about forty feet above the ground. This ledge, carefully leveled with the pick, became a sort of landing to which was fastened the first ladder, whose swaying was thus diminished by half, and which a rope permitted them to draw up to the level of Granite House. As for the second ladder, it was secured both at its lower end, which rested on the ledge, and at its upper end, fastened to the door itself. In this manner, the ascent became notably easier. Besides, Cyrus Smith intended to install later a hydraulic elevator which would save all fatigue and all loss of time to the inhabitants of Granite House.

The colonists quickly accustomed themselves to using this ladder. They were nimble and adroit, and Pencroff, in his capacity as a sailor, accustomed to run up the ratlines of the shrouds, was able to give them lessons. But he had to give them to Top as well. The poor dog, with his four paws, was not built for this exercise. Yet Pencroff was so zealous a master that Top ended by performing his ascents creditably, and soon climbed the ladder as his fellows commonly do in circuses. How proud the sailor was of his pupil cannot be told. Nevertheless, and more than once, Pencroff carried him up on his back, of which Top never complained.

It will be observed here that during these works, which were actively pushed forward, however, for the bad season was approaching, the question of food had not been neglected. Every day, the reporter and Herbert, now decidedly become the purveyors of the colony, spent several hours in hunting. They as yet exploited only the Jacamar Woods, on the left bank of the river, for, for want of a bridge and a boat, the Mercy had not yet been crossed. All those immense forests to which had been given the name of the Forests of the Far West were therefore not explored. This important excursion was reserved for the first fine days of the coming spring. But the Jacamar Woods were sufficiently stocked with game; kangaroos and wild boars abounded there, and the iron-tipped spears, the bow and the arrows of the hunters did wonders. Moreover, Herbert discovered, toward the southwest angle of the lagoon, a natural warren, a sort of slightly damp meadow, covered with willows and aromatic herbs which perfumed the air, such as thyme, wild thyme, basil, savory—all fragrant species of the labiate family, of which rabbits show themselves so fond.

Upon the reporter’s observation that, since the table was set for rabbits, it would be astonishing if rabbits were lacking, the two hunters carefully explored this warren. In any case, it produced useful plants in abundance, and a naturalist would have had there the opportunity of studying many specimens of the vegetable kingdom. Herbert thus gathered a certain quantity of shoots of basil, rosemary, balm, betony, etc., which possess various therapeutic properties, some pectoral, astringent, febrifugal, others antispasmodic or antirheumatic. And when, later, Pencroff asked of what use all this harvest of herbs would be:

“To doctor ourselves,” answered the boy, “to treat ourselves when we are sick.”

“Why should we be sick, since there are no doctors on the island?” answered Pencroff very seriously.

To that there was nothing to reply, but the boy nonetheless made his harvest, which was very well received at Granite House. All the more so as to these medicinal plants he was able to add a notable quantity of *Monarda didyma*, which are known in North America under the name of “Oswego tea,” and produce an excellent beverage.

Finally, that day, by searching thoroughly, the two hunters came upon the true site of the warren. The ground there was perforated like a skimmer.

“Burrows!” cried Herbert.

“Yes,” answered the reporter, “I see them plainly.”

“But are they inhabited?”

“That is the question.”

The question was not long in being solved. Almost immediately, hundreds of little animals, resembling rabbits, fled in all directions, and with such rapidity that Top himself could not outstrip them. Hunters and dog ran in vain; these rodents easily escaped them. But the reporter was quite resolved not to leave the spot before having captured at least half a dozen of these quadrupeds. He wished to stock the larder first of all, even if they should domesticate those that might be taken later. With a few snares set at the mouth of the burrows, the operation could not fail to succeed. But at this moment, no snares, nor anything to make them of. They had therefore to resign themselves to visiting each burrow, to poking it out with a stick, to doing, by dint of patience, what they could not do otherwise.

At last, after an hour of searching, four rodents were taken in their burrows. They were rabbits quite similar to their fellows of Europe, and which are commonly known under the name of “American rabbits.”

The product of the hunt was therefore carried back to Granite House, and it figured at the evening meal. The tenants of this warren were not to be disdained, for they were delicious. This was a precious resource for the colony, and one which seemed as if it must be inexhaustible.

On May 31, the partitions were finished. Nothing remained but to furnish the rooms, which would be the work of the long winter days. A fireplace was built in the first room, which served as a kitchen. The flue intended to carry the smoke outside gave some trouble to the improvised chimney-builders. It seemed simpler to Cyrus Smith to make it of brick-clay; as there could be no thought of giving it an outlet through the upper plateau, a hole was pierced in the granite above the window of the said kitchen, and to this hole the pipe, obliquely directed, led like that of a sheet-iron stove. Perhaps, doubtless even, during the high east winds which beat directly against the front, the chimney would smoke, but these winds were rare, and, besides, Master Neb, the cook, was not so particular.

When these interior arrangements had been completed, the engineer set about obstructing the orifice of the old outlet which led to the lake, so as to forbid all access by that way. Blocks of rock were rolled to the opening and strongly cemented. Cyrus Smith did not yet carry out the project he had formed of submerging this orifice beneath the waters of the lake by bringing them back to their former level by a dam. He contented himself with concealing the obstruction by means of grasses, shrubs, or brushwood, which were planted in the interstices of the rocks, and which the coming spring would cause to grow luxuriantly.

Nevertheless, he utilized the outlet in such a way as to bring to the new dwelling a trickle of the fresh waters of the lake. A small cut, made below their level, produced this result, and this diversion of a pure and inexhaustible spring gave a yield of twenty-five to thirty gallons[^1] a day. Water would therefore never be lacking at Granite House.

At last, all was finished, and it was high time, for the bad season was arriving. Thick shutters permitted the windows of the front to be closed, until the engineer should have time to manufacture window-glass.

Gideon Spilett had very artistically arranged, in the projections of the rock around the windows, plants of varied species, as well as long trailing grasses, and, in this way, the openings were framed in a picturesque verdure of charming effect.

The inhabitants of the solid, healthful, and safe dwelling could not, therefore, but be delighted with their work. The windows allowed their gaze to sweep over a boundless horizon, which the two Mandible Capes closed on the north and Claw Cape on the south. All of Union Bay stretched out magnificently before them. Yes, these worthy colonists had reason to be satisfied, and Pencroff did not stint his praises of what he humorously called “his apartment on the fifth floor above the entresol!”

[^1]: The gallon is equal to about four and a half liters.
