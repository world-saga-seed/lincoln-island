# Herbert Recovers and the Expedition Sets Out (final)

CHAPTER LIII.

INEXPLICABLE MYSTERY—HERBERT’S CONVALESCENCE—THE PARTS OF THE ISLAND TO BE EXPLORED—PREPARATIONS FOR DEPARTURE—THE FIRST DAY—THE NIGHT—THE SECOND DAY—THE KAURIS—THE PAIR OF CASSOWARIES—FOOTPRINTS IN THE FOREST.

Spilett took the box and opened it. It contained about two hundred grains of a white powder, a few particles of which he carried to his lips. The extreme bitterness of this substance could not deceive him. It was indeed the precious alkaloid of quinquina, the anti-periodic above all others.

This powder must be given to Herbert without hesitation. How it came to be there would be discussed later.

“Some coffee,” said Spilett.

A few moments after, Neb brought a cup of the lukewarm infusion. Spilett threw into it about eighteen grains (1) of the quinine, and they succeeded in making Herbert drink the mixture.

(1) 10 grams.

There was still time, for the third attack of the pernicious fever had not yet shown itself!

And, it may be added here, it was not to return!

Besides, it must be said also, all had recovered hope. The mysterious influence had again been exerted, and at a supreme moment, when they had despaired of it!

At the end of a few hours Herbert was resting more quietly. The colonists were then able to talk over this incident. The intervention of the unknown was more evident than ever. But how had he been able to make his way during the night into Granite House? It was absolutely inexplicable, and, in truth, the manner in which the “genius of the island” proceeded was not less strange than the genius himself.

During that day, and about every three hours, the sulphate of quinine was administered to Herbert.

From the next day Herbert felt a certain improvement. Certainly he was not cured, and intermittent fevers are subject to frequent and dangerous relapses; but he did not lack for care. And then, the specific was there, and not far off, no doubt, was he who had brought it! In short, an immense hope returned to the hearts of all.

This hope was not deceived. Ten days after, on the 20th of December, Herbert entered upon his convalescence. He was still weak, and a severe diet had been imposed upon him, but no attack had returned. And then, the docile boy submitted so willingly to all the prescriptions that were laid upon him! He so longed to get well!

Pencroff was like a man who has been drawn up from the bottom of an abyss. He had fits of joy which bordered on delirium. When the time of the third attack had gone by, he had hugged the reporter in his arms until he nearly stifled him. Since then he called him nothing but Doctor Spilett.

The true doctor remained to be discovered.

“We will discover him!” the sailor repeated.

And certainly this man, whoever he might be, must expect some rough embrace from the worthy Pencroff!

The month of December ended, and with it the year 1867, during which the colonists of Lincoln Island had been so hardly tried. They entered upon the year 1868 with magnificent weather, a superb heat, a tropical temperature, which the sea-breeze happily came to cool. Herbert was coming to life again, and from his bed, placed near one of the windows of Granite House, he breathed in this healthful air, charged with saline emanations, which was restoring his health to him. He began to eat, and God knows what good little dishes, light and savory, Neb prepared for him!

“It was enough to make one wish to have been dying!” said Pencroff.

During all this period the convicts had not once shown themselves in the neighborhood of Granite House. Of Ayrton there was no news; and if the engineer and Herbert still kept some hope of finding him again, their companions no longer doubted that the unfortunate man had perished. However, this uncertainty could not last, and as soon as the lad was well, the expedition, the result of which must be so important, would be undertaken. But they must wait perhaps a month, for all the strength of the colony would not be too much to get the better of the convicts.

For the rest, Herbert was getting better and better. The congestion of the liver had disappeared, and the wounds might be considered as finally healed.

During this month of January important works were done on Prospect Plateau; but they consisted solely in saving what could be saved of the devastated crops, whether of wheat or of vegetables. The seeds and the plants were gathered, so as to furnish a new harvest for the coming half-season. As to raising again the buildings of the poultry-yard, the mill, and the stables, Smith preferred to wait. While he and his companions were in pursuit of the convicts, these might very well pay a new visit to the plateau, and they must not be given cause to take up again their trade of plunderers and incendiaries. When the island had been purged of these malefactors, they would see about rebuilding.

The young convalescent had begun to get up in the second half of the month of January, at first for one hour a day, then two, then three. His strength came back to him visibly, so vigorous was his constitution. He was eighteen years old then. He was tall, and promised to become a man of noble and handsome presence. From this time his convalescence, while it still required some care, and Doctor Spilett showed himself very strict, went on regularly.

Towards the end of the month Herbert was already walking over Prospect Plateau and the beach. Some sea-baths which he took in company with Pencroff and Neb did him the greatest good. Smith thought that he might now name the day of departure, which was fixed for the 15th of February following. The nights, very clear at that time of the year, would be favorable to the search which was to be made over the whole island.

The preparations required by this exploration were therefore begun, and they were to be considerable, for the colonists had sworn not to return to Granite House until their double object had been attained: on the one hand, to destroy the convicts and to find Ayrton again, if he still lived; on the other, to discover him who presided so effectually over the fortunes of the colony.

Of Lincoln Island the colonists knew thoroughly all the eastern coast from Claw Cape to the Mandible Capes, the vast Tadorn’s Fens, the neighborhood of Lake Grant, Jacamar Wood lying between the corral road and the Mercy, the courses of the Mercy and of Red Creek, and lastly the spurs of Mount Franklin, among which the corral had been established.

They had explored, but in an imperfect manner only, the vast shore of Washington Bay from Claw Cape to Reptile End, the wooded and marshy border of the west coast, and those interminable dunes which ended at the half-open mouth of Shark Gulf.

But they had in no way surveyed the large wooded portions which covered the Serpentine Peninsula, all the right of the Mercy, the left bank of Fall River, and the tangle of those spurs and counter-valleys which bore three-quarters of the base of Mount Franklin on the west, the north, and the east, where so many deep retreats no doubt existed. Consequently several thousands of acres of the island had still escaped their investigations.

It was therefore decided that the expedition should be carried across the Far West, so as to take in all the part lying on the right of the Mercy.

It would perhaps have been better to go first to the corral, where it was to be feared that the convicts had again taken refuge, either to pillage it or to establish themselves there. But either the devastation of the corral was an accomplished fact by now, and it was too late to prevent it, or the convicts had found it to their interest to entrench themselves there, and there would always be time to go and drive them out of their retreat.

So, after discussion, the first plan was kept, and the colonists resolved to make their way through the woods to Reptile End. They would travel with the axe, and would thus lay down the first line of a road which should put Granite House in communication with the end of the peninsula, over a length of sixteen to seventeen miles.

The cart was in perfect order. The onaggas, well rested, could make a long journey. Provisions, camp effects, a portable kitchen, and various utensils were loaded upon the cart, as well as the arms and ammunition, chosen with care from the now so complete arsenal of Granite House. But it must not be forgotten that the convicts were perhaps roaming the woods, and that in the midst of these thick forests a shot was quickly fired and received. Hence the necessity for the little band of colonists to keep close together, and not to separate under any pretext.

It was also decided that no one should remain at Granite House. Top and Jup themselves were to form part of the expedition. The inaccessible dwelling could guard itself.

The 14th of February, the eve of the departure, was a Sunday. It was given entirely to rest, and was sanctified by the thanksgivings which the colonists addressed to the Creator. Herbert, entirely cured, but still a little weak, was to have a place kept for him on the cart.

The next day, at daybreak, Smith took the necessary measures to put Granite House beyond the reach of any invasion. The ladders which had formerly served for the ascent were carried to the Chimneys and buried deep in the sand, so that they might serve on the return, for the drum of the elevator was taken down, and nothing was left of the apparatus. Pencroff remained last in Granite House to finish this task, and he came down from it by means of a rope, the double of which was held below, and which, once drawn down to the ground, left no communication whatever between the upper landing and the beach.

The weather was magnificent.

“A hot day getting ready!” said the reporter gaily.

“Pooh! Doctor Spilett,” answered Pencroff, “we shall walk under shelter of the trees, and shall not even catch sight of the sun!”

“Forward!” said the engineer.

The cart was waiting on the shore, before the Chimneys. The reporter had required that Herbert should take his place in it, at least during the first hours of the journey, and the lad had to submit to the prescriptions of his physician.

Neb placed himself at the head of the onaggas. Smith, the reporter, and the sailor went on in front. Top frisked about with a joyous air. Herbert had offered Jup a place in his vehicle, and Jup had accepted without ceremony. The moment of departure had come, and the little band set out.

The cart first turned the angle of the river’s mouth; then, after going up the left bank of the Mercy for a mile, it crossed the bridge at the end of which the road to Balloon Harbor began; and there the explorers, leaving that road on their left, began to plunge under the cover of those immense woods which formed the region of the Far West.

During the first two miles the trees, widely spaced, allowed the cart to move about freely; from time to time it was necessary to cut through some creepers and forests of brushwood, but no serious obstacle stopped the march of the colonists.

The thick boughs of the trees kept up a cool shade on the ground. Deodars, Douglas firs, casuarinas, banksias, gum-trees, dragon-trees, and other kinds already known, followed one another beyond the limits of sight. The world of birds usual to the island was to be found there complete: grouse, jacamars, pheasants, lories, and all the chattering family of cockatoos, paroquets, and parrots. Agoutis, kangaroos, and capybaras scampered among the grass; and all this recalled to the colonists the first excursions they had made on their arrival on the island.

“Nevertheless,” observed Smith, “I notice that these animals, both quadrupeds and birds, are more timid than formerly. These woods have therefore lately been traversed by the convicts, of whom we shall certainly find traces.”

And, in fact, in many places they could make out the more or less recent passage of a band of men: here, branches broken from the trees, perhaps with the object of marking the way; there, the ashes of an extinguished fire, and footprints which certain clayey portions of the ground had preserved. But, on the whole, there was nothing which appeared to belong to a settled encampment.

The engineer had recommended his companions to abstain from hunting. The reports of firearms might have given the alarm to the convicts, who were perhaps prowling in the forest. Moreover, the hunters would necessarily have been drawn to some distance from the cart, and it was strictly forbidden to walk alone.

In the second part of the day, about six miles from Granite House, the way became rather difficult. In order to pass certain thickets it was necessary to fell trees and make a road. Before entering upon them, Smith took care to send Top and Jup into these thick coppices, and they conscientiously performed their office. When the dog and the orang came back without having signalled anything, it was because there was nothing to fear, either on the part of the convicts or on the part of wild beasts, two sorts of individuals of the animal kingdom whom their ferocious instincts placed on the same level.

On the evening of this first day the colonists camped about nine miles from Granite House, on the bank of a little tributary of the Mercy, of whose existence they had been ignorant, and which must belong to the hydrographic system to which this soil owed its astonishing fertility.

They supped plentifully, for the colonists’ appetites were sharply whetted, and measures were taken that the night might pass without mishap. If the engineer had had to deal only with wild beasts, jaguars or others, he would simply have lighted fires around his encampment, which would have been enough to defend it; but the convicts would rather have been attracted than stopped by these flames, and in that case it was better to surround themselves with deep darkness.

The watch, besides, was strictly organized. Two of the colonists were to watch together, and it was agreed that every two hours they should be relieved by their comrades. Now, as Herbert, in spite of his protests, was excused from guard, Pencroff and Spilett on the one hand, and the engineer and Neb on the other, mounted guard by turns at the approaches to the camp.

For the rest, there were scarcely a few hours of night. The darkness was due rather to the thickness of the boughs than to the disappearance of the sun. The silence was hardly disturbed by the hoarse howlings of jaguars and the sneering of monkeys, which seemed particularly to irritate Master Jup.

The night passed without incident, and the next day, the 16th of February, the march, slow rather than toilsome, was resumed across the forest.

That day they could not cover more than six miles, for at every instant it was necessary to clear a road with the axe. Like true “settlers,” the colonists spared the large and fine trees, the felling of which would besides have cost them enormous labor, and they sacrificed the small ones. But the result was that the road took a direction far from straight, and was lengthened by numerous windings.

During this day Herbert discovered new kinds of trees, the presence of which had not yet been noted in the island, such as tree-ferns, with drooping fronds, which seemed to spread out like the waters of a basin, and carob-trees, the long pods of which the onaggas browsed greedily, and which furnished a sugary pulp of excellent taste. There, too, the colonists found again some magnificent kauris, standing in groups, the cylindrical trunks of which, crowned with a cone of verdure, rose to a height of two hundred feet. These were indeed those king-trees of New Zealand, as celebrated as the cedars of Lebanon.

As to the fauna, it presented no other specimens than those of which the hunters had had knowledge until then. However, they caught sight of, though without being able to approach, a pair of those large birds which are peculiar to Australia, a sort of cassowary, which are called emus, and which, five feet high and brown of plumage, belong to the order of waders. Top darted after them with all the speed of his four legs, but the cassowaries easily outdistanced him, so prodigious was their swiftness.

As to the traces left by the convicts in the forest, some more were found. Near a fire which appeared to have been recently put out, the colonists noticed footprints, which were observed with extreme attention. By measuring them one after the other, according to their length and their breadth, they easily made out the marks of the feet of five men. The five convicts had evidently camped on this spot; but—and this was the object of so minute an examination!—no sixth footprint could be discovered, which in that case would have been that of the foot of Ayrton.

“Ayrton was not with them!” said Herbert.

“No,” answered Pencroff, “and if he was not with them, it is because those wretches had already killed him! But have these rascals no den, then, where one could go and track them down like tigers!”

“No,” answered the reporter. “It is more probable that they are going at random, and it is their interest to wander so until the time when they shall be masters of the island.”

“Masters of the island!” cried the sailor. “Masters of the island!” he repeated, and his voice was choked as if an iron grip had seized him by the throat. Then, in a calmer tone:

“Do you know, Mr. Smith,” said he, “what ball I have rammed into my gun?”

“No, Pencroff!”

“It is the ball that went through Herbert’s breast, and I promise you that one will not miss its mark!”

But these just reprisals could not give back life to Ayrton, and from this examination of the footprints left on the ground they must, alas! conclude that there was no longer any hope to be kept of ever seeing him again!

That evening the camp was established fourteen miles from Granite House, and Smith reckoned that it could not be more than five miles from Reptile End.

And, in fact, the next day the end of the peninsula was reached, and the forest crossed throughout its whole length; but no sign had allowed them to find the retreat where the convicts had taken refuge, nor that, not less secret, which gave shelter to the mysterious unknown.
