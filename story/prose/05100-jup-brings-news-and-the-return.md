# Jup Brings News and the Return (final)

CHAPTER LI.

NO NEWS OF NEB—A PROPOSAL OF PENCROFF AND THE REPORTER WHICH IS NOT ACCEPTED—SOME SALLIES OF SPILETT—A RAG OF CLOTH—A MESSAGE—HASTY DEPARTURE—ARRIVAL ON PROSPECT PLATEAU.

The convalescence of the young patient went on regularly. One thing only was now to be desired, that his state should allow them to bring him back to Granite House. However well arranged and provisioned the dwelling at the corral might be, the comfort of the wholesome granite dwelling could not be found there. Besides, it did not offer the same security, and its inmates, in spite of their watchfulness, were always there under the threat of some shot from the convicts. Yonder, on the contrary, in the midst of that impregnable and inaccessible mass, they would have nothing to dread, and every attempt against their persons must necessarily fail. They therefore waited impatiently for the moment when Herbert might be moved without danger to his wound, and they were determined to make the removal, although the communications through Jacamar Wood were very difficult.

There was no news of Neb, but no uneasiness on his account. The courageous negro, well entrenched in the depths of Granite House, would not let himself be surprised. Top had not been sent back to him, and it had seemed useless to expose the faithful dog to some shot which would have deprived the colonists of their most useful auxiliary.

They waited, therefore, but the colonists were eager to be reunited at Granite House. It pained the engineer to see his forces divided, for that was playing the game of the pirates. Since the disappearance of Ayrton they were only four against five, for Herbert could not yet be counted. And this was not the least care of the brave boy, who well understood the trouble of which he was the cause!

The question of how, in the present conditions, they should act against the convicts, was thoroughly discussed on the 29th of November between Smith, Spilett, and Pencroff, at a moment when Herbert, dozing, could not hear them.

"My friends," said the reporter, after Neb had been spoken of, and the impossibility of communicating with him, "I believe, as you do, that to venture on the road to the corral would be to risk receiving a shot without being able to return it. But do you not think that what we ought to do now would be to give open chase to these wretches?"

"That is what I was thinking of," answered Pencroff. "We have not come, I suppose, to fearing a bullet, and, for my part, if Mr. Smith approves, I am ready to throw myself upon the forest! What the devil! One man is worth another!"

"But is he worth five?" asked the engineer.

"I will join Pencroff," answered the reporter, "and both of us, well armed, and accompanied by Top—"

"My dear Spilett, and you, Pencroff," returned Smith, "let us reason coolly. If the convicts were lodged in one spot of the island, if that spot were known to us, and if it were only a question of driving them out of it, I could understand a direct attack. But is there not reason to fear, on the contrary, that they would be sure of firing the first shot?"

"Eh, Mr. Smith," cried Pencroff, "a bullet does not always go to its mark!"

"The one which struck Herbert did not go astray, Pencroff," replied the engineer. "Besides, observe that if you both left the corral, I should remain here alone to defend it. Will you answer for it that the convicts will not see you leave it, that they will not let you get deep into the forest, and that they will not attack it during your absence, knowing that there will be no one here but a wounded boy and a man?"

"You are right, Mr. Smith," answered Pencroff, whose breast was swelling with a smothered anger, "you are right. They will do everything to retake the corral, which they know to be well stocked! And alone you could not hold out against them! Ah! if we were at Granite House!"

"If we were at Granite House," answered the engineer, "the situation would be very different! There I should not fear to leave Herbert with one of us, and the other three would go and search the forests of the island. But we are at the corral, and it is fitting to remain here until the moment when we can all leave it together!"

There was nothing to answer to the reasoning of Smith, and his companions well understood it.

"If only Ayrton were still one of us!" said Spilett. "Poor man! His return to social life will have been of short duration!"

"If he is dead?—" added Pencroff, in a rather singular tone.

"Do you hope, then, Pencroff, that these rascals have spared him?" asked Spilett.

"Yes! if they have had an interest in doing so!"

"What! you would suppose that Ayrton, finding his old accomplices again, forgetting all that he owes us—"

"Who knows?" answered the sailor, who did not hazard this unpleasant supposition without hesitating.

"Pencroff," said Smith, taking the arm of the sailor, "that is a bad thought you have there, and you would grieve me much if you persisted in speaking so! I answer for the fidelity of Ayrton!"

"And so do I," added the reporter quickly.

"Yes—yes!—Mr. Smith—I am wrong," answered Pencroff. "It is a bad thought, indeed, that I had there, and nothing justifies it! But what would you have? I have not quite got my head about me. This imprisonment at the corral weighs upon me horribly, and I have never been so excited as I am now!"

"Be patient, Pencroff," answered the engineer. "How long will it be, my dear Spilett, before you think Herbert can be carried to Granite House?"

"That is difficult to say, Cyrus," answered the reporter, "for an imprudence might bring fatal consequences. But at least his convalescence is going on regularly, and if in eight days from this his strength has come back to him, well, we shall see!"

Eight days! That put off the return to Granite House till the first days of December only.

At this time the spring was already two months old. The weather was fine, and the heat was beginning to be great. The forests of the island were in full leaf, and the time was approaching when the usual harvests ought to be gathered. The return to Prospect Plateau would therefore be followed by great agricultural labors, which would be interrupted only by the expedition planned in the island.

It may be understood, then, how much this seclusion at the corral must injure the colonists. But if they were obliged to bow before necessity, they did not do so without impatience.

Once or twice the reporter ventured out on the road and made the round of the palisaded enclosure. Top accompanied him, and Spilett, his carbine cocked, was ready for any event.

He had no bad encounter and found no suspicious trace. His dog would have warned him of any danger, and, as Top did not bark, it might be concluded that there was nothing to fear, for the moment at least, and that the convicts were occupied in another part of the island.

However, on his second sortie, on the 27th of November, Spilett, who had ventured a quarter of a mile under the trees, to the south of the mountain, noticed that Top scented something. The dog had no longer his indifferent gait; he went and came, ferreting in the grass and the brushwood, as if his scent had revealed to him some suspicious object.

Spilett followed Top, encouraged him, urged him on with his voice, all the while keeping his eye on the watch, his carbine at his shoulder, and taking advantage of the shelter of the trees to cover himself. It was unlikely that Top had scented the presence of a man, for in that case he would have announced it by half-stifled barks and a sort of dull anger. Now, since he gave no growl, it was because the danger was neither near nor at hand.

About five minutes passed thus, Top ferreting, the reporter following him with caution, when, all at once, the dog rushed towards a thick bush and drew out of it a rag of cloth.

It was a piece of clothing, stained and torn, which Spilett immediately brought back to the corral.

There the colonists examined it, and they recognized that it was a piece of the jacket of Ayrton, a piece of that felt manufactured only in the workshop at Granite House.

"You see, Pencroff," observed Smith, "there has been resistance on the part of the unfortunate Ayrton. The convicts have dragged him off against his will! Do you still doubt his honesty?"

"No, Mr. Smith," answered the sailor, "and it is a long time since I got over my moment's mistrust! But there is, it seems to me, a consequence to be drawn from this fact."

"What is it?" asked the reporter.

"It is that Ayrton was not killed at the corral! It is that they dragged him off alive, since he resisted! Now, perhaps he is still alive!"

"Perhaps, indeed," answered the engineer, who remained thoughtful.

Here was a hope, to which the companions of Ayrton might cling again. In fact, they had been obliged to believe that, surprised at the corral, Ayrton had fallen under some bullet, as Herbert had fallen. But if the convicts had not killed him at once, if they had carried him off alive to some other part of the island, might it not be admitted that he was still their prisoner? Perhaps even one of them had found again in Ayrton an old comrade of Australia, Ben Joyce, the chief of the escaped convicts? And who knows whether they had not conceived the impossible hope of bringing Ayrton back to them! He would have been so useful to them, if they could have made a traitor of him!—

This incident was therefore favorably interpreted at the corral, and it no longer seemed impossible that Ayrton should be found again. On his side, if he were only a prisoner, Ayrton would no doubt do everything to escape from the hands of these bandits, and he would be a powerful auxiliary to the colonists!

"In any case," observed Spilett, "if, by good luck, Ayrton succeeds in escaping, it is to Granite House that he will go directly, for he does not know of the attempt at assassination of which Herbert has been the victim, and, consequently, he cannot believe that we are imprisoned at the corral."

"Ah! I wish he were there, at Granite House!" cried Pencroff, "and that we were there too! For, after all, if the rascals can attempt nothing against our dwelling, at least they can sack the plateau, our plantations, our poultry-yard!"

Pencroff had become a true farmer, attached with all his heart to his crops. But it must be said that Herbert was more impatient than any of them to return to Granite House, for he knew how necessary the presence of the colonists was there. And it was he who kept them at the corral! So this one idea occupied his mind: to leave the corral, to leave it in spite of all! He believed he could bear the removal to Granite House. He declared that his strength would come back more quickly in his room, with the air and the sight of the sea!

Several times he pressed Spilett, but the latter, fearing, with reason, that the wounds of Herbert, badly healed, might open again on the way, did not give the order to start.

However, an incident occurred which led Smith and his two friends to yield to the young boy's wishes, and God knows what grief and remorse this determination might cause them!

It was the 29th of November. It was seven o'clock in the morning. The three colonists were talking in Herbert's room, when they heard Top barking sharply.

Smith, Pencroff, and Spilett seized their guns, always ready to fire, and went out of the house.

Top, having run to the foot of the palisaded enclosure, was jumping and barking, but it was from contentment, not anger.

"Some one is coming!"

"Yes!"

"It is not an enemy!"

"Neb, perhaps?"

"Or Ayrton?"

Hardly had these words been exchanged between the engineer and his two companions, when a body bounded over the palisade and fell again on the ground of the corral.

It was Jup, Master Jup in person, to whom Top gave a true friend's welcome!

"Jup!" cried Pencroff.

"It is Neb who sends him to us!" said the reporter.

"Then," answered the engineer, "he must have some note about him."

Pencroff rushed towards the orang. Evidently, if Neb had some important fact to make known to his master, he could not employ a surer or more rapid messenger, who could pass where neither the colonists nor even Top himself might perhaps have been able to do so.

Smith had not been mistaken. Round the neck of Jup hung a little bag, and in this bag was a note traced by the hand of Neb.

Let the despair of Smith and his companions be judged, when they read these words:—

"Friday, 6 A.M.

"Plateau invaded by the convicts!

"NEB."

They looked at one another without uttering a word, then they went back into the house. What were they to do? The convicts on Prospect Plateau, that was disaster, devastation, ruin!

Herbert, on seeing the engineer, the reporter, and Pencroff come in again, understood that the situation had just grown worse, and when he saw Jup, he no longer doubted that some misfortune threatened Granite House.

"Mr. Smith," said he, "I wish to go. I can bear the journey! I wish to go!"

Spilett approached Herbert. Then, after having looked at him—

"Let us go, then!" said he.

The question was soon decided, whether Herbert should be carried on a litter or in the wagon which had been brought to the corral by Ayrton. The litter would have had gentler motions for the wounded lad, but it required two bearers; that is to say, two guns would be wanting to the defense, if an attack should be made on the way.

Could they not, on the contrary, by employing the wagon, leave every arm free? Was it, then, impossible to place in it the mattresses on which Herbert lay, and to advance with so much precaution that every jolt should be spared him? It could be done.

The wagon was brought. Pencroff harnessed the onagga to it. Smith and the reporter raised the mattresses of Herbert, and laid them on the bottom of the wagon between the two side-rails.

The weather was fine. Bright rays of sunshine glided through the trees.

"Are the arms ready?" asked Smith.

They were. The engineer and Pencroff, each armed with a double-barrelled gun, and Spilett, holding his carbine, had nothing more to do but to start.

"Are you comfortable, Herbert?" asked the engineer.

"Ah! Mr. Smith," answered the boy, "do not be uneasy, I shall not die on the road!"

While he spoke thus, it could be seen that the poor boy was calling up all his energy, and that, by a supreme effort of will, he was holding back his strength, which was ready to die away.

The engineer felt his heart painfully wrung. He still hesitated to give the signal for departure. But that would have been to drive Herbert to despair, perhaps to kill him.

"Forward!" said Smith.

The gate of the corral was opened. Jup and Top, who knew when to be silent, rushed on ahead. The wagon went out, the gate was shut again, and the onagga, led by Pencroff, advanced at a slow pace.

Certainly it would have been better to take some other road than that which led directly from the corral to Granite House, but the wagon would have had great difficulty in moving under the trees. It was necessary, then, to follow this way, although it must be known to the convicts.

Smith and Spilett walked one on each side of the wagon, ready to answer any attack. However, it was not probable that the convicts had yet left Prospect Plateau. The note of Neb had evidently been written and sent as soon as the convicts had shown themselves there. Now, this note was dated six o'clock in the morning, and the active orang, accustomed to come frequently to the corral, had taken scarcely three quarters of an hour to cross the five miles which separated it from Granite House. The road, then, must be safe at this moment; and if there were any firing to be done, it would probably be only on the approaches to Granite House.

However, the colonists kept strictly on their guard. Top and Jup, the latter armed with his stick, now in front, now beating the wood at the sides of the road, signalled no danger.

The wagon advanced slowly, under the guidance of Pencroff. It had left the corral at half-past seven. An hour after, four miles out of the five had been passed over, without any incident having occurred.

The road was deserted, like all that part of Jacamar Wood which stretched between the Mercy and the lake. No alarm took place. The thickets seemed as deserted as on the day when the colonists landed on the island.

They were approaching the plateau. One mile more, and they would see the little bridge over Glycerine Creek. Smith did not doubt that this bridge was in its place, whether the convicts had entered by that spot, or whether, after having crossed one of the streams which shut in the enclosure, they had taken the precaution of lowering it, so as to keep a retreat open for themselves.

At last the opening through the last trees allowed the sea horizon to be seen. But the wagon continued on its way, for none of its defenders could think of leaving it.

At that moment Pencroff stopped the onagga, and in a terrible voice:—

"Ah! the wretches!" he cried.

And with his hand he pointed to a thick smoke which was whirling above the mill, the stables, and the buildings of the poultry-yard.

A man was moving about in the midst of these vapors.

It was Neb.

His companions uttered a cry. He heard them, and ran towards them—

The convicts had left the plateau about half an hour before, after having devastated it!

"And Mr. Herbert?" cried Neb.

Spilett returned to the wagon at this moment.

Herbert had lost consciousness!
