# The Night on the Islet (draft 1)

CHAPTER III.

FIVE O’CLOCK IN THE AFTERNOON—THE LOST ONE—THE DESPAIR OF NEB—SEARCH TO THE NORTHWARD—THE ISLET—A SAD NIGHT OF ANGUISH—THE FOG OF THE MORNING—NEB SWIMMING—SIGHT OF THE LAND—FORDING THE CHANNEL.

The engineer, through the meshes of the net, which had given way, had been swept away by a wave. His dog had disappeared at the same time. The faithful animal had of its own accord sprung to the rescue of its master.

“Forward!” cried the reporter, and all four, Gideon Spilett, Herbert, Pencroff, and Neb, forgetting exhaustion and fatigue, began their search. Poor Neb wept with rage and despair at the thought of having lost all that he loved in the world.

Not more than two minutes had passed between the moment that Smith had disappeared, and the instant of his companions landing. They were, therefore, hopeful of being in time to rescue him.

“Hunt, hunt for him!” cried Neb.

“Yes, Neb, and we will find him!” replied Spilett.

“Alive?”

“Alive!”

“Can he swim?” demanded Pencroff.

“Oh, yes,” responded Neb. “And, besides, Top is with him—”

The sailor, hearing the roaring of the sea, shook his head.

It was at a point northward from this shore, and about half a mile from the place where the castaways had landed, that the engineer had disappeared, and if he had been able to reach the nearest point of the shore, that point could be at most half a mile distant.

It was nearly 6 o’clock. The fog had risen and made the night very dark. The castaways followed northward along the east coast of that land upon which chance had thrown them. A land unknown, whose geographical situation they could not even guess. They walked upon a sandy soil, mixed with stones, seemingly destitute of any kind of vegetation. The ground, very uneven and very rough, seemed in certain places to be riddled with small holes, making the march very painful. From these holes, great, heavy-flying birds rushed forth every moment, fleeing in all directions, unseen in the darkness. Others, more active, rose in flocks, and fled away like the clouds. The sailor thought he recognized gulls and sea-mews, whose sharp cries contended with the raging of the sea.

From time to time the castaways would stop and call loudly, listening for an answering voice from the ocean. They must have thought, too, that if they were near the place where the engineer might have been washed ashore, and he had been unable to give any sign of life, at least the barking of the dog Top would have been heard. But no sound was distinguishable above the roaring of the waves and the rattle of the surf. Then the little party would resume their march, searching the smallest windings of the shore.

After a walk of twenty minutes the four castaways were suddenly stopped by a foaming line of breakers. The solid ground failed them. They found themselves upon the extremity of a sharp point upon which the sea broke with fury.

“This is a promontory,” said the sailor, “and it will be necessary to turn back, keeping to the right in order to gain the main land.”

“But if he is there!” cried Neb, pointing towards the ocean, whose enormous waves showed white through the gloom.

“Well, let us call again.”

And all together, uniting their voices, uttered a vigorous cry, but without response. They waited for a lull, and tried once more. And again there was no answer.

Then the castaways turned back, following the opposite side of the promontory over ground equally sandy and rocky. However, Pencroff observed that the shore was bolder, that the land rose, and he supposed that it must join, by a rather long slope, a high coast whose mass was dimly visible through the darkness. The birds were less numerous on this shore. The sea also seemed less surging and less noisy, and it was even noticeable that the agitation of the waves was sensibly subsiding. They hardly heard the sound of the surf, and doubtless, this side of the promontory formed a semi-circular bay, protected by its sharp point from the long roll of the sea.

But by following this direction they were walking towards the south, which was going away from that place where Smith might have landed. After a tramp of a mile and a half, the shore still presented no curve which would permit of a return towards the north. It was evident that this promontory, the point of which they had turned, must be joined to the mainland. The castaways, although their strength was exhausted, pushed on courageously, hoping each moment to find a sudden turn which would take them in their first direction. What, then, was their disappointment when, after having walked about two miles, they found themselves again arrested by the sea, upon a rather high point of slippery rocks.

“We are on an islet,” exclaimed Pencroff; “and we have measured it from end to end!”

The words of the sailor were true. The castaways had been thrown, not upon a continent, nor even upon an island, but upon an islet not more than two miles long, and of evidently inconsiderable breadth.

This arid islet, strewn with stones, without vegetation, desolate refuge of a few sea-birds, did it belong to a more important archipelago? They could not tell. The party in the balloon, when from their basket they saw the land through the mists, had not been able sufficiently to determine its size. But Pencroff, with the eyes of a sailor accustomed to piercing the gloom, thought, at the moment, that he could distinguish in the west confused masses, which indicated a high coast. But at this time they were unable, on account of the obscurity, to determine to what system, whether simple or complex, the islet belonged. They were unable to get off, as the sea surrounded them, and it was necessary to wait until the next day to search for the engineer; who, alas! had made no cry to signal his presence.

“The silence of Cyrus proves nothing,” said the reporter. “He may have fainted, or be wounded, and unable to reply for the moment, but let us not despair.”

The reporter then suggested the idea of lighting a fire upon some point of the islet, which would serve as a signal for the engineer. But they searched in vain for wood or dry branches. Sand and stones were all they found.

One can understand the grief of Neb and his companions, who were strongly attached to that intrepid Cyrus Smith. It was too evident that they could not help him now, and that they must wait till day. Either the engineer had been able to save himself alone, and had already found refuge upon some point of the coast, or he was lost forever! The hours were long and dreadful, the cold was intense, and the castaways suffered cruelly, but they scarcely realized it. They did not even think of taking a moment’s rest. Forgetting themselves for their chief, hoping, wishing to hope, they moved back and forth upon that arid islet, constantly returning to the northern end, where they would be closest to the place of the catastrophe. They listened, they shouted, they tried to catch some last call, and their hallooes must have sounded far into the distance, for a certain calm then reigned in the air, and the roar of the surf was beginning to fall with the swell.

Once one of Neb’s cries even seemed to be repeated by an echo; and Herbert made Pencroff notice it, saying:—“That would prove that there is a coast not far to the west.”

The sailor nodded; besides, his eyes could not deceive him. If he had made out land, however faintly, then land was there. But this distant echo was the only answer to the cries of Neb, and over all the east side of the islet the immensity remained silent. Meanwhile the sky was clearing slowly. Towards midnight, some stars shone out, and, had the engineer been there with his companions, he would have noticed that these stars did not belong to the northern hemisphere. The pole star was not visible in this new horizon, the constellations in the zenith were not such as he had been accustomed to observe in the northern part of the New World, and the Southern Cross shone resplendent at the south pole of the heavens.

The night passed; and towards 5 o’clock in the morning, the 25th of March, the upper heavens began faintly to brighten, though the horizon remained obscure; until with the first rays of day, a fog rose from the sea, so dense that the eye could scarcely penetrate twenty paces into its depths, and unrolled itself in great, heavy-moving masses. This was unfortunate, as the castaways were unable to distinguish anything about them. While the gaze of Neb and the reporter was directed towards the sea, the sailor and Herbert searched for the land in the west; but not a scrap of land was visible.

“Never mind,” said Pencroff, “if I do not see the land, I feel it; it is there,—there,—just as sure as that we are no longer in Richmond!”

But the fog, which was nothing more than a fine-weather mist, soon rose. A clear sun warmed its upper layers, its heat penetrating to the surface of the islet. At half-past 6, three quarters of an hour after sunrise, the mist was growing more transparent. Though thickening overhead, it dissolved below, and soon all the islet appeared, as if it had come down from a cloud. Then the sea showed itself in a circular plane, limitless towards the east, but bounded on the west by a high and abrupt coast.

Yes, the land was there! There, safety was at least provisionally assured. The islet and the main land were separated by a channel half a mile wide, through which an extremely rapid current rushed noisily. Into this current one of the party, consulting only his heart, without taking counsel of his companions, without even saying a single word, at once precipitated himself. It was Neb. He was anxious to be upon that coast and to be pushing forward towards the north. No one could keep him back. Pencroff called to him in vain. The reporter prepared to follow, but the sailor ran to him, exclaiming:—

“Do you wish to cross this channel?”

“I am,” replied Spilett.

“Well, then, wait, believe me. Neb can rescue his master alone. If we throw ourselves into the channel we are in danger of being carried out to sea by the current, which is extremely violent. Now, if I am not mistaken it is caused by the ebb. You see the tide is going down on the sand. Let us have patience, then, and at low water it is possible that we may find a ford.”

“You are right,” answered the reporter; “we will keep together as much as possible.”

Meantime, Neb was swimming vigorously in a diagonal direction, against the current; his black shoulders were seen rising with each stroke. He was drifting with extreme swiftness, but he was gaining towards the other shore. It took him more than half an hour to cross the half mile which separated the islet from the mainland, and when he reached the other side it was at a place several thousand feet from the point opposite to that which he had left.

Neb, having landed at the base of a high granite wall, shook himself vigorously; then, running, he soon disappeared behind a point of rocks projecting into the sea, about abreast of the northern end of the islet.

Neb’s companions had watched with anxiety his daring attempt, and, when he was out of sight, they fixed their eyes upon that land from which they were going to demand refuge. They ate some of the shellfish which they found upon the sands; it was a poor meal, but then it was better than nothing.

The opposite coast formed an immense bay, terminated to the south by a very sharp point bare of all vegetation, and having a most forbidding aspect. This point joined the shore in a rather capricious outline, and was abutted by high granite rocks. Towards the north, on the contrary, the bay widened, with a shore more rounded, extending from the southwest to the northeast, and ending in a narrow cape. Between these two points, the distance must have been about eight miles. A half mile from the shore the islet occupied a narrow strip of the sea, and resembled an enormous whale, of which it represented the carcass, greatly enlarged. Its greatest width could not have been greater than a quarter of a mile.

Before the islet, the shore began with a sandy beach strewn with black rocks, at this moment beginning to appear above the receding tide. Beyond this rose, like a curtain, a perpendicular granite wall, at least 300 feet high and terminated by a ragged edge. This extended for three miles, ending abruptly on the right in a smooth face, as if cut by the hand of man. To the left on the contrary, above the promontory, this kind of irregular cliff, breaking into prismatic fragments, and composed of heaped-up rocks and fallen stones, sank by a long slope and gradually mingled with the rocks of the southern point.

Upon the upper level of the coast not a tree was visible. It was a bare table-land, like that which overlooks Cape Town, at the Cape of Good Hope, but of smaller proportions. At least so it appeared from the islet. To the right, however, and back of the smooth face of rock, some verdure appeared. The confused massing of large trees was easily distinguishable extending far as the eye could reach. This verdure gladdened the sight, much saddened by the harsh lines of the granite face. Finally, back of and above the plateau, distant towards the northwest at least seven miles, shone a white summit, reflecting the sun’s rays. It was the snowy cap of some distant mountain.

It was not possible at present to say whether this land was an island or part of a continent; but at the sight of the convulsed rocks heaped together on the left a geologist would not have hesitated to ascribe to them a volcanic origin, as they were incontestably the product of plutonic action.

Gideon Spilett, Pencroff, and Herbert looked earnestly upon this land where they were to live, perhaps for long years; upon which, if it were out of the track of ships, they would even die!

“Well,” demanded Herbert, “what do you think of it, Pencroff?”

“Well,” replied the sailor, “there’s good and bad in it, as with everything else. We shall see. But look, the ebb is making itself felt. In three hours we will try the passage, and once over there, we will try to shift for ourselves and to find Mr. Smith!”

Pencroff was not wrong in his predictions. Three hours later, at low tide, the greater part of the sandy bed of the channel was bare. A narrow channel, which would doubtless be easily crossed, was all that separated the islet from the shore. And, in fact, about 10 o’clock, Spilett and his two companions, stripped of their clothing, which they carried in packages on their heads, waded through the water, which was nowhere more than five feet deep. Herbert, where the water was too deep, swam like a fish, acquitting himself well; and all arrived without difficulty at the other shore. There, the sun having quickly dried them, they put on their clothes, which had not touched the water, and took counsel together.
