# The Chimneys and the Raft of Wood (draft 1)

CHAPTER IV.

THE LITHODOMES—THE MOUTH OF THE RIVER—THE “CHIMNEYS”—CONTINUATION OF THE SEARCH—THE FOREST OF EVERGREENS—GETTING FIREWOOD—WAITING FOR THE TIDE—ON TOP OF THE CLIFF—THE TIMBER-FLOAT—THE RETURN TO THE COAST.

Presently the reporter told the sailor to wait just where he was until he should come back, and without losing a moment, he walked back along the coast in the direction which the negro Neb had taken some hours before, and disappeared quickly around a turn in the shore, so impatient was he for news of the engineer.

Herbert wished to go with him.

“Stay, my boy,” said the sailor. “We must pitch our camp, and see whether we can find something to eat more solid than shellfish. Our friends will need food when they come back. Every one to his task.”

“I am ready, Pencroff,” said Herbert.

“Good,” said the sailor, “that will do. Let us set to work methodically. We are tired, cold, and hungry: we need shelter, fire, and food. There is plenty of wood in the forest, and we can get eggs from the nests; but we must find a house.”

“Well,” said Herbert, “I will look for a cave in these rocks, and I shall certainly find some hole in which we can stow ourselves.”

“Right,” said Pencroff; “let us start at once, my boy.”

They both walked along the base of the enormous wall, on the strand left widely bare by the receding waves. But instead of going northwards, they turned to the south. Pencroff had noticed, some hundreds of paces below the place where they had been thrown ashore, a narrow inlet in the coast, which he thought must be the mouth of a river or of a brook. Now it was important to pitch the camp in the neighborhood of fresh water; and, besides, it was not impossible that the current had carried Smith in that direction.

The lofty wall, as has been said, rose 300 feet, but it was massive throughout, and even at its base, scarcely washed by the sea, there was not the least cleft which might serve as a temporary abode. It was a perpendicular wall of very hard granite, never corroded by the waves. About the summit hovered a host of aquatic birds, mainly various species of the web-footed tribe, with long, narrow, pointed beaks. Very noisy, they cared little for the presence of man, who, for the first time no doubt, was thus disturbing their solitude. Among these web-footed birds Pencroff recognized several skuas, a kind of gull sometimes called stercoraries, and also some small voracious sea-mews, which nested in the crevices of the granite. A shot into the midst of the flock would have brought down a great number; but to fire a shot one must have a gun, and neither Pencroff nor Herbert had one. Besides, these sea-mews and skuas are barely eatable, and even their eggs have a detestable flavor.

Meanwhile Herbert, who was now to the left, soon noticed some rocks thickly strewn with sea weed, which would evidently be submerged again in a few hours. On them, among the slippery wrack, lay hosts of bivalves, not to be disdained by hungry men. Herbert called to Pencroff, who came running to him.

“Ah! they are mussels!” cried the sailor. “Here is something to make up for the eggs we have not got!”

“They are not mussels,” said young Herbert, who was carefully examining the mollusks attached to the rocks, “they are lithodomes.”

“Can we eat them?” said Pencroff.

“Certainly.”

“Then let us eat some lithodomes.”

The sailor could rely on Herbert, who was very well versed in Natural History, and had always had a real passion for that science. He owed his acquaintance with this study in great part to his father, who had entered him in the classes of the best professors in Boston, where the child’s industry and intelligence had endeared him to them. And so his instincts as a naturalist were to be turned to account more than once afterwards, and in this, his first trial, he made no mistake.

These lithodomes were oblong shell-fish, adhering in clusters, and very firmly, to the rocks. They belonged to that species of boring mollusk which can perforate a hole in the hardest stone, and whose shell has the peculiarity of being rounded at both ends, which is not observed in the common mussel.

Pencroff and Herbert made a good meal of these lithodomes, which lay gaping in the sun. They ate them like oysters, and found them of a strongly peppery flavor, which left them no regret at having neither pepper nor condiments of any kind.

Their hunger was allayed for the moment, but their thirst was increased by the spicy flavor of the mollusks. The thing now was to find fresh water, which was not likely to fail them in a region so capriciously uneven. Pencroff and Herbert, after having taken the precaution to fill their pockets and handkerchiefs with lithodomes, regained the foot of the high land.

Two hundred paces further on they reached the inlet, through which, as Pencroff had surmised, a little river was flowing with full current. Here the rocky wall seemed to have been torn asunder by some violent plutonic convulsion. At its base lay a little creek, whose further end formed a rather acute angle. The water in this place was 100 feet across, while the banks on either side were scarcely 20 feet broad. The river buried itself almost directly between the two walls of granite, which began to decline as one went up stream; then it turned abruptly, and disappeared beneath a copse half a mile off.

“Here is water!” said Pencroff, “and over there is wood! Well, Herbert, now we only want the house!”

The river water was clear. The sailor found that at this moment of the tide, that is to say at low water, when the rising sea did not reach it, the water was fresh. When this important point had been settled, Herbert looked for some cave which might give them shelter, but it was in vain. Everywhere the wall was smooth, flat, and perpendicular.

However, at the very mouth of the watercourse, and above high-water mark, the detritus had formed, not a grotto, but a pile of enormous rocks, such as are often met with in granitic countries, and which are called Chimneys.

Pencroff and Herbert went some way in between the rocks, into those sandy corridors, which did not want for light, since it came in through the gaps between the masses of granite, some of which only kept their equilibrium by a miracle. But with the light the wind came in,—a regular draught of the corridors,—and with the wind the piercing cold of the outer air. Still, the sailor thought that by blocking up certain parts of these corridors, and stopping up some of the openings with a mixture of stones and sand, the Chimneys might be rendered habitable. The plan of the Chimneys resembled the typographical sign &, which stands for et cætera in abbreviation; and by cutting off the upper curve of the sign, through which the south and the west wind rushed in, they could succeed without doubt in utilizing its lower portion.

“This is just what we want,” said Pencroff, “and if we ever see Mr. Smith again, he will know how to take advantage of this labyrinth.”

“We shall see him again, Pencroff,” cried Herbert, “and when he comes back he must find here a home that is tolerably comfortable. We can make this so if we can build a fireplace in the left corridor with an opening for the smoke.”

“That we can do, my boy,” answered the sailor, “and these Chimneys—it was the name which Pencroff kept for this temporary dwelling—will just serve our purpose. But first we must get together some firing. I fancy wood will not come amiss, too, in blocking up these holes through which the devil is blowing his trumpet!”

Herbert and Pencroff left the Chimneys, and turning the angle, walked up the left bank of the river, whose current was rather rapid, and carried down some dead wood. The rising tide, which could already be felt, would drive it back with force to a considerable distance. “Why not utilize this flux and reflux,” thought the sailor, “in the carriage of heavy objects?”

After a quarter of an hour’s walk, the two reached the elbow which the river made in turning to the left. From this point onward it flowed through a forest of magnificent trees, which had preserved their verdure in spite of the advanced season; for they belonged to that great cone-bearing family indigenous everywhere, from northern climates to the tropics. The young naturalist recognized especially the “deodara,” so numerous in the Himalayan region, with their agreeable perfume. Among these fine trees grew clusters of pines, whose opaque parasols spread wide. Among the tall grass Pencroff felt dry branches crush under his foot, crackling like fireworks.

“Good, my boy,” said the sailor to Herbert, “I may not know the name of these trees, but at least I know they belong to the genus firewood, and just now that’s the only one for us!”

“Let us get in our stock!” answered Herbert, who set to work at once.

It was an easy matter to gather the firewood. They did not need even to strip the trees; enormous quantities of dead wood lay at their feet. But if fuel was not wanting, the means of carrying it left much to be desired. This wood, being very dry, would burn rapidly, and so they would need a large supply at the Chimneys; and what two men could carry would not be enough. Herbert pointed this out.

“Ah! my boy,” said the sailor, “there must be some way of carrying this wood. There’s a way to do everything! If we had a car or a boat it would be too easy.”

“But we have the river!” said Herbert.

“Exactly,” said Pencroff. “The river shall be for us a road that walks by itself. Timber-floats were not invented for nothing.”

“Only, our road is walking just now in the wrong direction,” said Herbert, “since the tide is coming up from the sea.”

“We have only to wait for the turn of tide,” answered the sailor, “and then the tide itself will carry our fuel to the Chimneys. Let us get our float ready meanwhile.”

The sailor, followed by Herbert, walked towards the angle which the edge of the forest made with the river, each carrying, in proportion to his strength, a load of wood tied up in fagots. On the bank, too, lay quantities of dead boughs, among grass which the foot of man had probably never pressed before. Pencroff began to get ready his float.

In an eddy caused by an angle of the shore, which broke the flow of the current, they set afloat the larger pieces of wood, bound together by dry liana stems so as to form a sort of raft. On this raft they piled all the wood they had gathered, which would have been a load for twenty men at least. In an hour their work was finished, and the float was moored to the bank to wait for the turn of the tide. There were some hours to fill, and Pencroff and Herbert, by common consent, resolved to employ them in gaining a more extended view of the country from the higher plateau. Two hundred paces behind the angle of the river, the wall terminating in irregular masses of rocks, sloped away gently to the edge of the forest. The two climbed this natural staircase, and thanks to the vigor of their legs soon attained the summit, and posted themselves at the angle overlooking the mouth of the river.

Their first look was at that ocean over which they had been so frightfully swept. They beheld with emotion the northern part of the coast, the scene of the catastrophe, and of Smith’s disappearance. They looked to see whether some wreck of their balloon, to which a man might cling, was still floating on the surface. Nothing! The sea was but a vast watery desert. The coast, too, was desolate. Neither Neb nor the reporter could be seen. But it was possible that both were at that moment so far off that they could not be made out.

“Something tells me,” cried Herbert, “that a person so energetic as Mr. Smith would not let himself be drowned like an ordinary man. He must have got to shore; don’t you think so, Pencroff?”

The sailor shook his head sadly. He had little hope now of seeing Smith again; but he wished to leave Herbert some hope.

“No doubt, no doubt,” said he, “our engineer is a man to save himself where any one else would perish!”

Meanwhile he observed the coast with extreme attention. Beneath his eyes stretched out the sandy beach, bounded, upon the right of the river-mouth, by lines of breakers. The rocks which still were visible above the water were like groups of amphibious monsters lying in the surf. Beyond them the sea sparkled in the rays of the sun. A sharp point terminated the southern horizon, and it was impossible to tell whether the land stretched further in that direction, or whether it trended southeast and southwest, so as to make a sort of very elongated peninsula. At the northern end of the bay, the outline of the coast was continued to a great distance, in a more rounded line. There the shore was low and flat, without cliffs, but covered by great sandbanks, left by the receding tide.

When Pencroff and Herbert turned towards the west, their looks fell on the snowcapped mountain, which rose six or seven miles away. Vast wooded masses, relieved by great green patches of evergreens, extended from its first declivities to within two miles of the coast. Then from the edge of this forest to the coast stretched a broad green plateau strewn at random with clumps of trees. On the left, the waters of the little river sparkled now and then through the glades, and its rather winding course seemed to lead it back towards the spurs of the mountain, among which it must have its source. At the point where the sailor had left his timber-float, it began to flow between the two high walls of granite; but if on the left bank the walls remained clean and abrupt, on the right bank, on the contrary, they sank little by little, the masses changing into isolated rocks, the rocks into stones, the stones into pebbles, as far as the extremity of the point.

“Are we upon an island?” muttered the sailor.

“At all events, it would seem to be big enough!” said the boy.

“An island’s an island, no matter how big,” said Pencroff.

But this important question could not yet be decided; its solution must be put off till another time. The country itself, isle or continent, seemed fertile, pleasant in its aspects, and diversified in its products.

“That is fortunate,” observed Pencroff, “and in our misfortune we must thank Providence for it.”

“God be praised, then!” answered Herbert, whose pious heart was full of gratitude to the Author of all things.

For a long time Pencroff and Herbert examined the country on which their destiny had cast them; but it was difficult to imagine, after so hasty an inspection, what the future held in store for them.

Then they returned along the southern ridge of the granite plateau, outlined by a long fringe of fantastic rocks, which took the most whimsical shapes, and in whose cavities lived hundreds of birds. A whole flock of them soared aloft as Herbert jumped over the rocks.

“Ah!” cried he, “these are neither gulls nor sea-mews.”

“What are they?” said Pencroff. “They look for all the world like pigeons.”

“So they are,” said Herbert, “but they are wild pigeons, or rock pigeons. I know them by the two black bands on the wing, the white rump, and the ash-blue feathers. The rock pigeon is good to eat, and its eggs ought to be delicious; and if they have left a few in their nests—”

“We will give them no time to hatch, unless it be in an omelet!” said Pencroff, gaily.

“But what will you make your omelet in?” asked Herbert; “in your hat?”

“Well!” said the sailor, “I am not quite conjurer enough for that. We must fall back on eggs in the shell, my boy, and I will undertake to despatch the hardest!”

Pencroff and the boy examined carefully the cavities of the granite, and succeeded in discovering eggs in some of them. Some dozens were collected in the sailor’s handkerchief, and, high tide approaching, the two went down again to the water-course.

It was 1 o’clock when they arrived at the elbow of the river, and the tide was already on the turn. They must therefore take advantage of the ebb to bring the timber-float to the mouth. Pencroff had no intention of letting his timber float at random, nor did he wish to get on and steer it. But a sailor is never troubled in a matter of ropes or cordage, and Pencroff quickly twisted from the dry lianas a rope several fathoms long. This was fastened behind the raft, and the sailor held it in his hand, while Herbert kept the float in the current by pushing it off from the shore with a long pole.

This expedient proved an entire success. The enormous load of wood, which the sailor held back as he walked along the bank, kept well in the current. The bank was very steep, and there was no fear lest the float should ground; before 2 o’clock they reached the mouth of the stream, a few paces from the Chimneys.
