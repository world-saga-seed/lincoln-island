# A1: III.14 ¶30–¶41

¶30

Cyrus Smith and his companions had not failed to observe how severe the temperature was during the winters of Lincoln Island. The cold was comparable to that felt in the States of New England, which lie at about the same distance from the equator as the island. If, in the northern hemisphere, or at least in that part of it occupied by New Britain and the north of the United States, this phenomenon is explained by the flat conformation of the territories which border upon the pole, and upon which no swelling of the ground opposes any obstacle to the hyperborean winds, here, so far as Lincoln Island was concerned, that explanation could not hold.

¶31

"It has even been observed," said Cyrus Smith one day to his companions, "that, in equal latitudes, islands and coast regions are less tried by the cold than inland countries. I have often heard it asserted that the winters of Lombardy, for example, are more rigorous than those of Scotland, and this would arise from the sea giving back during the winter the heat which it has received during the summer. Islands are, therefore, in the best condition to profit by this restitution."

¶32

"But then, Mr. Cyrus," asked Harbert, "why does Lincoln Island seem to escape the common law?"

¶33

"That is difficult to explain," replied the engineer. "However, I should be disposed to admit that this singularity arises from the situation of the island in the southern hemisphere, which, as you know, my boy, is colder than the northern hemisphere."

¶34

"Indeed," said Harbert, "and floating ice is met with in lower latitudes in the south than in the north of the Pacific."

¶35

"That is true," replied Pencroff, "and, when I followed the trade of a whaler, I saw icebergs as far up as abreast of Cape Horn."

¶36

"Then, perhaps," said Gédéon Spilett, "the severe cold which visits Lincoln Island might be explained by the presence of ice or of ice-packs at a comparatively very short distance."

¶37

"Your opinion is very admissible, indeed, my dear Spilett," replied Cyrus Smith, "and it is evidently to the nearness of the ice-pack that we owe our severe winters. I would point out to you also that a cause entirely physical renders the southern hemisphere colder than the northern. In fact, since the sun is nearer to this hemisphere during the summer, it is necessarily farther from it during the winter. This explains, then, why there is an excess of temperature in both directions; and, if we find the winters very cold in Lincoln Island, let us not forget that the summers, on the contrary, are very hot there."

¶38

"But why, if you please, Mr. Smith," asked Pencroff, knitting his brows, "why is our hemisphere, as you call it, so badly served in the sharing? That is not just!"

¶39

"Friend Pencroff," replied the engineer, laughing, "just or not, we must submit to the situation, and this is whence the peculiarity comes. The earth does not describe a circle round the sun, but an ellipse, as the laws of rational mechanics require. The earth occupies one of the foci of the ellipse, and, consequently, at one period of its course it is at its apogee, that is to say, at its greatest distance from the sun, and at another period at its perigee, that is to say, at its least distance. Now it happens that it is precisely during the winter of the southern countries that it is at its point farthest from the sun, and, consequently, in the conditions required for those regions to suffer greater cold. There is nothing to be done against that, and men, Pencroff, however learned they may be, will never be able to change anything whatever in the cosmographical order established by God Himself."

¶40

"And yet," added Pencroff, who showed a certain difficulty in resigning himself, "the world is very learned! What a big book, Mr. Cyrus, could be made of all that is known!"

¶41

"And what a bigger book still of all that is not known," replied Cyrus Smith.
