# A4: III.16 ¶27, ¶38–¶50, ¶62

¶27

"As a great criminal, no doubt?" replied Captain Nemo, letting a haughty smile pass over his lips. "Yes, a rebel, put perhaps under the ban of humanity!"

¶38

Captain Nemo was an Indian, the Prince Dakkar, son of a rajah of the then independent territory of Bundelkund, and nephew of the hero of India, Tippo-Saïb. His father sent him to Europe from the age of ten years, in order that he might there receive a complete education, and with the secret intention that he might one day be able to struggle, with equal arms, against those whom he regarded as the oppressors of his country.

¶39

From ten years of age to thirty, the Prince Dakkar, endowed in a superior degree, great of heart and of mind, instructed himself in all things, and in the sciences, in letters, in the arts, he carried his studies high and far.

¶40

The Prince Dakkar travelled over the whole of Europe. His birth and his fortune caused him to be sought after, but the seductions of the world never attracted him. Young and handsome, he remained serious, sombre, devoured by the thirst for learning, with an implacable resentment riveted to his heart.

¶41

The Prince Dakkar hated. He hated the only country on which he had never been willing to set foot, the only nation whose advances he constantly refused: he hated England, and all the more because on more than one point he admired her.

¶42

For this Indian summed up in himself all the fierce hatreds of the vanquished against the victor. The invader had not been able to find grace with the invaded. The son of one of those sovereigns whose servitude the United Kingdom has been able to secure only in name, this prince, of the family of Tippo-Saïb, brought up in ideas of reclamation and of vengeance, bearing within him an ineluctable love for his poetic country, laden with English chains, would never set foot upon that land, accursed by him, to which India owed her enslavement.

¶43

The Prince Dakkar became an artist, whom the marvels of art impressed nobly; a man of learning, to whom nothing in the higher sciences was strange; a statesman, who was formed in the midst of the courts of Europe. In the eyes of those who observed him incompletely, he passed perhaps for one of those cosmopolitans, curious of knowledge but disdainful of action; for one of those opulent travellers, proud and platonic spirits, who range the world unceasingly and are of no country.

¶44

It was nothing of the kind. This artist, this man of learning, this man, had remained an Indian in heart, an Indian in his desire for vengeance, an Indian in the hope which he nourished of being able one day to reclaim the rights of his country, to drive out the stranger, to restore to it its independence.

¶45

Thus it was that the Prince Dakkar returned to Bundelkund in the year 1849. He married a noble Indian lady, whose heart bled, as did his own, at the misfortunes of her country. He had by her two children, whom he cherished. But domestic happiness could not make him forget the enslavement of India. He waited for an opportunity. It presented itself.

¶46

The English yoke had perhaps been laid too heavily upon the Hindoo populations. The Prince Dakkar borrowed the voice of the discontented. He instilled into their minds all the hatred which he felt against the foreigner. He travelled not only through the still independent countries of the Indian peninsula, but also through the regions directly subject to the English administration. He recalled the great days of Tippo-Saïb, who died heroically at Seringapatam in the defence of his country.

¶47

In 1857 the great revolt of the Sepoys broke out. The Prince Dakkar was its soul. He organized the immense rising. He placed his talents and his wealth at the service of that cause. He spared not his own person; he fought in the front rank; he risked his life like the humblest of those heroes who had risen to free their country; he was wounded ten times in twenty encounters, and had not been able to find death when the last soldiers of independence fell before the English bullets.

¶48

Never did the British power in India run such a danger, and if, as they had hoped, the Sepoys had found help from without, it would perhaps have been all over in Asia with the influence and the dominion of the United Kingdom.

¶49

The name of the Prince Dakkar was illustrious then. The hero who bore it did not hide himself, and fought openly. A price was set upon his head, and, though there was not found a traitor to deliver it up, his father, his mother, his wife, his children paid for him, even before he could know the dangers which on his account they ran . . .

¶50

Right, this time again, had fallen before might. But civilization never recedes, and it seems as though it borrowed every right from necessity. The Sepoys were vanquished, and the country of the old rajahs fell back under the closer dominion of England.

¶62

For six years the captain had been there, no longer navigating, awaiting death, that is to say, the moment when he should be reunited to his companions, when chance made him a witness of the fall of the balloon which was carrying away the prisoners of the Southerners. Clad in his diving-dress, he was walking beneath the waters, a few cables' lengths from the shore of the island, when the engineer was thrown into the sea. A good impulse carried the captain away . . . and he saved Cyrus Smith.
