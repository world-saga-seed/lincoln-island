# The Nitro-Glycerine and the New Fall (draft 1)

CHAPTER XVII

A VISIT TO THE LAKE—THE CURRENT THAT SHOWS THE WAY—THE PROJECTS OF CYRUS SMITH—THE DUGONG FAT—THE USE OF THE SCHISTOUS PYRITES—THE SULPHATE OF IRON—HOW GLYCERINE IS MADE—SOAP—SALTPETRE—SULPHURIC ACID—NITRIC ACID—THE NEW FALL.

The next day, the 7th of May, Smith and Spilett, leaving Neb to prepare the breakfast, climbed Prospect Plateau, while Herbert and Pencroff went up the river after a fresh supply of wood.

The engineer and the reporter soon arrived at the little beach, situated at the southern point of the lake, where the dugong lay stranded. Already flocks of birds had gathered about the carcass, and it was necessary to drive them off with stones, as the engineer wished to preserve the fat for the use of the colony. As to the flesh of the dugong, it would undoubtedly furnish excellent food, as in certain portions of the Malay archipelago it is especially reserved for the table of the native princes. But it was Neb’s affair to look after that.

Just now, Cyrus Smith was thinking of other things. The incident of the day before had not been effaced from his mind, and did not cease to occupy him. He wanted to solve the mystery of that submarine combat, and to know what congener of the mastodons or other marine monsters had given the dugong so strange a wound.

He stood there, then, upon the border of the lake, looking, observing; but nothing appeared beneath its tranquil waters, which sparkled under the first rays of the rising sun.

At the little beach where the dugong lay, the waters were shallow; but from that point the bottom of the lake sank little by little, and it was probable that at the centre the depth was considerable. The lake might be likened to a large basin, filled by the supply from Red Creek.

“Well, Cyrus,” questioned the reporter, “I don’t see anything suspicious in these waters?”

“No, my dear fellow, and I am at a loss how to explain yesterday’s affair.”

“I confess,” returned Spilett, “that the wound on this beast is strange, to say the least, and I can no more understand how Top could have been thrown so vigorously out of the water. One would really suppose that it had been done by a strong arm, and that that same arm, wielding a poignard, had afterwards given the dugong his death-wound.”

“Yes,” answered the engineer, who had become thoughtful. “There is something here which I cannot understand. But do you understand any better, my dear Spilett, how I myself was saved; how I was snatched from the waves and borne to the downs? No, is it not so? Therefore, I foresee here some mystery which we will doubtless some day discover. Let us watch, then; but let us not dwell upon these singular incidents before our companions, and keep our thoughts for each other, and continue our work.”

It will be remembered that Smith had not yet discovered what became of the surplus water of the lake, and as there was no indication of its ever overflowing, an outlet must exist somewhere. Now, he was rather surprised on noticing a tolerably strong current just at this place. Throwing in some little bits of wood, and seeing that they drifted towards the southern angle, he followed this current, walking along the bank, and it brought him to the southern end of the lake.

Here there was a sort of depression in the waters, as if they were suddenly lost in some opening below.

Smith listened, placing his ear to the surface of the lake, and very distinctly heard the sound of a subterranean fall.

“It is there,” said he, rising, “there that the water is discharged, there, doubtless, through a passage in the massive granite that it goes to join the sea, through cavities which we will be able to utilize to our profit! Well! I will find out!”

The engineer cut a long branch, stripped off its leaves, and, plunging it down at the angle of the two banks, he found that there was a large open hole only a foot below the surface. This was the long-sought-for outlet, and such was the force of the current that the branch was snatched from his hands and disappeared.

“There can be no doubt of it now,” repeated the engineer. “It is the mouth of the outlet, and I am going to work to uncover it.”

“How?” inquired Spilett.

“By lowering the lake three feet.”

“And how will you do that?”

“By opening another vent larger than this.”

“Whereabouts, Cyrus?”

“Where the bank is nearest the coast.”

“But it is a bank of granite,” exclaimed Spilett.

“Very well,” replied Smith. “I will blow it up, this granite, and the waters, escaping, will subside so as to discover the orifice—”

“And will make a waterfall in falling on to the beach,” added the reporter.

“A fall that we will make use of!” answered Cyrus. “Come, come!”

The engineer hurried off his companion, whose confidence in Smith was such that he doubted not the success of the undertaking. And yet, this bank of granite, how would they open it? how, without powder, with but imperfect tools, could they blast the rock? Was it not a work beyond his powers, to which the engineer was about to bend himself so obstinately?

When Smith and the reporter re-entered the Chimneys, they found Herbert and Pencroff occupied in unloading their raft.

“The wood-choppers will soon have finished, sir,” said the sailor, laughing, “and when you want masons—”

“Not masons, but chemists,” interrupted the engineer.

“Yes,” added Spilett, “we are going to blow up the island.”

“Blow up the island?” cried the sailor.

“A part of it, at least,” answered the reporter.

“Listen to me, my friends,” said the engineer, who thereupon made known the result of his observations. His theory was, that a cavity, more or less considerable, existed in the mass of granite which upheld Prospect Plateau, and he undertook to penetrate to it. To do this, it was first necessary to free the opening through which the waters rushed, in other words to lower the level of the lake by giving the water a larger issue. To do this they must manufacture an explosive with which to make a large drain in another part of the bank. It was this Smith was going to attempt to do, with the minerals Nature had placed at his disposal.

It is needless to say with what enthusiasm all, and Pencroff more particularly, entered into the proposal. To employ such grand means, to rip open the granite, to create a cascade, this suited the sailor! And he would be chemist as well as mason or bootmaker, since the engineer wanted chemists. He would be anything they liked, “even a professor of dancing and deportment,” said he to Neb, if such a thing were ever necessary.

Neb and Pencroff were at once detailed to extract the fat from the dugong and to preserve the flesh for food. They set off immediately, without even asking for any further explanation. Their confidence in the engineer was absolute.

Soon after their departure the others, dragging the hurdle, went up the river to the vein of coal, where abounded those schistous pyrites which are found, in fact, in the most recent transition formations, and of which Smith had already procured a specimen.

The whole day was employed in bringing a quantity of these pyrites to the Chimneys, and by evening they had several tons.

On the next day, the 8th of May, the engineer began his manipulations. The schistous pyrites were principally composed of carbon, of silica, of alumina, and sulphuret of iron,—this last in excess,—it was necessary to separate the sulphuret and change it into sulphate by the quickest means. The sulphate obtained, they would extract the sulphuric acid. That, indeed, was the end to be attained.

Sulphuric acid is one of the agents in most general use, and the industrial importance of a nation can be measured by its consumption. In the future this acid would be of the greatest use to the colonists in making candles, tanning skins, etc., but at present the engineer reserved it for another purpose.

Smith chose, behind the Chimneys, a place upon which the earth was carefully levelled. On this he made a pile of branches and cut wood, on which were placed pieces of schistous pyrites leaning against each other, and then all was covered over with a thin layer of pyrites previously reduced to the size of nuts.

This done, they set the wood on fire, which in turn inflamed the schist, as it contained carbon and sulphur. Then new layers of pyrites were arranged so as to form an immense heap, surrounded with earth and grass, with air-holes left here and there, just as is done in reducing a pile of wood to charcoal.

Then they left the transformation to complete itself. It would take no less than ten or twelve days for the sulphuret of iron and the alumina to change into sulphates, which substances were equally soluble; the others—silica, burnt carbon, and cinders—were not so.

While this chemical process was accomplishing itself, Smith employed his companions upon other branches of the work. They brought to it more than zeal; it was dogged persistence.

Neb and Pencroff had taken the fat from the dugong, which had been placed in large earthen jars. It was necessary to separate from this fat one of its elements, the glycerine, by saponifying it. It was sufficient, in order to do this, to treat it with soda or lime. Either of these substances, in fact, after attacking the fat, would form a soap and set free the glycerine, and it was precisely this glycerine which the engineer wished to obtain. Lime was not wanting, as is known; but by this treatment the soap would be calcareous, insoluble, and therefore useless, while by using soda, a soluble soap, which could be employed for domestic purposes, would be the result. Cyrus Smith, being a practical man, preferred to try to get the soda. Was this difficult? No, since many kinds of marine plants abounded on the shore, glass-worts, ficoides, and all those fucaceæ which form the wrack and the tangle. They therefore gathered a great quantity of these seaweed, which were first dried, and, afterwards, burnt in trenches in the open air. The combustion of these plants was continued for many days, so that the heat rose high enough to fuse the ashes, and the result of the incineration was the greyish compact mass, long known as “natural soda.”

This accomplished, the engineer treated the fat with the soda, which gave both a soluble soap and the neutral substance, glycerine.

But this was not all. Smith wanted, in view of his future operations, another substance, nitrate of potash, better known as saltpetre.

He could make this by treating carbonate of potash, which is easily extracted from vegetable ashes, with nitric acid. But this acid, which was precisely the acid he wished to obtain in the end, he did not have. There was here, therefore, a vicious circle, from which he would never have escaped. Very fortunately, this time, Nature furnished him with saltpetre, without any labor other than picking it up. Herbert had found a vein of this mineral in the north of the island, at the foot of Mount Franklin, and all they had to do was to purify the salt.

These different undertakings, which occupied about eight days, were finished before the sulphate of iron was ready. During the interval the colonists made some refractory pottery in plastic clay, and constructed a brick furnace of a peculiar shape, in which to distil the sulphate of iron. All was finished about the 18th of May, at nearly the moment when the chemical transformation was completed. Spilett, Herbert, Neb, and Pencroff, cleverly guided by the engineer, had become the most adroit workmen in the world. Necessity is, besides, of all masters the one most listened to, and the one who teaches best.

When the heap of pyrites had been entirely reduced by the fire, the result of the operation, consisting of sulphate of iron, sulphate of alumina, silica, and a residue of charcoal and cinders, was placed in a basin full of water. Having stirred up the mixture, they let it settle, and at length poured off a clear liquid holding the sulphates of iron and alumina in solution, the other matters remaining solid, since they were insoluble. Finally, this liquid was partly evaporated, the sulphate of iron crystalized, and the mother-water, that is, the liquid not evaporated, which contained the sulphate of alumina, was thrown away.

Smith had now a tolerably large quantity of crystals of sulphate of iron, from which the sulphuric acid was to be extracted.

In manufacturing practice, the making of sulphuric acid requires a costly establishment. Large works are needed, special tools, platinum apparatus, and leaden chambers which the acid cannot attack, and in which the transformation is effected, etc. The engineer had no such means at his command, but he knew that in Bohemia particularly sulphuric acid is made by simpler means, which have, moreover, the advantage of producing it at a higher degree of concentration. It is thus that the acid known as Nordhausen acid is made. For obtaining the acid in this way, all the engineer had to do was to calcinize the crystals in a closed jar in such a manner that the sulphuric acid distilled in vapor, which would in turn produce the acid by condensation.

It was for this that the refractory jars, in which the crystals were placed, and the furnace, whose heat was to distil the sulphuric acid, had been made. The operation was perfectly conducted; and on the 20th of May, twelve days after having begun, Smith was the possessor of the agent which he expected to use later in so many different ways.

What did he want with it now? Simply to produce nitric acid, which was easy, since the saltpetre, attacked by the sulphuric acid, gave him that very acid by distillation.

But, after all, how would he use this nitric acid? His companions did not yet know, for he had not said the last word of his work.

However, the engineer was nearing his object, and one last operation procured him the substance which had required all this labor. The engineer mixed the nitric acid with the glycerine, which latter had been previously concentrated by evaporation in a water-bath, and, even without employing any freezing mixture, obtained several pints of an oily yellow liquid.

This last operation Smith had conducted alone, at some distance from the Chimneys, as he feared an explosion, and when he returned, with a flagon of this liquid, to his friends, he simply said:—“Here is some nitro-glycerine!”

It was, in truth, that terrible product, whose explosive power is, perhaps, ten times as great as that of ordinary gunpowder, and which has already caused so many accidents! However, since means have been found of transforming it into dynamite, that is, of mixing it with clay or sugar or some solid substance sufficiently porous to hold it, the dangerous liquid can be used with more safety. But dynamite was not known when the colonists were at work on Lincoln Island.

“And is that stuff going to blow up the rocks?” asked Pencroff, rather incredulously.

“Yes, my friend,” answered the engineer, “and it will do all the better since the granite is extremely hard and will oppose more resistance to the explosion.”

“And when will we see all this, sir?”

“To-morrow, when we have drilled a hole,” answered the engineer.

Early the next morning, the 21st of May, the miners betook themselves to a point which formed the east bank of Lake Grant, not more than 500 paces from the coast. At this place the plateau was lower than the lake, which was held in only by its frame of granite. It was plain that could they break this the waters would escape by this vent, and, forming a stream, flow over the inclined surface of the plateau, and be precipitated on to the shore. Consequently, there would be a general lowering of the lake, and the orifice of the outlet would be uncovered—this was the final aim.

It was the frame, then, that must be broken. Pencroff, directed by the engineer, attacked its outer facing adroitly and vigorously. The hole which he made with his pick began upon a horizontal edge of the bank, and penetrated obliquely so as to reach a level sensibly lower than the lake’s surface. Thus the blowing up of the rocks would permit the water to escape freely and consequently lower the lake sufficiently.

The work was tedious, as the engineer, wishing to produce a violent shock, had determined to use not less than ten litres of nitro-glycerine in the operation. But Pencroff and Neb, taking turns at the work, did so well, that by 4 o’clock in the afternoon it was achieved.

Now came the question of igniting the explosive. Ordinarily, nitro-glycerine is ignited by the explosion of fulminated caps, as, if lighted without percussion, this substance burns and does not explode.

Smith could doubtless make a cap. Lacking fulminate, he could easily obtain a substance analogous to gun-cotton, since he had nitric acid at hand. This substance pressed in a cartridge, and introduced into the nitro-glycerine, could be lighted with a slow match, and produce the explosion.

But Smith knew that their liquid had the property of exploding under a blow. He determined, therefore, to make use of this property, reserving the other means in case this experiment failed.

The blow of a hammer upon some drops of the substance spread on a hard stone, suffices to provoke an explosion. But no one could give those blows without being a victim to the operation. Smith’s idea was to suspend a mass of iron weighing several pounds by means of a vegetable fibre to an upright post, so as to have the iron hang directly over the hole. Another long fibre, previously soaked in sulphur, was to be fastened to the middle of the first and laid along the ground to a distance of several feet from this excavation. The fire was to be applied to this second fibre, it would burn till it reached the first and set it on fire, then the latter would break and the iron be precipitated upon the nitro-glycerine.

The apparatus was fixed in place; then the engineer, after having made his companions go away, filled the hole so that the fluid was level with the opening, and spread some drops on the surface of the rock, underneath the mass of suspended iron.

This done, Smith lit the end of the sulphured fibre, and, leaving the place, rejoined his companions at the Chimneys.

The fibre was to burn for twenty-five minutes, and, in fact, twenty-five minutes after, an explosion, of which no idea can be given, was heard. It seemed as if the whole island trembled to its base. A volley of stones rose into the air as if they had been vomited from a volcano. The concussion of the air was such that the rocks of the Chimneys shook. The colonists, though more than two miles from the mine, were thrown to the ground. Rising again, they clambered up to the plateau and hurried towards the place where the bank of the lake must have been torn open by the explosion.

A triple hurrah burst from their breasts! The granite frame was split over a large space! A rapid stream of water escaped through it, leaping and foaming across the plateau, and, reaching the brink, fell a distance of 300 feet to the shore below.
