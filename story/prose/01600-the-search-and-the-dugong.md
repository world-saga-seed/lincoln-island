# The Search for a Dwelling and the Dugong (final)

CHAPTER XVI.

THE QUESTION OF A DWELLING DISCUSSED AGAIN—PENCROFF’S FANCIES—AN EXPLORATION TO THE NORTH OF THE LAKE—THE NORTHERN BOUNDARY OF THE PLATEAU—THE SERPENTS—THE END OF THE LAKE—TOP’S ALARM—TOP SWIMMING—A FIGHT UNDER WATER.

It was the 6th of May, corresponding to the 6th of November in the Northern Hemisphere. For some days the sky had been cloudy, and it was important to make provision against winter. However, the temperature had not yet lessened much, and a centigrade thermometer transported to Lincoln Island would still have averaged 10° or 12° above zero. This mean would not be surprising, since Lincoln Island, very probably situated between the thirty-fifth and fortieth parallels, must be subject, in the Southern Hemisphere, to the same climatic influences as Greece or Sicily in the Northern. But just as Greece and Sicily experience intense cold, which produces snow and ice, so, in the height of winter, this island would doubtless experience certain falls in the temperature against which it would be well to provide.

At any rate, if the cold was not yet threatening, the rainy season was at hand, and upon this isolated island, in the wide Pacific, exposed to all the inclemency of the open sea, the storms would be frequent, and, probably, terrible.

The question of a more comfortable habitation than the Chimneys ought, therefore, to be seriously considered, and promptly acted upon.

Pencroff having discovered the Chimneys, naturally had a predilection for them; but he understood very well that another place must be found. This refuge had already been visited by the sea, under circumstances which will be remembered, and it would not do to expose themselves to a like accident.

“Moreover,” added Smith, who was discussing these things with his companions that day, “there are some precautions to take.”

“Why? The island is not inhabited,” said the reporter.

“Probably not,” answered the engineer, “although we have not yet explored the whole of it; but if there are no human beings, I fear dangerous beasts are numerous. So it will be better to provide a shelter against a possible attack, than for one of us to be forced to keep watch every night to tend the fire. And then, my friends, we must foresee everything. We are here in a part of the Pacific often frequented by Malay pirates—”

“What, at this distance from land?” exclaimed Herbert.

“Yes, my boy,” answered the engineer. “These pirates are hardy sailors as well as formidable villains, and we must provide for them accordingly.”

“Well,” said Pencroff, “we will fortify ourselves against two and four-footed savages. But, sir, wouldn’t it be as well to explore the island thoroughly before doing anything else?”

“It would be better,” added Spilett; “who knows but we may find on the opposite coast one of those caves which we have looked for here in vain.”

“Very true,” answered the engineer, “but you forget, my friends, that we must be somewhere near running water, and that from the summit of Mount Franklin we were unable to see either brook or river towards the west. Here, on the contrary, we are between the Mercy and Lake Grant, which is a considerable advantage, not to be neglected. And, moreover, as this coast faces the east, it is not exposed, as the other is, to the trade winds, which blow from the northwest in this hemisphere.”

“Well, then, Mr. Smith,” replied the sailor, “let us build a house on the edge of the lake. We are no longer without bricks and tools. After having been brickmakers, potters, founders, and smiths, we ought to be masons easily enough, what the devil!”

“Yes, my friend; but before deciding we must look about. A habitation which nature had made ready at her own cost would save us a great deal of work, and would, doubtless, offer a still surer retreat, for it would be as well defended against enemies within as against those without.”

“True, Cyrus,” answered the reporter, “but we have already examined the whole of this great granite wall of the coast, and not a hole, not even a crack!”

“No, not one!” added Pencroff. “Ah! if we could only dig a place in it high out of reach, that would be the thing! I can see it now, on the part overlooking the sea, five or six chambers—”

“With windows to light them!” said Herbert, laughing.

“And a staircase to go up to them!” added Neb.

“You laugh,” cried the sailor, “and why? What is there impossible in what I propose? Haven’t we picks and mattocks? Cannot Mr. Smith make powder to blow up the mine? You will be able, won’t you, sir, to make powder when we want it?”

The engineer had listened to the enthusiastic sailor developing these somewhat imaginative projects. To attack this mass of granite, even by mining, was an Herculean task, and it was truly vexing that nature had not done the hardest part of the work for them. But he answered Pencroff, by simply proposing to examine the wall more attentively, from the mouth of the river to the angle which ended it to the north. They therefore went out and examined it with extreme care for about two miles. But everywhere it rose, uniform and upright, without any visible cavity. The rock-pigeons flying about its summit had for nests, in reality, only holes drilled in the very crest, or upon the irregularly cut edge of the granite.

To attempt to make a sufficient excavation in such a massive wall even with pickaxe and powder was not to be thought of. It was vexatious enough. By chance, Pencroff had discovered in the Chimneys, which must now be abandoned, the only temporary, habitable shelter on this part of the coast.

When the survey was ended the colonists found themselves at the northern angle of the wall, where it sunk by long declivities to the shore. From this point to its western extremity it was nothing more than a sort of talus, a thick mass of stones, earth, and sand bound together by plants, shrubs, and grass, in a slope of only 45°. Here and there the granite thrust its sharp points out from the cliff. Groups of trees grew over these slopes and a rather thick grass carpeted them. But the vegetation went no farther, and the long stretch of sand, beginning at the foot of the talus, merged into the beach.

Smith thought, not without reason, that the overflow of the lake must fall in this direction in the form of a cascade, as the excess of water from Red Creek must necessarily be discharged somewhere, and the engineer had not yet found this point on any part of the banks already explored, that is to say from the mouth of the creek, on the west, as far as Prospect Plateau.

The engineer proposed to his companions that they clamber up the talus and return to the Chimneys by the heights, exploring the northern and eastern shores of the lake. The proposition was accepted, and, in a few minutes, Herbert and Neb had climbed to the plateau, the others following more leisurely.

Two hundred feet distant the beautiful sheet of water shone through the leaves in the sunlight. The landscape was charming at this spot. The trees, in autumn tints, were marvellously grouped, a feast for the eyes. Some huge old trunks, felled by age, stood out by their blackish bark in sharp relief against the green turf which covered the ground, and a whole world of noisy cockatoos, like moving prisms, chattered there, hopping from one branch to another. It seemed as if the light came through this singular foliage only decomposed.

The colonists, instead of proceeding directly to the north bank of the lake, bore along the edge of the plateau, so as to come back to the mouth of the creek, on its left bank. It was a circuit of a mile and a half at most. The walk was easy, as the trees, set wide apart, left free passage between them. They could see that the fertile zone stopped at this point, and that the vegetation here was less vigorous than anywhere between the creek and the Mercy.

Smith and his companions moved with some caution over this unexplored neighborhood. Bows and arrows and iron-pointed sticks were their sole weapons. But no beast showed itself, and it was probable that the animals kept to the thicker forests in the south. The colonists, however, experienced a disagreeable surprise in seeing Top stop before a huge serpent 14 or 15 feet long. Neb killed it with a blow of his stick. Smith examined the reptile, and pronounced it to belong to the species of diamond-serpents eaten by the natives of New South Wales and not venomous, but it was possible others existed whose bite was mortal, such as the forked-tail deaf viper, which rise up under the foot, or the winged serpents, furnished with two ear-like appendages, which enable them to shoot forward with extreme rapidity. Top having gotten over his surprise, pursued these reptiles with a fierceness which made them fear for him, and his master was constantly obliged to call him in.

The mouth of Red Creek, where it emptied into the lake, was soon reached. The party recognized on the opposite bank the point visited on their descent from Mount Franklin. Smith ascertained that the supply of water from the creek was rather considerable; there therefore must be an outlet for the overflow somewhere. It was this place which must be found, as, doubtless, it made a fall which could be utilized as a motive power.

The colonists, strolling along, without, however, straying too far from each other, began to follow round the bank of the lake, which was very abrupt. The water seemed to be extremely full of fishes, and Pencroff promised himself soon to manufacture some apparatus with which to capture them.

It was necessary first to double the sharp point at the northeast. It might have been supposed that the discharge would be here, as the end of the lake came almost to the edge of the plateau. But as it was not here, the colonists continued along the bank, which, after a slight curve, followed parallel with the sea-shore.

On this side the bank was less wooded, but clumps of trees, here and there, added to the picturesqueness of the landscape. The whole extent of the lake, unmoved by a single ripple, was visible before them. Top, beating the bush, flushed many coveys of various birds, which Spilett and Herbert saluted with their arrows. One of these birds, cleverly hit by the lad, dropped in the rushes. Top rushing after it, brought back a beautiful slate-colored water fowl, with a short bill, a very large frontal plate, toes widened by a scalloped border, and wings edged with a white stripe. It was a coot, as large as a big partridge, belonging to the group of macrodactyls, which form the division between the waders and the palmipedes. Poor game, on the whole, whose taste must have left something to be desired, but as Top would doubtless be less difficult to please than his masters, it was agreed that the bird would answer for his supper.

The colonists were then following the eastern bank of the lake, and would soon reach the part they had previously visited. The engineer was very much surprised, as he had seen no indication of an outlet to the surplus water. In talking with the reporter and the sailor, he did not conceal his astonishment.

At this moment, Top, who had been behaving himself quietly, showed signs of alarm. The intelligent animal, running along the bank, suddenly stopped, with one foot raised, and looked into the water as if pointing some invisible game. Then he barked furiously, questing, as it were, and again was suddenly silent.

At first neither Smith nor his companions paid any attention to the dog’s actions, but his barking became so frequent, that the engineer grew uneasy at it.

“What is it, Top?” he called.

The dog bounded towards his master, and, showing a real anxiety, rushed back to the bank. Then, suddenly, he threw himself into the lake.

“Come back here, Top,” cried the engineer, not wishing his dog to venture in those suspicious waters.

“What’s going on under there?” asked the sailor examining the surface of the lake.

“Top has smelt something amphibious,” answered Herbert.

“An alligator, no doubt?” said the reporter.

“I don’t think so,” answered Smith. “Alligators are only met with in lower latitudes.”

Meantime, Top came ashore at the call of his master, but he could not be quiet; he rushed along the bank, through the tall grass, and, guided by instinct, seemed to be following some object, invisible under the water, which was hugging the shore. Nevertheless the surface was calm and undisturbed by a ripple. Several times the colonists stood still on the bank and watched the water, but they could discover nothing. There was some mystery here, and the engineer was much perplexed.

“We will follow out this exploration to the end,” he said.

In half an hour all had arrived at the southeast angle of the lake, and were again upon Prospect Plateau. They had made the circle of the bank without the engineer having discovered either where or how the surplus water was discharged.

“Nevertheless, this outlet exists,” he repeated, “and, since it is not outside, it must penetrate the massive granite of the coast!”

“But of what importance is it to you to find that out, my dear Cyrus?” asked Spilett.

“Of considerable importance,” answered the engineer, “for if the outlet is through the solid rock it is possible that there is some cavity, which could be easily rendered habitable, after having turned the water in another direction.”

“But, Mr. Smith, may not the water flow into the sea, through a subterranean outlet at the bottom of the lake?” asked Herbert.

“Perhaps so,” answered Smith, “and in that case, since Nature has not borne the first cost of construction, we must build our house ourselves.”

As it was 5 o’clock in the evening, the colonists were thinking of returning to the Chimneys across the plateau, when Top again became excited, and, barking with rage, before his master could hold him, he sprang a second time into the lake. Every one ran to the bank. The dog was already more than twenty feet off, and Smith was calling him back urgently, when suddenly an enormous head emerged from the water, which did not appear to be deep at that spot.

Herbert instantly recognized the kind of amphibious animal to which this conical head belonged, with large eyes and long silky moustaches.

“A manatee!” he cried.

Although not a manatee, it was a specimen of that species, included in the order of cetaceans, which bears the name of “dugong,” for its nostrils were open on the upper part of its muzzle.

The enormous animal threw himself upon the dog, which tried in vain to escape by returning to the bank. His master could do nothing to save him, and, before Spilett or Herbert had even thought of drawing their bows, Top, seized by the dugong, had disappeared under the water.

Neb, his iron-pointed spear in hand, would have sprung to the rescue of the dog, resolved to attack the formidable monster in its own element.

“No, Neb,” said the engineer, holding back his courageous servant.

Meanwhile a struggle was going on under the water—a struggle which, owing to the powerlessness of the dog, was inexplicable; a struggle which, they could see by the agitation of the surface, must be terrible; in short, a struggle which could only be terminated by the death of the dog. But suddenly, through the midst of a circle of foam, Top appeared, shot upward by some unknown force, rising ten feet in the air, and falling again into the tumultuous waters, from which he escaped to shore without any serious wounds, miraculously saved.

Cyrus Smith and his companions looked on without understanding. Still more inexplicable, it seemed as if the struggle under water continued. Doubtless the dugong, after having let go the dog, had been attacked by some powerful animal, and had been obliged to defend itself.

But this did not last much longer. The water grew red with blood, and the body of the dugong, emerging from a scarlet sheet which spread widely, ran aground on a little strand at the southern angle of the lake.

The colonists ran to where the animal lay, and found it dead. Its body was enormous, measuring between 15 and 16 feet long and which must have weighed between 3,000 and 4,000 pounds. On its neck, yawned a wound, which seemed to have been made by a sharp blade.

What was the amphibious creature that had been able, by this terrible cut, to kill the formidable dugong? No one could have said, and, rather preoccupied with this incident, they returned to the Chimneys.
