# The Silent Wire and a Shot (final)

CHAPTER XLVIII.

THE PROJECTED EXPEDITION—AYRTON AT THE CORRAL—VISIT TO PORT BALLOON—PENCROFF’S REMARKS ON BOARD THE BONADVENTURE—DESPATCH SENT TO THE CORRAL—NO ANSWER FROM AYRTON—SETTING OUT NEXT DAY—WHY THE WIRE DID NOT ACT—A DETONATION.

Meanwhile the thing uppermost in the colonists’ thought was to achieve the complete exploration of the island which had been decided upon, an exploration which now would have two objects: —First, to discover the mysterious being whose existence was no longer a matter of doubt; and, at the same time to find out what had become of the pirates, what hiding place they had chosen, what sort of life they were leading, and what was to be feared from them.

Smith would have set off at once, but as the expedition would take several days, it seemed better to load the wagon with various necessaries for camping out, and with utensils which would facilitate the arrangement of their halts. Now at this time one of the onaggas, wounded in the leg, could not bear harness; it must have several days’ rest, and they thought it would make little difference if they delayed the departure a week, that is, till November 20. November in this latitude corresponds to the May of the Northern Hemisphere, and they were therefore in the fine season. The sun was reaching the Tropic of Capricorn, and gave the longest days in the year, so that the time would be altogether favorable to the projected expedition, which, if it did not attain its principal object, might be fruitful in discoveries, especially as regards natural productions. For Smith intended to explore those thick forests of the Far West, which stretched to the end of Serpentine Peninsula.

During the nine days which would precede their setting out, it was agreed that they should finish work on Prospect Plateau. But Ayrton had to go back to the corral to take care of their domesticated animals. It was settled that he should stay there two days, and not come back to Granite House until he had left the stables well stocked with fodder. Just as he was setting out, Smith asked him if he would like to have one of them with him, pointing out that the island was less safe than formerly. Ayrton replied that it would be useless, as he could do everything by himself, and that, besides, he feared nothing. If anything happened at or near the corral, he would instantly acquaint the colonists of it by a telegram sent to Granite House.

So Ayrton set off on the 9th at dawn, taking the wagon, drawn by a single onagga, and two hours afterwards the electric bell gave notice that he had found everything in order at the corral.

During these two days Smith was busy at a project which would finally secure Granite House from a surprise. The point was to hide completely the upper orifice of the former weir, which had been already blocked up with stones, and half hidden under grass and plants, at the southern angle of Lake Grant. Nothing could be easier, since by raising the level of the lake two or three feet, the hole would be entirely under water.

Now to raise the level, they had only to make a dam across the two trenches by which Glycerine Creek and Waterfall Creek were fed. The colonists were invited to the task, and the two dams, which were only seven or eight feet wide, by three feet high, were rapidly erected of closely cemented stones. When the work had been done, no one could have suspected that at the end of the lake there was a subterranean conduit, by which the overflow of its waters had formerly escaped. It need hardly be said that the little stream which served to feed the reservoir at Granite House, and to work the elevator, had been carefully kept to its channel, and that water would in no case be wanting. The elevator once raised, this safe and comfortable retreat might defy any surprise or attack.

This work had been quickly finished, and Pencroff, Spilett, and Herbert found time for an expedition to Port Balloon. The sailor was very anxious to know whether the little inlet up which the Bonadventure was moored had been visited by the convicts.

“The fact is,” he observed, “these gentry got to land on the southern shore, and if they followed the line of the coast it is to be feared that they have discovered the little harbor, in which case I wouldn’t give half a dollar for our Bonadventure.”

Pencroff’s fears were not without some foundation, and a visit to Port Balloon appeared very opportune.

So off the three went in the afternoon of November 10. They were well armed, and as Pencroff ostentatiously slipped two bullets into each barrel of his gun, he shook his head in a way which presaged no good to whoever came too near, “beast or man,” as he said. Spilett and Herbert also took their guns, and about three o’clock all three left Granite House. Neb went with them to the elbow of the Mercy, and lifted the bridge after them. It was agreed that they should give notice of their return by firing a shot, when Neb would come back and re-establish communication between the two banks of the river.

The little band walked straight along the road to the harbor, towards the south coast of the island. The distance was only three miles and a half, but they took two hours to walk it. For they had searched the whole border of the road, on the side of the thick forest as well as on that of Tadorn’s Fens. They found no trace of the fugitives, who, doubtless, not yet knowing the number of the colonists nor the means of defense at their disposal, must have made for the least accessible parts of the island. Arriving at Port Balloon, Pencroff saw with extreme satisfaction that the Bonadventure was quietly moored in the narrow inlet. Indeed, Port Balloon was so well hidden among those high rocks that it could be discovered neither from sea nor shore, unless one were upon it or within it.

“After all,” said Pencroff, “the rascals haven’t been here yet. The tall grass suits reptiles better, and it is evidently in the Far-West that we shall find them.”

“And it’s a very fortunate thing,” added Herbert, “for if they had found the Bonadventure, they would have made use of her in getting away, and that would have prevented our going back to Tabor Island before long.”

“Yes,” replied the reporter, “it will be important to carry a paper to Tabor Island stating the situation of Lincoln Island and Ayrton’s new residence, in case the Scotch yacht should come after him.”

“Well, the Bonadventure is still there, Mr. Spilett,” said the sailor, “ready to start with her crew at the first signal!”

“I think, Pencroff, that it is a thing to be done as soon as our expedition in the island is ended. It is possible, after all, that this stranger, if we succeed in finding him, may know a great deal both about Lincoln Island and about Tabor Island. Let us not forget that he is beyond question the author of the document, and perhaps he knows what to expect as to the return of the yacht!”

“A thousand devils!” cried Pencroff, “who can he be? He knows us, this fellow, and we do not know him! If he is only a shipwrecked man, why does he hide himself? We are honest folk, I suppose, and the company of honest folk is disagreeable to nobody! Did he come here of his own free will? Can he leave the island if he likes? Is he here still? Is he here no longer?”

Talking thus, they got on board and walked about the deck. On a sudden the sailor, after examining the bit around which the cable of the anchor was wound, cried,

“Hallo! well, this beats all!”

“What’s the matter, Pencroff?” asked the reporter.

“The matter is that that knot was never tied by me——”

And Pencroff pointed to a rope which made the cable fast to the bit, so as to prevent its tripping.

“How, never tied by you?” asked Spilett.

“No, I could swear to it. This is a reef knot, and my habit is to make two half-hitches.(1)”

(1) A kind of knot familiar to sailors, and which has the advantage of always keeping tight.

“You must be mistaken, Pencroff.”

“No, I’m not mistaken,” insisted the sailor. “One has that in one’s hand, naturally, and the hand makes no mistakes!”

“Then have the convicts been on board?” asked Herbert.

“I don’t know,” said Pencroff, “but what is certain is that somebody has raised the Bonadventure’s anchor and dropped it again! And look here! here is another proof. Some of the cable has been paid out, and its service(2) is no longer at the hawse-hole. I tell you again, somebody has been using our boat!”

(2) The service is a piece of old canvas wrapped round the cable of the anchor, so that it may not wear in the part which bears in the hawse-hole.

“But if the convicts had used her, they would either have plundered her, or else they would have fled——”

“Fled! where to? to Tabor Island?” replied Pencroff. “Do you think, then, that they would have risked themselves in a boat of so small a tonnage?”

“Besides, we should have to suppose that they knew of the islet,” replied the reporter.

“However that may be,” said the sailor, “as sure as I am Bonadventure Pencroff, of the Vineyard, our Bonadventure has sailed without us!”

The sailor was so positive that neither Spilett nor Herbert could contest his assertion. It was evident that the boat had shifted place more or less since Pencroff had brought it back to Balloon Harbor. As for the sailor, he had no doubt that the anchor had been pulled up and cast again. Now, why had these manœuvres taken place unless the boat had been used on some expedition?

“Then why did we not see the Bonadventure pass the offing?” said the reporter, who wanted to raise every possible objection.

“But, Mr. Spilett,” answered the sailor, “they could have set out in the night with a good wind, and in two hours have been out of sight of the island.”

“Well,” said Spilett, “I still ask with what object the convicts used the Bonadventure, and why, after using her, they brought her back to port?”

“Well, Mr. Spilett,” said the sailor, “let us put that among the things which cannot be explained, and think no more of it. The important thing was that the Bonadventure should be there, and it is here! Unfortunately, if the convicts take it a second time, it may very well not be found in its place again.”

“Then, Pencroff,” said Herbert, “perhaps it would be prudent to bring the Bonadventure back in front of Granite House?”

“Yes and no,” answered the sailor, “or rather no. The mouth of the Mercy is a bad place for a boat; the sea is rough there.”

“But by hauling it up on the sand, to the very foot of the Chimneys——”

“Perhaps—yes,” answered Pencroff. “In any case, as we have to leave Granite House for a rather long expedition, I believe the Bonadventure will be safer here during our absence, and we shall do well to leave her here until the island is rid of these rascals.”

“That is my opinion, too,” said the reporter. “At least in case of bad weather, she will not be exposed as she would be at the mouth of the Mercy.”

“But if the convicts should pay her another visit?” said Herbert.

“Well, my boy,” said Pencroff, “if they did not find the boat here they would soon go and look for her near Granite House; and in our absence there would be nothing to prevent their carrying her off. I agree with Mr. Spilett that we had better leave her at Balloon Harbor; but if we have not rid the island of these wretches by the time we come back it will be prudent to take our boat back to Granite House, until she has no more ill-disposed visits to fear.”

“All right,” said Spilett. “Let us go back now.”

When they returned to Granite House, they told Smith what had happened, and the latter approved their present and future plans. He even promised Pencroff he would examine that part of the channel situated between the islet and the coast, so as to see if it would be possible to make an artificial harbor by means of dams. In this way the Bonadventure would be always within reach, in sight of the colonists, and locked up if necessary.

On the same evening they sent a telegram to Ayrton, asking him to bring back from the corral a couple of goats, which Neb wished to acclimatize on the meadows of the plateau. Strange to say, Ayrton did not acknowledge the receipt of this despatch, as was his custom to do. This surprised the engineer. But it might be that Ayrton was not at the corral at the moment, or even that he was on his way back to Granite House. In fact, two days had elapsed since his departure; and it had been agreed that on the evening of the 10th or the morning of the 11th, at latest, he would return.

The colonists were now waiting to see Ayrton on Prospect Plateau. Neb and Herbert even watched the approaches to the bridge, so as to let it down when their companion should appear, but when about 10 o’clock in the evening came, and there was no sign whatever of Ayrton, they agreed to send another despatch, asking for an immediate answer.

The bell at Granite House remained silent.

The uneasiness of the colonists was now great. What had happened? Was Ayrton not at the corral? or, if there, had he not power over his own movements? Ought they to go to the corral on this dark night?

They discussed the point. Some were for going, and others for waiting.

“But,” said Herbert, “perhaps some accident has happened to the telegraphic apparatus, so that it no longer works?”

“That may be,” said the reporter.

“Let us wait until to-morrow,” said Smith. “It is possible, in fact, that Ayrton has not received our despatch, or even that we have not received his.”

They waited, as may be imagined, not without some anxiety. At daylight on the 11th of November, Smith again sent the electric current along the wire, but received no answer. Again, with the same result.

“Let us set off for the corral,” said he.

“And well armed!” added Pencroff.

It was at once agreed that Granite House must not be deserted, so Neb was left behind to take charge. After accompanying his companions to Glycerine Creek, he put up the bridge again, and hid behind a tree, to wait either for their return or for that of Ayrton. In case the pirates should appear, and should attempt to force the passage, he would try to defend it with his gun; and in the last resort he would take refuge in Granite House, where, the elevator once drawn up, he would be in safety. The others were to go direct to the corral, and failing to find Ayrton there, were to scour the neighboring woods.

At 6 o’clock in the morning the engineer and his three companions had crossed Glycerine Creek, and Neb posted himself behind a slight embankment, crowned by some large dragon trees on the left side of the brook. The colonists, after leaving Prospect Plateau, took the direct route to the corral. They carried their guns on their arms, ready to fire at the first sign of hostility. The two rifles and the two guns had been loaded with ball.

On either side of the path was a dense thicket, which might easily hide malefactors, who, as they were armed, would be indeed formidable. The colonists walked on rapidly without a word. Top preceded them, sometimes keeping to the path, and sometimes making a detour into the wood, but always silent, and not appearing to suspect anything unusual; and they might depend upon it that the faithful dog would not be taken by surprise, and would bark at the slightest appearance of danger.

Along this same path Smith and his companions followed the telegraphic wires which connected the corral with Granite House. For the first two miles or so they did not notice any solution of continuity. The posts were in good condition, the insulators uninjured, and the wire evenly stretched. However, from this point the engineer noticed that the tension seemed less complete, and at last, arriving at post No. 74, Herbert, who was ahead of the others, stopped and cried, “The wire is broken!”

His companions hastened forward and arrived at the spot where the boy had stopped. There the overturned post was lying across the path. They had discovered the break, and it was evident that the dispatches from Granite House could not have been received at the corral, nor those from the corral at Granite House.

“It can’t be the wind that has overturned this post,” said Pencroff.

“No,” answered the reporter, “the earth has been dug away at its foot; it has been uprooted by the hand of man.”

“Besides, the wire is broken,” added Herbert, showing the two ends of the wire which had been violently torn asunder.

“Is the break a fresh one?” asked Smith.

“Yes,” said Herbert, “it was certainly made a short time ago.”

“To the corral! to the corral!” cried the sailor.

The colonists were then midway between Granite House and the corral, and had still two miles and a half to go. They started on a run.

In fact, they might well fear that something serious had happened at the corral. Ayrton doubtless might have sent a telegram which had not arrived. It was not this which alarmed his companions, but a circumstance more inexplicable. Ayrton, who had promised to come back the evening before, had not reappeared! Lastly, it was not without a motive that all communication between the corral and Granite House had been cut off, and who but the convicts had any interest in cutting it off?

They hurried on, their hearts beating quick with fear for their new comrade, to whom they were sincerely attached. Were they to find him struck down by the hand of those he had formerly led?

Soon they reached the place where the road lay along by the little brook flowing from Red Creek, which watered the meadows of the corral. They had moderated their pace, so as not to be out of breath at the moment when a struggle might perhaps become necessary. Their guns were no longer at half-cock, but cocked. Each of them watched one side of the woods. Top kept up a low, ill-omened growling.

At last the fenced enclosure appeared behind the trees. They saw no signs of devastation. The door was closed as usual; a profound silence reigned at the corral. Neither the accustomed bleatings of the moufflons nor the voice of Ayrton was to be heard.

“Let us go in,” said Smith, and the engineer advanced, while his companions, keeping guard twenty paces from him, stood ready to fire.

Smith raised the inner latch, and was about to push back one leaf of the door, when Top barked violently. There was a report from above the fence, followed by a cry of pain.

Herbert, struck by a bullet, lay on the ground!
