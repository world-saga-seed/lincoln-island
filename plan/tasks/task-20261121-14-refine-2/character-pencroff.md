# Character check: Pencroff (R1121-138)

Rows C1, C3, C4, C5, C6, C9, C10, C11, C12, C13, C14, C16, C17. Read: the beats, draft 4 of 01950, the cards for Pencroff and Cyrus Smith, and Verne's French. Citations are part.chapter ¶paragraph. Draft names are White's (Herbert, Neb); the French has Harbert and Nab.

Counts: in character 7 (C1, C3, C6, C10, C11, C14, C16); plausible 2 (C4, C5); strained 4 (C9, C12, C13, C17); out of character 0.

## C1. Out in the cold rain, with a joke about the fish: in character

Like cases:
- III.14 ¶29: «Ils n’étaient point hommes à s’embarrasser du vent qui leur tordait la chevelure, ni de la pluie qui les traversait jusqu’aux os»
- II.9 ¶4, the hail, he runs to his wheat: «Il fut lapidé à sa place, mais il ne s’en plaignit pas.»
- I.19 ¶21, the man for the joke: «Pencroff ayant toujours le mot pour rire»

Verdict: in character. Bad weather does not stop him, and a dry crack at himself is his manner (II.13 ¶38: «Et Pencroff de rire de sa plaisanterie»). Neb's "madmen" is not mine to rule.

## C3. "Back before the turn, my boy," aloud: in character

Like cases:
- II.11 ¶22, asking to take the boy: «comme il faut tout prévoir, je vous demanderai la permission de n’emmener qu’Harbert avec moi»
- II.13 ¶30: «Pencroff était un marin prudent»
- II.13 ¶49, in the reefs of Tabor, Harbert at the helm, he in the bow: «prêt à amener sa voile, dont il tenait la drisse en main»

Verdict: in character. He gives orders in a sailor's short phrases and uses "mon garçon" (I.4 ¶7: «En route, mon garçon.»). A spoken limit with the boy is what he does.

## C4. Drags the wrack off the ball: plausible

Like cases (his hands, not this act):
- I.15 ¶28: «Le marin et le nègre se tirèrent adroitement de leur opération»
- I.17 ¶67: «armé d’un pic qu’il maniait adroitement et vigoureusement»

Verdict: plausible. Nothing against it; Verne gives no like act, only the hands that do it.

## C5. Saws blind at the hidden cord a long while: plausible

Like cases:
- II.10 ¶1: «Lorsque Pencroff s’était mis un projet en tête, il n’avait et ne laissait pas de cesse qu’il n’eût été exécuté»
- I.9 ¶63, at the fire: «l’entêté marin»
- I.4 ¶63, cord is his trade: «un marin n’est jamais embarrassé, quand il s’agit de câbles ou de cordages»

Verdict: plausible. The stubbornness and the cordage are his; nothing against it. One note: a long blind sawing, on a bar with the tide due, sits ill with the caution he shows in C3 and C14, and it feeds C9 (see below).

## C6. "I have put in at many good ports, my boy, and I have never seen the like": in character

Like cases:
- III.14 ¶35: «quand je faisais le métier de baleinier, j’ai vu des icebergs jusque par le travers du cap Horn»
- III.8 ¶16: «J’en ai vu bien d’autres»
- I.2 ¶24: «qui avait couru toutes les mers du globe»

Verdict: in character. He measures things by what he has seen in his seafaring, and says so.

## C9. Forgets his limit and the water, and the low-water hour goes by: strained

Like cases (against):
- II.13 ¶30: «Pencroff était un marin prudent»
- II.13 ¶49: «prêt à amener sa voile, dont il tenait la drisse en main»
- II.14 ¶30, of the boat: «le droit des pères est d’être souvent inquiet plus que de raison»
- II.19 ¶57, he reads the sky: «cyrrhus effilés, éparpillés au zénith»

Like cases (for): his mind can be taken by a thing. II.10 ¶46: «Cela agaçait surtout Pencroff et lui donnait des distractions pendant son travail.» But that is at the bench, not at sea.

Verdict: strained. It leans against what Verne shows every time the sea and a person he loves are in the case: he watches. It leans harder because the draft makes the day one of squalls (¶1, ¶10, ¶11) and he has just said his limit aloud (C3). Verne's man who reads a west gale from the cirrus would not forget a turning tide in rain.

Plainest change: he keeps his eye on the water and misjudges, not forgets: he looks up, thinks he has the half hour for the three letters, and the squall and the flood come in together and early. Rests on II.13 ¶30, II.19 ¶57 (he reads weather, and can be caught by it) and II.14 ¶30.

## C10. The boy's foot in the old net, dug at by hand, a sea over them, cut free with the knife, then taken by the arm: in character

Like cases:
- II.14 ¶49, the ape on Harbert: «Se jeter sur ce monstre, le terrasser à son tour, lui arracher Harbert»
- I.7 ¶36, in the storm: «en reprenant ses idées avec cette promptitude particulière aux gens de mer»
- I.13 ¶18, what a blade is to him: «Ah ! si nous avions seulement un couteau !»

Verdict: in character. Quick, bodily, to the boy first, a seaman's cutting of a cord, and then he takes the lad in hand. I find nothing against it.

## C11. Herbert coughs and laughs; Pencroff does not laugh: in character

Like cases:
- III.7 ¶25, Harbert hit: «Pencroff se tut, mais, une réaction s’opérant en lui, de grosses larmes inondèrent son visage.»
- III.3 ¶11, Harbert back: «Pencroff parvint à se rendre assez maître de lui pour comprimer son émotion»
- III.8 ¶11, at the wounded boy's bed: «racontait les histoires les plus invraisemblables»

Verdict: in character that he is grave when the boy has been in danger, and that the feeling shows after. Note the direction: Verne's Pencroff, moved, speaks, roars or weeps, and does not go quiet and shut. The draft's silence (here and at the ladder) is a little less than he is; a word or a hand on the boy (he has the "great hand" at C16) would sit nearer.

## C12. The silence at the foot of the ladder: strained

Pencroff asks what they would give the others "but a hope, with nothing to show"; Herbert agrees. Try A adds Herbert's ducking line and Pencroff's silent look. Try B has Pencroff's "A ship in every cloud, all winter?"

Like cases:
- II.11 ¶24, his promise to Smith about the boy: «nous ne vous causerons pas ce chagrin-là»
- II.11 ¶23, Smith's answer, in the same scene, shows what the boy's safety is to Smith: «cet enfant, dont le hasard a fait notre fils»
- I.19 ¶21, Smith: «Sa foi dans l’ingénieur était absolue»
- II.10 ¶33, when there is a secret in the colony, it is kept from him: «le marin ne se douta de rien»
- II.16 ¶24, his view of a secret: «Il a des secrets…»

Verdict: strained. (1) Pencroff holds back from Smith nothing that touches the boy; the near-drowning and the whole find are exactly what he would carry to Smith. (2) In Verne secrets are Smith's (I.22 ¶55: «l’ingénieur ne communiqua ses impressions qu’à Gédéon Spilett»), or a surprise got up by others; Pencroff is the man it is kept from, and he is uneasy at them. What is nearest in him is the sceptic about hope (II.15 ¶69: «l’incrédule Pencroff»), so the reasoning "it would be only a hope" is the part that holds.

On the tries: A is nearer (his silence is a thing he does: I.9 ¶65, «Mais il se tut.»). B is out of key: Verne's Pencroff is not weary of ships; he says (I.14 ¶53) «nous trouverons le moyen de nous en aller», and in III.1 he cannot stand still when one appears (III.1 ¶55: «ne pouvant tenir en place»).

Plainest change: let the delay, not the secret, be his: "We'll tell Mr. Smith when we have the thing in our hands", with the boy's ducking told in the joke at the door, as the draft already has him do. Rests on II.11 ¶24 and I.19 ¶21.

## C13. Tell only the joke, keep the float from Smith, Spilett and Neb, and never bring it up: strained

Like cases:
- I.19 ¶26: «Quand le marin s’enthousiasmait»
- II.10 ¶46, he cannot keep a thing in: «La nuit, il en rêvait à voix haute»
- II.12 ¶121, when beaten he holds his tongue to resume: «L’entêté marin ne répondit pas et laissa tomber la conversation, bien décidé à la reprendre»

Verdict: strained, the weaker for "never". The held tongue is his; the held tongue for good is not. The "madmen" and "round thing" he would have shared; and II.12 ¶121 shows him letting a thing drop only to take it up again.

Plainest change: he keeps it from them for now, with a reason he can say (nothing to show), and brings it up when it can cost nothing, with the lad's help: a word to Smith some days on. Rests on II.12 ¶121 and I.19 ¶26.

## C14. On 3 June he keeps the lad on his landward side and does not take his eyes off the gut: in character

Like cases:
- II.13 ¶49: «prêt à amener sa voile, dont il tenait la drisse en main»
- II.14 ¶30: «le droit des pères est d’être souvent inquiet plus que de raison»
- II.19 ¶67, the boat: «il y tenait plus qu’à sa propre peau»

Verdict: in character. He would be more careful after, and the boy is "son enfant" (III.3 ¶11).

## C16. Shakes himself as Top does, "Bah! The sea has taken, and the sea will give us something else," hand on the lad's shoulder: in character

Like cases:
- I.5 ¶34, the lost matches: «il ne fût point homme à s’embarrasser de peu, ni de beaucoup»
- I.14 ¶53: «qui éclata de rire, en remuant ses larges épaules»
- II.7 ¶51, of his luck: «la providence avait un faible pour lui»
- I.2 ¶54, "Bah" as he lightens the car: «Bah ! un de plus !»

Against, for fairness: III.13 ¶63, «véritablement consterné de la perte de son embarcation», and III.14 ¶40, «qui montra une certaine difficulté à se résigner». Real losses of his own weigh on him. Here the loss is a hope that was never shown to any, so the quick Bah is fair.

Verdict: in character. The simile of the dog is the narrator's, not his, and Verne uses animal likenesses for him.

## C17. Keeps the man-made thing on the bar from Smith, who built against others coming (I.19 ¶5), because it may be a stone and he will not raise hopes: strained

Like cases:
- I.19 ¶10, where Pencroff himself says the island is not safe until explored: «Nous n’en serons sûrs, évidemment, que lorsque nous l’aurons explorée dans toutes ses parties»
- I.10 ¶9, Smith to him: «c’était assurément plus qu’un homme»
- III.6 ¶31, when he finds something odd he calls Smith at once: «Ceci est un nœud plat»
- II.16 ¶88, what he does keep for himself is a piece of work: «Comme toujours, Pencroff était enchanté de son ouvrage»

Verdict: strained. This is the plainest breach. A man-made thing near the shore is the case Smith asked him about (I.19 ¶9: «En êtes-vous bien sûr, Pencroff ?»), and he admitted he was not sure. With his absolute faith in Smith (I.19 ¶21: «Sa foi dans l’ingénieur était absolue») he is the man who tells at once.

Plainest change: rest the silence on a gift, not on hope: he means to bring the thing up the ladder in his arms and lay it before Smith (the draft's own ¶85 reckoning about the window is that sort of thought), and the tide takes it first. Rests on II.16 ¶88 and I.19 ¶21. Better still, short of that: he tells Smith there is something round on the bar, and Smith's own answer (a hope or a stone) is the draft's scene.

## The beats as a whole

Taken together they make a Pencroff who is quieter, closed, and one who sits on a secret: the drift runs **more secretive**, then **softer** (grave, silent where Verne's man speaks), with a touch of **more careless** at C9. Each beat in the action (C1, C3, C6, C10, C14, C16) is his own. The turn comes in C12, C13 and C17: keeping the find from Smith and from the others, and never speaking of it, is what Verne's reader would not know. Verne's Pencroff shouts his hopes and his plans (I.19 ¶21, II.10 ¶46), trusts Smith without reserve, and is the one a secret is kept from. If the three are brought to "a delay, with a reason, told after", the man is Verne's.
