# The Brig with the Black Flag (final)

Part III / THE SECRET OF THE ISLAND

CHAPTER XLIII.

LOSS OR SAFETY?—AYRTON SUMMONED—AN IMPORTANT DISCUSSION—IT IS NOT THE DUNCAN—A SUSPICIOUS VESSEL—PRECAUTIONS TO BE TAKEN—THE SHIP APPROACHES—A CANNON-SHOT—THE BRIG ANCHORS IN SIGHT OF THE ISLAND—NIGHT COMES ON.

For two years and a half the castaways of the balloon had been thrown upon Lincoln Island, and up to that time no communication had been possible between them and their fellow-men. Once the reporter had tried to put himself in communication with the inhabited world, by intrusting to a bird the notice which contained the secret of their situation; but that was a chance on which it was impossible to count seriously. Ayrton alone, and under the circumstances which are known, had come to join the members of the little colony. Now, on this very day, the 17th of October, other men had unexpectedly appeared in sight of the island, upon that ever-deserted sea!

There could be no more doubt! A ship was there! But would she pass out to sea, or would she put in? Before a few hours were over, the colonists would evidently know what to expect.

Smith and Herbert, having at once called Spilett, Pencroff, and Neb into the great hall of Granite House, had told them what was going on. Pencroff, seizing the telescope, swept the horizon rapidly, and, stopping on the point indicated, that is to say, on the one which had made the imperceptible spot on the photographic plate, said:—

"A thousand devils! It is indeed a ship!" and his voice betrayed no extraordinary satisfaction.

"Is she coming towards us?" asked Spilett.

"It is impossible to say anything yet," answered Pencroff, "for only her masts appear above the horizon, and not a bit of her hull can be seen!"

"What must we do?" said the lad.

"Wait," answered Smith.

And for a rather long time the colonists remained silent, given up to all the thoughts, all the emotions, all the fears, all the hopes, which this incident could raise in them,—the gravest which had occurred since their arrival on Lincoln Island.

Certainly the colonists were not in the situation of those castaways abandoned on a barren islet, who dispute their miserable existence with a stepmother nature, and are incessantly devoured by the longing to see inhabited lands again. Pencroff and Neb above all, who found themselves at once so happy and so rich, would not have left their island without regret. They were used, besides, to this new life, in the midst of this domain which their intelligence had, so to speak, civilized! But after all, this ship meant, in any case, news from the continent; it was perhaps a bit of their native land which was coming to meet them! She carried beings like themselves, and it will be understood that their hearts beat high at the sight of her!

From time to time Pencroff took up the glass again and posted himself at the window. From there he examined with extreme attention the vessel, which was at a distance of twenty miles to the east. The colonists had therefore as yet no means of signalling their presence. A flag would not have been perceived; a gun would not have been heard; a fire would not have been visible.

However, it was certain that the island, dominated by Mount Franklin, could not have escaped the eyes of the ship's look-outs. But why should this vessel put in there? Was it not mere chance which drove her into this part of the Pacific, where the charts mentioned no land save Tabor Island? And Tabor itself lay out of the routes usually followed by the long-voyage ships from the Polynesian archipelagos, from New Zealand, and from the American coast.

To this question, which each was asking himself, an answer was suddenly made by Herbert.

"Can it be the Duncan?" cried he.

The Duncan, it will not have been forgotten, was the yacht of Lord Glenarvan, which had abandoned Ayrton on the islet, and which was to return one day to fetch him. Now, the islet was not so far from Lincoln Island but that a vessel steering for the one might pass in sight of the other. Only a hundred and fifty miles separated them in longitude, and seventy-five miles in latitude.

"We must let Ayrton know," said Spilett, "and send for him at once. He alone can tell us if it is the Duncan."

This was the opinion of all, and the reporter, going to the telegraphic apparatus which put the corral in communication with Granite House, sent this telegram:—

"Come with all haste."

A few moments after, the bell sounded.

"I am coming," answered Ayrton.

Then the colonists went on observing the ship.

"If it is the Duncan," said Herbert, "Ayrton will know her without difficulty, since he sailed on board of her for some time."

"And if he knows her," added Pencroff, "it will be a famous emotion for him!"

"Yes," answered Smith; "but now Ayrton is worthy to go on board the Duncan again. And Heaven grant that it may indeed be Lord Glenarvan's yacht, for any other ship would seem suspicious to me! These seas are ill frequented, and I have always feared for our island a visit from some Malay pirates."

"We should defend it!" cried Herbert.

"No doubt, my boy," answered the engineer, smiling, "but it is better not to have to defend it."

"One simple observation," said Spilett. "Lincoln Island is unknown to navigators, since it is not even marked on the most recent charts. Do you not think, then, Cyrus, that that is a reason why a ship, finding herself unexpectedly in sight of this new land, should seek to visit it rather than to flee from it?"

"Certainly," answered Pencroff.

"I think so too," added the engineer. "It may even be affirmed that it is the duty of a captain to report, and consequently to come and survey, any land or island not yet catalogued; and Lincoln Island is in that case."

"Well, then," said Pencroff, "suppose that this ship puts in, that she anchors there, a few cables' lengths from our island, what shall we do?"

This question, abruptly put, at first remained without answer. But Smith, after reflecting, replied in the calm tone which was usual with him:—

"What we shall do, my friends, what we ought to do, is this: we will communicate with the ship, we will take passage on board of her, and we will leave our island, after having taken possession of it in the name of the States of the Union. Then we will return to it with all those who are willing to follow us, to colonize it for good, and to endow the American republic with a useful station in this part of the Pacific Ocean!"

"Hurrah!" cried Pencroff, "and it will be no small present that we shall be making to our country there! The colonization is already almost finished; names are given to every part of the island; there is a natural port, a watering-place, roads, a telegraph line, a dockyard, a factory; and there will be nothing left but to put Lincoln Island on the charts!"

"But if it is taken from us during our absence?" observed Spilett.

"A thousand devils!" cried the sailor. "I would rather stay here all alone to guard it; and, on the word of Pencroff, they should not steal it from me as they would a watch from the pocket of a gaping idler!"

For an hour it was impossible to say with certainty whether the vessel was or was not steering towards Lincoln Island. She had drawn nearer to it, however; but on what point of sailing was she going? That was what Pencroff could not make out. However, as the wind was blowing from the north-east, it was likely that the ship was sailing on the starboard tack. Besides, the breeze was fair to carry her towards the approaches of the island; and in this calm sea she had nothing to fear in coming near it, although its soundings were not laid down on the chart.

Towards four o'clock,—an hour after he had been sent for,—Ayrton arrived at Granite House. He entered the great hall, saying:—

"At your service, gentlemen."

Smith gave him his hand, as he was accustomed to do, and, leading him to the window, said:—

"Ayrton, we have begged you to come for a serious reason. A ship is in sight of the island."

Ayrton at first turned slightly pale, and his eyes were troubled for an instant. Then, leaning out of the window, he swept the horizon, but he saw nothing.

"Take this telescope," said Spilett, "and look well, Ayrton, for it is possible that this ship may be the Duncan, come into these seas to take you home."

"The Duncan!" murmured Ayrton. "Already!"

This last word escaped as if involuntarily from the lips of Ayrton, who let his head fall into his hands.

Did twelve years of abandonment on a desert islet, then, not seem to him a sufficient expiation? Did the repentant criminal not yet feel himself forgiven, whether in his own eyes or in the eyes of others?

"No," said he, "no! it cannot be the Duncan."

"Look, Ayrton," then said the engineer, "for it is important that we should know beforehand what to expect."

Ayrton took the glass and pointed it in the direction indicated. For some minutes he observed the horizon without moving, without uttering a single word. Then:—

"It is indeed a ship," said he, "but I do not think it is the Duncan."

"Why should it not be she?" asked Spilett.

"Because the Duncan is a steam-yacht, and I can see no trace of smoke, either above that vessel or near her."

"Perhaps she is only sailing under canvas?" observed Pencroff. "The wind is fair for the course she seems to be following, and she must have reason to save her coal, being so far from any land."

"It is possible that you may be right, Mr. Pencroff," answered Ayrton, "and that the ship has put out her fires. Let her close with the coast, then, and we shall soon know what to expect."

This said, Ayrton went and sat down in a corner of the great hall, and remained there silent. The colonists still discussed the unknown ship, without Ayrton taking any part in the discussion.

All were then in a state of mind which would not have allowed them to go on with their work. Spilett and Pencroff were singularly nervous, going and coming, unable to keep still. Herbert felt curiosity rather. Neb alone kept his usual calm. Was not his country wherever his master was? As to the engineer, he remained absorbed in his thoughts, and at bottom he dreaded rather than desired the arrival of this ship.

Meanwhile, the vessel had drawn a little nearer to the island. With the help of the glass it had been possible to make out that she was a ship of the long voyage, and not one of those Malay proas which the pirates of the Pacific commonly use. It was therefore permissible to believe that the engineer's apprehensions would not be justified, and that the presence of this vessel in the waters of Lincoln Island was no danger to it. Pencroff, after minute attention, believed he could affirm that the ship was rigged as a brig, and that she was running obliquely towards the coast, on the starboard tack, under her courses, topsails, and topgallant sails. This was confirmed by Ayrton.

But if she held on in that way she must soon disappear behind the point of Claw Cape, for she was heading south-west. To observe her it would then be necessary to go up to the heights of Washington Bay, near Balloon Harbor. This was an unfortunate circumstance, for it was already five o'clock in the evening, and the twilight would soon make any observation very difficult.

"What shall we do when night comes?" asked Spilett. "Shall we light a fire to signal our presence on this coast?"

That was a serious question; and yet, whatever presentiments the engineer may have kept, it was settled in the affirmative. During the night the ship might disappear, might go away for ever; and, this ship once gone, would another ever come again into the waters of Lincoln Island? And who could foresee what the future held for the colonists?

"Yes," said the reporter, "we must let this vessel, whatever she may be, know that the island is inhabited. To neglect the chance which is offered us would be to lay up regrets for ourselves hereafter!"

It was therefore decided that Neb and Pencroff should go to Balloon Harbor, and that there, once night had come, they should light a great fire, the blaze of which would necessarily attract the attention of the brig's crew.

But at the moment when Neb and the sailor were preparing to leave Granite House, the vessel altered her course, and bore away and ran straight for the island, heading for Union Bay. She was a good sailer, this brig, for she came on rapidly.

Neb and Pencroff then put off their departure, and the glass was put into Ayrton's hands, so that he might make out once for all whether this ship was or was not the Duncan. The Scotch yacht was also rigged as a brig. The question was therefore whether a funnel rose between the two masts of the vessel observed, which was now no more than ten miles off.

The horizon was still very clear. The verification was easy, and Ayrton soon let the glass fall again, saying:—

"It is not the Duncan! It could not be she!..."

Pencroff again brought the brig into the field of the telescope, and he made out that this brig, of from three to four hundred tons burden, wonderfully sharp, boldly masted, admirably built for speed, must be a swift runner of the seas. But to what nation did she belong? That was hard to say.

"And yet," added the sailor, "a flag is flying at her gaff, but I cannot make out its colors."

"Within half an hour we shall be certain of that," answered the reporter. "Besides, it is quite evident that the captain of this ship means to put in; and consequently, if not to-day, then to-morrow at the latest, we shall make his acquaintance."

"No matter!" said Pencroff. "It is better to know whom one has to deal with, and I should not be sorry to make out that fellow's colors!"

And, while speaking thus, the sailor did not leave his glass.

The day was beginning to fail, and with the day the wind from the offing was falling too. The brig's flag, less stretched, became entangled in the halyards, and it grew more and more difficult to observe.

"That is not an American flag," Pencroff said from time to time, "nor an English one, the red of which could easily be seen, nor the French or German colors, nor the white flag of Russia, nor the yellow of Spain... One would say it is of one uniform color... Let us see... in these seas... what should we find most commonly?... the Chilian flag? But that is tricolor... Brazilian? It is green... Japanese? It is black and yellow... whereas this one..."

At that moment a breeze stretched out the unknown flag. Ayrton, seizing the glass which the sailor had let fall, put it to his eye, and in a hollow voice:—

"The black flag!" cried he.

In fact, a dark bunting was unfolding at the brig's gaff, and it was with good reason that she might now be held for a suspicious ship!

Had the engineer been right, then, in his presentiments? Was this a pirate vessel? Was she scouring these low seas of the Pacific, in rivalry with the Malay proas which still infest them? What was she coming to seek on the approaches of Lincoln Island? Did she see in it an unknown land, unheeded, fit to become a receiver of stolen cargoes? Was she coming to ask of these coasts a port of refuge for the winter months? Was the honest domain of the colonists destined to be turned into an infamous refuge,—a sort of capital of the piracy of the Pacific?

All these ideas presented themselves instinctively to the minds of the colonists. There was no doubt, moreover, of the meaning which ought to be attached to the color of the flag hoisted. It was indeed that of the sea-robbers! It was the one which the Duncan would have carried, had the convicts succeeded in their criminal designs!

No time was lost in discussion.

"My friends," said Smith, "perhaps this ship only wishes to observe the shore of the island? Perhaps her crew will not land? That is a chance. However that may be, we must do everything to hide our presence here. The mill, set up on Prospect Plateau, is too easily recognized. Let Ayrton and Neb go and take down its sails. Let us likewise hide the windows of Granite House under thicker branches. Let all the fires be put out. Let nothing, in short, betray the presence of man on this island!"

"And our boat?" said Herbert.

"Oh!" answered Pencroff, "she is sheltered in Balloon Harbor, and I defy those rascals to find her there!"

The engineer's orders were immediately carried out. Neb and Ayrton went up to the plateau, and took the necessary measures for hiding every sign of habitation. While they were busy at this task, their companions went to the edge of Jacamar Wood, and brought back a great quantity of branches and creepers, which should, at a certain distance, look like natural foliage, and veil pretty well the openings in the granite wall. At the same time the ammunition and arms were placed so that they could be used at the first instant, in case of an unexpected attack.

When all these precautions had been taken:—

"My friends," said Smith,—and one could feel by his voice that he was moved,—"if these wretches mean to seize Lincoln Island, we shall defend it, shall we not?"

"Yes, Cyrus," answered the reporter, "and, if need be, we will all die to defend it!"

The engineer held out his hand to his companions, who pressed it warmly.

Ayrton alone, who had stayed in his corner, had not joined the colonists. Perhaps he, the former convict, still felt himself unworthy!

Smith understood what was passing in Ayrton's soul, and, going to him:—

"And you, Ayrton," he asked, "what will you do?"

"My duty," answered Ayrton.

Then he went and posted himself near the window, and his gaze plunged through the foliage.

It was then half-past seven. The sun had disappeared about twenty minutes before, behind Granite House. Consequently the eastern horizon was darkening little by little. Meanwhile the brig still came on towards Union Bay. She was then not more than eight miles off, and exactly abreast of Prospect Plateau; for, after going about off Claw Cape, she had gained well to the north, helped by the current of the rising tide. It may even be said that at this distance she had already entered the vast bay, for a straight line drawn from Claw Cape to Mandible Cape would have lain to the west of her, on her starboard quarter.

Was the brig going to run into the bay? That was the first question. Once in the bay, would she anchor there? That was the second. Would she not content herself with observing the shore, and then stand out to sea again without landing her crew? They would know within an hour. The colonists had nothing to do, then, but wait.

Smith had not seen the suspicious vessel hoist the black flag without deep anxiety. Was it not a direct threat against the work which his companions and he had carried through until then? Had the pirates,—for it could not be doubted that the seamen of this brig were such,—already frequented this island, since on making land there they had hoisted their colors? Had they formerly made some landing upon it, which would have explained certain peculiarities that had remained inexplicable till then? Was there, in its parts not yet explored, some accomplice ready to enter into communication with them?

To all these questions, which he put to himself in silence, Smith knew not what to answer; but he felt that the situation of the colony could only be very seriously compromised by the arrival of this brig.

However, his companions and he were determined to resist to the last extremity. Were these pirates numerous, and better armed than the colonists? That was what it would have been very important to know! But how to get at them!

Night had come. The new moon, carried away in the radiance of the sun, had disappeared. A deep darkness wrapped the island and the sea. The heavy clouds, piled up on the horizon, let no glimmer filter through. The wind had fallen completely with the twilight. Not a leaf stirred on the trees, not a ripple murmured on the beach. Nothing could be seen of the ship; all her lights were masked; and, if she was still in sight of the island, it could not even be known what place she occupied.

"Eh! who knows?" then said Pencroff. "Perhaps this confounded vessel will have got under way during the night, and we shall not find her again at daybreak?"

As if in answer to the sailor's remark, a bright flash shot out in the offing, and a cannon-shot rang out.

The ship was still there, and there were pieces of artillery on board.

Six seconds had passed between the flash and the report.

Therefore the brig was about a mile and a quarter from the coast.

And at the same moment a noise of chains was heard, running and grinding through the hawse-holes.

The ship had just anchored in sight of Granite House!
