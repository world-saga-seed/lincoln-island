CHAPTER XIX.

CYRUS SMITH’S PLAN—THE FRONT OF GRANITE HOUSE—THE ROPE LADDER—PENCROFF’S DREAMS—AROMATIC HERBS—A NATURAL WARREN—DIVERSION OF THE WATERS FOR THE NEEDS OF THE NEW DWELLING—VIEW FROM THE WINDOWS OF GRANITE HOUSE.

On the morrow, May 22, were commenced the works destined for the special fitting-up of the new dwelling. The colonists were impatient, indeed, to exchange their insufficient shelter of the Chimneys for this vast and healthful retreat, hollowed out of the solid rock, sheltered from the waters of the sea and of the sky. The Chimneys were not to be entirely abandoned, however, and the engineer’s project was to make of them a workshop for heavy labors.

Cyrus Smith’s first care was to ascertain at what precise point the front of Granite House was situated. He repaired to the beach, at the foot of the enormous wall, and, as the pick which had slipped from the reporter’s hands must have fallen perpendicularly, it was enough to find this pick again in order to recognize the spot where the hole had been pierced in the granite.

The pick was easily found, and, indeed, a hole opened in a perpendicular line above the point where it had stuck into the sand, about eighty feet above the beach. Some rock-pigeons were already entering and leaving by this narrow opening. It truly seemed as if it were for them that Granite House had been discovered!

The engineer’s intention was to divide the right-hand portion of the cavern into several chambers preceded by an entrance passage, and to light it by means of five windows and a door pierced in the front. Pencroff readily admitted the five windows, but he did not understand the utility of the door, since the old outlet offered a natural staircase, by which it would always be easy to gain access to Granite House.

“My friend,” answered Cyrus Smith, “if it is easy for us to reach our dwelling by the outlet, it will be equally easy for others than ourselves. I intend, on the contrary, to obstruct this outlet at its orifice, to stop it up hermetically, and, if need be, even to conceal its entrance completely by causing, by means of a dam, a rise in the waters of the lake.”

“And how shall we enter?” asked the sailor.

“By an exterior ladder,” answered Cyrus Smith, “a rope ladder, which, once drawn up, will render access to our dwelling impossible.”

“But why so many precautions?” said Pencroff. “Hitherto the animals have not seemed to us to be very formidable. As for being inhabited by natives, our island is not!”

“Are you quite sure of that, Pencroff?” asked the engineer, looking at the sailor.

“We shall only be sure of it, evidently, when we have explored it in all its parts,” answered Pencroff.

“Yes,” said Cyrus Smith, “for we know as yet only a small portion of it. But, in any case, if we have no enemies within, they may come from without, for these regions of the Pacific are dangerous waters. Let us therefore take our precautions against every eventuality.”

Cyrus Smith spoke wisely, and, without making any further objection, Pencroff prepared to execute his orders.

The front of Granite House was thus to be lighted by means of five windows and a door, serving what constituted “the apartment” properly so called, and by means of a wide bay and bull’s-eyes which would allow light to enter in profusion into that marvelous nave which was to serve as the large hall. This front, situated at a height of eighty feet above the ground, faced the east, and the rising sun saluted it with its first rays. It extended along that portion of the curtain wall comprised between the projection forming an angle at the mouth of the Mercy, and a line drawn perpendicularly above the heap of rocks which formed the Chimneys. Thus the bad winds, that is to say those from the northeast, struck it only obliquely, for it was protected by the very orientation of the projection. Moreover, while waiting for the window-frames to be made, the engineer intended to close the openings with thick shutters, which would let through neither wind nor rain, and which he could conceal in case of need.

The first work consisted, therefore, in hollowing out these openings. The working of the pick upon this hard rock would have been too slow, and it is well known that Cyrus Smith was a man of great resources. He still had a certain quantity of nitro-glycerine at his disposal, and he employed it usefully. The effect of the explosive substance was suitably localized, and, under its force, the granite gave way at the very places chosen by the engineer. Then the pick and the mattock finished the ogive design of the five windows, of the vast bay, of the bull’s-eyes, and of the door; they dressed their casings, the profiles of which were somewhat capriciously shaped, and, a few days after the commencement of the works, Granite House was broadly lighted by that eastern light, which penetrated even into its most secret depths.

According to the plan decided upon by Cyrus Smith, the apartment was to be divided into five compartments looking out upon the sea: on the right, an entrance served by a door to which the ladder would lead, then a first room-kitchen, thirty feet wide, a dining-room, measuring forty feet, a sleeping-chamber, of equal width, and finally a “guest-chamber,” demanded by Pencroff, and which adjoined the large hall.

These rooms, or rather this suite of rooms, which formed the apartment of Granite House, were not to occupy the entire depth of the cavity. They were to be served by a corridor contrived between them and a long store-room, in which the utensils, provisions, and reserves would find ample space. All the products gathered in the island, those of the flora as well as those of the fauna, would be there in excellent conditions of preservation, and completely sheltered from dampness. Space was not wanting, and each object could be methodically arranged. Besides, the colonists still had at their disposal the small grotto situated above the great cavern, which would be like the garret of the new dwelling.

This plan decided upon, nothing remained but to put it into execution. The miners therefore became brick-makers again; then the bricks were brought and deposited at the foot of Granite House.

Hitherto Cyrus Smith and his companions had had access to the cavern only by the old outlet. This mode of communication obliged them first to ascend to Prospect Plateau by making a detour along the bank of the river, to descend two hundred feet by the passage, and then to climb up as much again when they wished to return to the plateau. Hence, loss of time and considerable fatigue. Cyrus Smith resolved, therefore, to proceed without delay to the fabrication of a stout rope ladder, which, once hauled up, would render the entrance to Granite House absolutely inaccessible.

This ladder was made with extreme care, and its side-ropes, formed of the fibers of the “curry-rush” braided by means of a reel, had the strength of a thick cable. As for the rungs, a species of red cedar, with light and tough branches, furnished them, and the apparatus was wrought with a master-hand by Pencroff.

Other ropes were likewise manufactured from vegetable fibers, and a sort of rude tackle was installed at the door. In this way the bricks could be easily hoisted up to the level of Granite House. The transport of materials was thus greatly simplified, and the interior fitting-up, properly so called, began immediately. Lime was not lacking, and several thousand bricks were there, ready to be used. The framework of the partitions, very rudimentary moreover, was easily erected, and, in a very short time, the apartment was divided into rooms and store-room, according to the agreed plan.

These various works were carried on rapidly, under the direction of the engineer, who himself handled the hammer and the trowel. No manual labor was foreign to Cyrus Smith, who thus set the example to intelligent and zealous companions. They worked with confidence, gaily even, Pencroff always having a word to make them laugh, now carpenter, now rope-maker, now mason, and communicating his good humor to all this little world. His faith in the engineer was absolute. Nothing could have shaken it. He believed him capable of undertaking everything and succeeding in everything. The question of clothes and shoes—a serious question, assuredly—that of lighting during the winter nights, the cultivation of the fertile portions of the island, the transformation of this wild flora into a civilized flora, all seemed easy to him with Cyrus Smith’s aid, and all would be done in its own good time. He dreamed of canalized rivers, facilitating the transport of the riches of the soil, of the working of quarries and mines to be undertaken, of machines suited to all industrial practices, of railroads, yes, of railroads! whose network would certainly cover Lincoln Island one day.

The engineer let Pencroff talk on. He checked none of the exaggerations of this brave heart. He knew how contagious confidence is; he even smiled to hear him speak, and said nothing of the anxieties which the future sometimes inspired in him. Indeed, in this part of the Pacific, out of the track of vessels, he might fear never to be rescued. It was upon themselves, therefore, upon themselves alone, that the colonists had to count, for the distance from Lincoln Island to any other land was such that to venture forth in a boat, of necessarily mediocre construction, would be a serious and perilous thing.

“But,” as the sailor said, “they surpassed by a hundred cubits the Robinsons of old, for whom everything had to be done by miracle.”

And indeed, they “knew,” and the man who “knows” succeeds where others would vegetate and inevitably perish.

During these works, Herbert distinguished himself. He was intelligent and active; he understood quickly, executed well, and Cyrus Smith became more and more attached to this boy. Herbert felt for the engineer a lively and respectful friendship. Pencroff clearly saw the close sympathy which was forming between these two beings, but he was not at all jealous of it.

Neb was Neb. He was what he would always be—courage, zeal, devotion, self-abnegation personified. He had the same faith in his master as Pencroff, but he manifested it less noisily. When the sailor grew enthusiastic, Neb always seemed to answer him: “Why, nothing is more natural.” Pencroff and he loved each other dearly, and had not been long in addressing each other like old comrades.

As for Gideon Spilett, he took his share of the common labor, and was not the most awkward—at which the sailor was always a little astonished. A “journalist” clever, not only at understanding everything, but at executing everything!

The ladder was finally installed on May 28. Not fewer than a hundred rungs were counted on this perpendicular height of eighty feet which it measured. Cyrus Smith had fortunately been able to divide it into two parts, by taking advantage of a projection of the wall which jutted out about forty feet above the ground. This ledge, carefully leveled with the pick, became a sort of landing to which was fastened the first ladder, whose swaying was thus diminished by half, and which a rope permitted them to draw up to the level of Granite House. As for the second ladder, it was secured both at its lower end, which rested on the ledge, and at its upper end, fastened to the door itself. In this manner, the ascent became notably easier. Besides, Cyrus Smith intended to install later a hydraulic elevator which would save all fatigue and all loss of time to the inhabitants of Granite House.

The colonists quickly accustomed themselves to using this ladder. They were nimble and adroit, and Pencroff, in his capacity as a sailor, accustomed to run up the ratlines of the shrouds, was able to give them lessons. But he had to give them to Top as well. The poor dog, with his four paws, was not built for this exercise. Yet Pencroff was so zealous a master that Top ended by performing his ascents creditably, and soon climbed the ladder as his fellows commonly do in circuses. How proud the sailor was of his pupil cannot be told. Nevertheless, and more than once, Pencroff carried him up on his back, of which Top never complained.

It will be observed here that during these works, which were actively pushed forward, however, for the bad season was approaching, the question of food had not been neglected. Every day, the reporter and Herbert, now decidedly become the purveyors of the colony, spent several hours in hunting. They as yet exploited only the Jacamar Woods, on the left bank of the river, for, for want of a bridge and a boat, the Mercy had not yet been crossed. All those immense forests to which had been given the name of the Forests of the Far West were therefore not explored. This important excursion was reserved for the first fine days of the coming spring. But the Jacamar Woods were sufficiently stocked with game; kangaroos and wild boars abounded there, and the iron-tipped spears, the bow and the arrows of the hunters did wonders. Moreover, Herbert discovered, toward the southwest angle of the lagoon, a natural warren, a sort of slightly damp meadow, covered with willows and aromatic herbs which perfumed the air, such as thyme, wild thyme, basil, savory—all fragrant species of the labiate family, of which rabbits show themselves so fond.

Upon the reporter’s observation that, since the table was set for rabbits, it would be astonishing if rabbits were lacking, the two hunters carefully explored this warren. In any case, it produced useful plants in abundance, and a naturalist would have had there the opportunity of studying many specimens of the vegetable kingdom. Herbert thus gathered a certain quantity of shoots of basil, rosemary, balm, betony, etc., which possess various therapeutic properties, some pectoral, astringent, febrifugal, others antispasmodic or antirheumatic. And when, later, Pencroff asked of what use all this harvest of herbs would be:

“To doctor ourselves,” answered the boy, “to treat ourselves when we are sick.”

“Why should we be sick, since there are no doctors on the island?” answered Pencroff very seriously.

To that there was nothing to reply, but the boy nonetheless made his harvest, which was very well received at Granite House. All the more so as to these medicinal plants he was able to add a notable quantity of *Monarda didyma*, which are known in North America under the name of “Oswego tea,” and produce an excellent beverage.

Finally, that day, by searching thoroughly, the two hunters came upon the true site of the warren. The ground there was perforated like a skimmer.

“Burrows!” cried Herbert.

“Yes,” answered the reporter, “I see them plainly.”

“But are they inhabited?”

“That is the question.”

The question was not long in being solved. Almost immediately, hundreds of little animals, resembling rabbits, fled in all directions, and with such rapidity that Top himself could not outstrip them. Hunters and dog ran in vain; these rodents easily escaped them. But the reporter was quite resolved not to leave the spot before having captured at least half a dozen of these quadrupeds. He wished to stock the larder first of all, even if they should domesticate those that might be taken later. With a few snares set at the mouth of the burrows, the operation could not fail to succeed. But at this moment, no snares, nor anything to make them of. They had therefore to resign themselves to visiting each burrow, to poking it out with a stick, to doing, by dint of patience, what they could not do otherwise.

At last, after an hour of searching, four rodents were taken in their burrows. They were rabbits quite similar to their fellows of Europe, and which are commonly known under the name of “American rabbits.”

The product of the hunt was therefore carried back to Granite House, and it figured at the evening meal. The tenants of this warren were not to be disdained, for they were delicious. This was a precious resource for the colony, and one which seemed as if it must be inexhaustible.

On May 31, the partitions were finished. Nothing remained but to furnish the rooms, which would be the work of the long winter days. A fireplace was built in the first room, which served as a kitchen. The flue intended to carry the smoke outside gave some trouble to the improvised chimney-builders. It seemed simpler to Cyrus Smith to make it of brick-clay; as there could be no thought of giving it an outlet through the upper plateau, a hole was pierced in the granite above the window of the said kitchen, and to this hole the pipe, obliquely directed, led like that of a sheet-iron stove. Perhaps, doubtless even, during the high east winds which beat directly against the front, the chimney would smoke, but these winds were rare, and, besides, Master Neb, the cook, was not so particular.

When these interior arrangements had been completed, the engineer set about obstructing the orifice of the old outlet which led to the lake, so as to forbid all access by that way. Blocks of rock were rolled to the opening and strongly cemented. Cyrus Smith did not yet carry out the project he had formed of submerging this orifice beneath the waters of the lake by bringing them back to their former level by a dam. He contented himself with concealing the obstruction by means of grasses, shrubs, or brushwood, which were planted in the interstices of the rocks, and which the coming spring would cause to grow luxuriantly.

Nevertheless, he utilized the outlet in such a way as to bring to the new dwelling a trickle of the fresh waters of the lake. A small cut, made below their level, produced this result, and this diversion of a pure and inexhaustible spring gave a yield of twenty-five to thirty gallons[^1] a day. Water would therefore never be lacking at Granite House.

At last, all was finished, and it was high time, for the bad season was arriving. Thick shutters permitted the windows of the front to be closed, until the engineer should have time to manufacture window-glass.

Gideon Spilett had very artistically arranged, in the projections of the rock around the windows, plants of varied species, as well as long trailing grasses, and, in this way, the openings were framed in a picturesque verdure of charming effect.

The inhabitants of the solid, healthful, and safe dwelling could not, therefore, but be delighted with their work. The windows allowed their gaze to sweep over a boundless horizon, which the two Mandible Capes closed on the north and Claw Cape on the south. All of Union Bay stretched out magnificently before them. Yes, these worthy colonists had reason to be satisfied, and Pencroff did not stint his praises of what he humorously called “his apartment on the fifth floor above the entresol!”

[^1]: The gallon is equal to about four and a half liters.

CHAPTER XIX *bis*.

THE FIRST OF JUNE—PENCROFF’S LINES—SOMETHING IN THE CHANNEL—A RAINY DAWN—THE SANDBAR AT LOW WATER—WHAT THE BARNACLES HID—THREE LETTERS IN THE GLASS—THE RISING TIDE—A SECRET KEPT.

The morning of the first day of June broke gray and heavy over Lincoln Island. The wind, which had shifted to the northeast during the night, came in damp gusts across Union Bay, driving low clouds before it and curling the waters of the channel into short, muddy waves. Winter had fairly set in, and within Granite House the colonists were busy enough setting their new domain in order.

But Pencroff, like all true sailors, could never stay long between four walls, even when those walls were cut eighty feet up in the living rock. Before the rest of the household had well stirred from their beds of dried wrack-grass, the honest fellow had swung himself down the eighty-foot rope ladder to the beach, with no companion but his own thoughts and the fresh salt breeze. He wanted to visit the set-lines he had laid out two days before at the mouth of the Mercy, just where the river’s current slackened against the shoals of Safety Islet. Neb had plenty of smoked kangaroo and salted boar in the storehouse, to be sure, and the warren at the southwest corner of the lagoon promised rabbits in abundance; yet Pencroff held that a table without fish was no table at all for men who lived within biscuit-toss of the Pacific.

The tide was at the dead low of the morning ebb, and the narrow channel between the mainland and the islet was almost bare, a glistening expanse of wet sandbars, shallow pools, and dark, weed-draped stones. Pencroff walked out along the edge of the river-mouth, his boots sinking an inch into the firm, ripple-marked sand, his keen gray eyes sweeping the water-line with the practiced habit of a man who had spent forty years reading the sea’s face for shoals, driftwood, or signs of weather.

He had barely reached his first stake when his gaze was arrested by an object lying half-way across the channel, on the crest of a low sandbar that the ebb had just uncovered.

It was round, dark, and seemingly about a foot in diameter, embedded to half its depth in the wet sand. At that distance, and in the dim morning light, it might have been a water-worn boulder, a lump of drift-kelp, or the carapace of some large turtle cast up by the night’s swell. Yet there was something in its smooth, regular curve—an unnatural roundness that belonged neither to stone nor to weed—which caught the sailor’s eye and held it.

Pencroff took a dozen long strides toward the bar, then halted abruptly. Even as he watched, a broader tongue of gray water slid over the outer edge of the shoal, hissed softly around the dark sphere, and withdrew, leaving a ring of white froth behind. Another followed, fuller and higher than the first. The flood was already making, and with a brisk northeast breeze behind it, the channel would be waist-deep in twenty minutes and impassable in half an hour.

The sailor stood for a moment, rubbing his chin with his rough palm. He was a man of impulse, bold and open as the day, yet he possessed a certain stubborn pride that was all his own. To go back up the ladder and announce to Cyrus Smith, Gideon Spilett, and the rest that he had spied something strange on the bar, only to find, when they had all come down in expectation, that it was nothing better than a round stone or a rotten gourd, would have mortified him beyond measure. What Pencroff brought home to Granite House he liked to lay upon the table whole and proven, a triumph of his own sharp eyes and ready hands.

He turned back to his lines, drew them in empty—for the fish had not bitten during the night—and re-baited the bone hooks with bits of mussel. By the time he regained the foot of the cliff, the sandbar was already under two feet of turbid water, and nothing showed above the ripples.

When he climbed into the great hall, he found the others gathered about the kitchen fire, where Neb was boiling an infusion of Oswego tea. Pencroff said not a word of what he had seen. All that day, while they worked at shaping wooden frames for the beds and fitting the thick shutters more closely against the rising wind, the sailor kept his counsel. Only late in the afternoon, when he and Herbert went down together to fetch a load of cedar branches from the Chimneys for firewood, did he broach the matter.

“Herbert, my boy,” said he, dropping his voice as they walked along the beach out of earshot of the cliff, “are you a sound sleeper?”

“No, Pencroff,” answered the lad, looking up in some surprise. “Why do you ask?”

“Because tomorrow morning, at the first streak of day, I want you to slip down the ladder with me before the others are astir. The low water will fall about five o’clock. I have a notion the lines will do better at the turn of the tide, and two pairs of hands are better than one.”

Herbert looked at the sailor’s broad, weather-beaten face and saw at once that there was more behind the request than a string of fish. Pencroff’s eyes had that bright, half-secretive twinkle which always meant he had scented some small adventure. The boy asked no questions, however, for he knew the sailor’s humor, and merely nodded with a quiet smile.

“I shall be ready, Pencroff. Wake me when you get up.”

The night came on wild and wet. Rain beat steadily against the closed shutters of Granite House, and the wind howled across the face of the cliff like a pack of wolves. Within the cavern, however, all was dry, warm, and snug; the clay stove-pipe drew tolerably well despite the easterly gusts, and the colonists slept soundly after their day’s labor.

True to his word, Pencroff was awake before dawn. The small oil-lamp in the corridor had burned out, and the hall was pitch-dark save for a faint gray line under the door-sill. He stepped noiselessly across to Herbert’s corner, laid a heavy hand lightly on the boy’s shoulder, and whispered his name. Herbert was on his feet in an instant. Without waking Cyrus Smith, Spilett, or Neb—who, indeed, had plenty of indoor carpentry planned for the morning—the two unfastened the door, lowered the upper section of the rope ladder softly, and descended into the raw, rain-swept morning.

Day was just breaking behind a thick curtain of gray cloud. A fine, steady rain was falling, blurring the outlines of Safety Islet and dimpling the surface of the river. Overhead, the five windows of Granite House were tightly closed with their heavy wooden shutters, and no sound came from within.

“Now, my lad,” said Pencroff, turning up the collar of his coarse jacket, “let us look to the lines first, in case any one opens a shutter.”

They walked down to the stakes at the mouth of the Mercy and hauled in the cords. Once again the hooks came up bare; the heavy surf outside had driven the fish into deeper water. Pencroff coiled the lines over his arm without a word of disappointment, and, jerking his thumb toward the channel, led the way out across the bared flats toward the middle sandbar.

They carried no tools with them. In truth, the colony possessed few enough—only the pickaxe which Spilett had dropped, a spade, a couple of rough hammers, and two knives which Cyrus Smith had forged from the steel halves of Top’s collar. Pencroff had one of these blades in his belt; to have carried a pick or a spade out of the storehouse at that hour would have provoked a dozen questions from the engineer and the reporter, and Pencroff was determined that whatever lay on the bar should be his own discovery first.

The tide was at its lowest ebb, leaving a broad ridge of wet, dark sand exposed midway between the beach and the rocky shore of the islet. As they approached it, Herbert uttered a low exclamation.

“Look, Pencroff! What is that?”

“Aha!” said the sailor, quickening his pace. “So you see it too, my boy! I thought yesterday morning my eyes had played me a trick.”

A few moments later they were standing beside the object, stooping over it in the falling rain.

It was a hollow sphere—or what appeared to be a sphere—a good twelve inches across, composed of thick, dark-green glass, rough and dull from long immersion, and encrusted over more than half its surface with a dense coat of dead barnacles and calcareous shells. Around its girth, deeply embedded in the crevices of the shell-crust, ran a stout, dark-colored cordage, knotted in a heavy net-work of hitches; and from the lower side of this lashing, strands of coarse, half-rotted netting trailed downward into the packed sand of the bar, where they were held fast as if anchored in cement.

Pencroff dropped to one knee and seized the glass globe with both hands, bracing his feet in the wet sand. He tugged with all his considerable strength, trying first to lift it and then to twist it from its bed. It did not yield by a hair’s breadth.

“Thunder!” muttered the sailor, wiping the rain from his forehead. “It is rooted like an oak! The sand has packed over the net beneath it, and the whole thing is as fast as a kedge-anchor in stiff clay.”

He drew his iron knife from his belt and began to probe around the base of the sphere, scraping away the wet sand with the point of the blade. Three or four inches down, his fingers encountered the stiff, sodden meshes of the old net, stretching outward in every direction beneath the bar, and bound tightly to the glass by thick, tarred seizings that lay far below his reach. To cut it free without digging away half a ton of wet sand was manifestly impossible with a six-inch blade.

“What do you make of it, Pencroff?” asked Herbert, kneeling beside him and examining the strange globe with intense curiosity.

“Why, it is a float, my boy—a net-float, as sure as I am a sailor,” answered Pencroff, sitting back on his heels and eyeing it critically. “Many a time off the banks of Newfoundland, and in the North Sea too, I have seen fishermen use floats to buoy their head-ropes. Only, in our country, they use billets of cork, or barrels, or bladders. Glass floats I have heard tell of among the Norwegians and the Portuguese—thick green bottle-glass that will stand the knock of a wave without splitting. Yet I never saw one quite of this build, nor half so heavy. Look at the thickness of that wall where the light catches it!”

Herbert leaned closer, his naturalist’s eye fixed not upon the glass, but upon the thick crust of marine shells that covered its upper hemisphere. He touched one of the conical, chalky plates with his finger-nail; it crumbled slightly at the edge, empty and dry within.

“These barnacles have been dead a long time, Pencroff,” said the boy thoughtfully.

“Dead? To be sure they are dead, since the thing is high and dry at low water.”

“No, I mean long before it washed ashore here,” Herbert explained, tracing the white, furrowed shells with his finger. “These are acorn-barnacles of a deep-water pelagic species—the kind that attach themselves to floating timber or gear that stays constantly at the surface of the open ocean. Look how the young ones have grown over the old, and then all of them perished together, perhaps when the float sank or was dragged under, or before the currents brought it here. It must have been drifting in the sea for years, Pencroff, and come from a great distance.”

The sailor’s face fell a little at these words. Like every castaway, whenever the sea cast any work of human hands upon their shore, his first, unvoiced thought was always of a ship—a vessel passing near Lincoln Island, or wrecked upon some neighboring reef, whose crew might still be reached or whose signals might be answered. Herbert’s quiet observation, spoken without emphasis, dimmed that hope at once. This globe was no relic of yesterday’s storm; it was a waif of the wide, empty Pacific, drifted from some unknown shore thousands of miles away.

Yet Pencroff was not a man to brood long over a disappointment when there was something tangible before his eyes. If it brought no news of a passing ship, it was still a prize—and a rare one! As he knelt there in the drizzle, his practical mind was already reckoning what that thick sphere of green glass would be worth at Granite House. The five windows of their new dwelling were still closed with blind wooden shutters for want of glass; even a single pane of curved green bottle-glass let into the hall shutter would admit the daylight during the long winter storms! Or, better still, freed from its rotten net and slung in fresh cordage, what a buoy it would make for his deep-water lines off the capes, where wooden floats were forever waterlogging or splitting against the rocks!

He said nothing of these plans to Herbert, however. Keeping his own counsel, he picked up a flat, water-worn pebble from the sand and began to tap cautiously at the thickest patch of barnacles near the crown of the globe, where a smooth curve of dark glass showed beneath the lime.

“Let us clean a bit of this crust off,” said he. “Sometimes the makers stamp their mark on the glass when it is blown.”

Herbert found another stone, and for twenty minutes the two worked patiently side by side, chipping and scraping at the stubborn calcareous deposit, careful not to strike too hard lest they crack the glass beneath. The rain fell steadily on their backs, and away to the eastward, beyond Mandible Cape, the gray horizon had darkened to the color of slate; yet neither of them looked up from his task.

At length a patch of smooth, dark-green glass, as broad as a man’s hand, lay clean and wet before them. Near its center, raised slightly in the substance of the glass itself as though pressed into the molten metal by a die, appeared the broken remains of a circular stamp or seal. Most of the rim had been chipped away or worn smooth by the friction of sand and tide, and the middle of the device was obscured by a pit where a large shell had eaten into the surface; but along the upper arc, three large, well-formed capital letters stood out distinctly in relief.

Herbert bent his head close to the glass, squinting through the rain-drops to trace the raised characters with his forefinger.

“There is a B,” he said slowly, “and then a space where the glass is pitted—then an L—and an I.”

Pencroff peered over the boy’s shoulder, his brow knitted in deep concentration, as if by staring hard enough at the three letters he could force them to spell out the name of a ship or a port.

“B—L—I,” repeated the sailor. “What word is that? Is it English? Or French, maybe?”

Herbert shook his head, still tracing the clean, square serifs of the molded letters.

“Latin, I think,” he said simply.

And he said no more. Neither of them could guess what Latin motto or inscription had once encircled that little seal, nor what hands had blown the glass or knotted the net that held it. They lingered over it nevertheless, turning the three letters over in their minds, scraping a little further to the left and right in the vain hope of uncovering another character, until nearly an hour had slipped away—the whole precious hour of dead low water.

All at once, a cold splash of salt water struck Pencroff squarely across the boots, followed immediately by a sharp hiss of foam that washed halfway up the sandbar.

The sailor started to his feet with an oath. While they had been absorbed in deciphering the seal, the wind had backed around to the east-southeast and freshened to a gale. A heavy squall was driving in from the open sea, bringing a wall of gray rain before it, and the flood tide, driven by the wind, was already pouring through the channel in white-crested rollers.

“Up, Herbert! Back to the shore!” cried Pencroff, seizing the boy by the arm. “The flood is upon us, and there is a squall behind it!”

Had they possessed two picks, or even one, they might have dug the float out in twenty minutes when they first reached the bar; five men working together could have wrenched it free, net and all, before the tide turned. Two of them, with nothing but a short iron knife and their bare hands, were helpless against the packed sand and the rising sea.

They waded back through the deepening channel, the water already swirling nearly to their knees, and gained the foot of the great granite cliff just as the squall broke over Union Bay in full fury. The wind shrieked along the face of the wall, whipping the rain into horizontal sheets, and the rope ladder, hanging loose against the rock, danced and swayed wildly in the gusts. To climb eighty feet up that swinging cordage in the first violence of the squall would have been risky work, even for a sailor; so Pencroff drew Herbert into a shallow recess at the base of the cliff, a few paces from the ladder, where an overhang of the granite sheltered them partially from the downpour.

From this refuge they could still look out across the narrow strait toward Safety Islet. The sandbar was now entirely submerged beneath a boiling cauldron of gray surf; yet for several minutes, each time a heavy roller withdrew into the trough, the dark green crown of the glass float could still be seen gleaming wetly amidst the foam, tethered to its buried net like a buoy riding out a gale.

“It will hold till the afternoon ebb,” said Pencroff, shouting above the roar of the wind, though his tone lacked conviction. “A float that has weathered the Pacific for ten years is not going to part its moorings in a June squall.”

Even as the words left his lips, a monstrous green wave, higher and steeper than any that had preceded it, swept in from the bay, broke with a dull thunder directly over the bar, and buried the shoal under ten feet of churning white water. The rotten hemp of the old net, weakened by years of immersion and strained to the utmost by the sudden surge, must have parted at that instant. A moment later, during a brief rift in the driving rain-squall, both Pencroff and Herbert saw a sharp, bright flash of green light leap up amidst the white spray against the black basalt rocks of Safety Islet—a single, brilliant sparkle as the glass sphere was hurled against the reef—and then the curtain of rain closed down again, and nothing more was to be seen.

Neither of them spoke. For ten minutes longer they stood silently beneath the overhang of the cliff until the first fury of the squall had blown itself out toward the forests of the west. Then, drenched to the skin and shivering in the sharp wind, they climbed the eighty rungs of the ladder in single file and entered the hall of Granite House.

It was already early afternoon—for the morning had worn away faster than they knew—and the rest of the colonists were gathered around the table, where Neb had just set out a steaming dish of stewed rabbit and hot Oswego tea.

“Well, Pencroff!” cried Gideon Spilett, looking up from his plate at the two dripping figures. “Where have you and Herbert been all morning? We thought you had gone down only to look at the lines.”

“And so we did, Mr. Spilett,” answered the sailor, shaking the water from his cap with a forced laugh, “and a fine morning’s work we made of it! The lines took nothing, and the sea took the pair of us.”

He said not another word about the sandbar or the glass float, nor did Herbert volunteer a syllable. Even now, though the float was gone, Pencroff could not bring himself to speak of a prize he had spotted and lost through his own delay. He still meant, when better weather returned, to bring home something worth showing and lay it upon the table before them all, a surprise of his own finding.

Two days later, on the morning of June 3, the weather cleared briefly at dawn, and the low tide fell once more with a smooth, quiet sea. Pencroff and Herbert went down the ladder together at daybreak and walked out across the channel.

The storm had reshaped the entire shoal. The ridge of sand where they had knelt had been scoured flat by the surge and heaped up thirty yards farther toward the river-mouth. Not a trace remained of the float, nor of the rotten net that had anchored it, nor even a splinter of green glass among the shingle of Safety Islet.

Pencroff stood for a long moment looking down at the smooth, unbroken sand, then gave a short, dry shake of his head.

“Five of us and the picks,” he muttered, “and we’d have had it.”

Herbert looked out across the quiet water toward the islet, where the morning sun was just touching the wet black rocks.

“We had it, Pencroff,” said the boy quietly. “For an hour.”

The sailor glanced at him, nodded once, and turned back toward the shore. And from that day forward, neither the man nor the boy ever spoke of what they had found on the sandbar—not to Cyrus Smith, not to Spilett, not to Neb, nor even to one another.

CHAPTER XX.

THE RAINY SEASON—THE QUESTION OF CLOTHING—A SEAL HUNT—CANDLE-MAKING—INTERIOR WORK IN GRANITE HOUSE—THE TWO BRIDGES—RETURN FROM A VISIT TO THE OYSTER-BED—WHAT HERBERT FOUND IN HIS POCKET.

The winter season set in earnestly with the month of June, which corresponds to December in the Northern Hemisphere. It began with showers and squalls which succeeded each other without intermission. The guests of Granite House were able to appreciate the advantages of a dwelling which the inclemency of the weather could not reach. The shelter of the Chimneys would have been truly insufficient against the rigors of a winter, and it was to be feared that the high tides, driven by the winds from the open sea, would again make an irruption there. Cyrus Smith even took some precautions, in anticipation of this contingency, in order to preserve, as far as possible, the forge and the furnaces which had been set up there.

During this whole month of June, the time was employed in various labors, which did not exclude either hunting or fishing, and the stores of the larder were abundantly kept up. Pencroff, as soon as he should have the leisure, proposed to set up traps from which he expected the greatest benefit. He had made snares of woody fibres, and not a day passed that the warren did not furnish its contingent of rodents. Neb spent almost all his time in salting or smoking meats, which secured him excellent preserves.

The question of clothing was then very seriously discussed. The colonists had no other garments than those they wore when the balloon cast them upon the island. These clothes were warm and strong; they had taken extreme care of them as well as of their linen, and kept them in a perfect state of cleanliness, but all these would soon need to be replaced. Besides, if the winter proved severe, the colonists would suffer greatly from the cold.

On this point the ingenuity of Cyrus Smith was at fault. He had been obliged to provide for the most pressing needs—to create a dwelling, to secure food—and the cold might overtake him before the question of clothing had been solved. They must therefore resign themselves to passing this first winter without too much complaint. When the fine season came, they would organize a serious hunt after those moufflons whose presence had been noted during the exploration of Mount Franklin, and, once the wool was gathered, the engineer would certainly know how to manufacture warm and strong stuffs—how, he would think over.

“Well, we shall get off with roasting our shins in Granite House!” said Pencroff. “Fuel abounds, and there is no reason to spare it.”

“Besides,” answered Gideon Spilett, “Lincoln Island is not situated in a very high latitude, and it is probable that the winters here are not severe. Did you not tell us, Cyrus, that this thirty-fifth parallel corresponded to that of Spain in the other hemisphere?”

“Doubtless,” replied the engineer, “but some winters are very cold in Spain! Snow and ice—nothing is wanting there, and Lincoln Island may be just as rigorously tried. However, it is an island, and, as such, I hope the temperature will be more moderate.”

“And why, Mr. Cyrus?” asked Herbert.

“Because the sea, my boy, may be considered as an immense reservoir in which are stored the heats of summer. When winter comes, it restores these heats, which secures to regions bordering on the oceans an average temperature less high in summer, but less low in winter.”

“We shall see,” answered Pencroff. “For my part, I ask not to trouble myself further about the cold that will or will not come. What is certain is that the days are already short and the evenings long. Suppose we take up the question of lighting a little.”

“Nothing is easier,” answered Cyrus Smith.

“To take up?” asked the sailor.

“To solve.”

“And when shall we begin?”

“To-morrow, by organizing a seal hunt.”

“To make tallow candles?”

“Fie, Pencroff! wax candles.”

Such, in fact, was the engineer’s project—a practicable project, since he had lime and sulphuric acid, and the amphibians of the islet would furnish him the fat necessary for their manufacture.

It was the 4th of June. It was Whit-Sunday, and there was a unanimous agreement to observe this festival. All work was suspended, and prayers ascended to heaven. But these prayers were now thanksgivings. The colonists of Lincoln Island were no longer the wretched castaways thrown upon the islet. They no longer petitioned; they gave thanks.

The next day, June 5, in rather uncertain weather, they set out for the islet. They were still obliged to take advantage of low tide to ford the channel, and, in this connection, it was agreed that they would build, after a fashion, a boat which would make communication easier, and would also allow them to ascend the Mercy at the time of the grand exploration of the southwest of the island, which was put off until the first fine days.

The seals were numerous, and the hunters, armed with their iron-tipped spears, easily killed half a dozen. Neb and Pencroff skinned them, and carried back to Granite House only their fat and their hides, these hides being intended for the manufacture of strong foot-gear.

The result of this hunt was about three hundred pounds of fat, which was to be entirely used in the manufacture of candles.

The operation was extremely simple, and, if it did not yield absolutely perfect products, at least they were serviceable. Had Cyrus Smith had nothing at his disposal but sulphuric acid, by heating this acid with neutral fatty bodies—in this case, seal fat—he could have isolated the glycerine; then, from the new combination, he would easily have separated the oleine, the margarine, and the stearine by employing boiling water. But, in order to simplify the operation, he preferred to saponify the fat by means of lime. In this way he obtained a calcareous soap, easy to decompose by sulphuric acid, which precipitated the lime in the state of sulphate and set free the fatty acids.

Of these three acids—oleic, margaric, and stearic—the first, being liquid, was expelled by sufficient pressure. As to the other two, they formed the very substance which was to serve for molding the candles.

The operation did not last more than twenty-four hours. The wicks, after several trials, were made of vegetable fibres, and, dipped in the liquefied substance, they formed genuine stearic candles, molded by hand, which lacked nothing but bleaching and polishing. They did not, doubtless, offer that advantage which wicks impregnated with boric acid have of vitrifying in proportion as they burn, and of consuming themselves entirely; but Cyrus Smith having made a fine pair of snuffers, these candles were greatly appreciated during the evenings at Granite House.

Throughout this month, work was not lacking in the interior of the new abode. The joiners had plenty to do. They improved the tools, which were very rudimentary. They also added to their number.

Scissors, among other things, were made, and the colonists were at last able to cut their hair, and, if not to shave their beards, at least to trim them to their fancy. Herbert had none, Neb had scarcely any, but their companions were so bristling with them as to justify the making of the said scissors.

The manufacture of a handsaw, of the kind called compass-saws, cost infinite pains, but at last they obtained an instrument which, vigorously handled, could divide the woody fibres of timber. They therefore made tables, seats, cupboards, which furnished the principal chambers, and bedsteads, all the bedding of which consisted of mattresses of sea-wrack. The kitchen, with its shelves on which rested the earthenware utensils, its brick stove, its sink, had a very good appearance, and Neb worked there gravely, as if he had been in a chemist’s laboratory.

But the joiners had soon to be replaced by the carpenters. In fact, the new outlet, created by blasting, rendered necessary the construction of two small bridges, one on Prospect Plateau, the other on the beach itself. Now, indeed, the plateau and the beach were cut transversely by a watercourse which had necessarily to be crossed when they wished to reach the north of the island. To avoid it, the colonists would have been obliged to make a considerable detour and go up westward beyond the sources of Red Creek. The simplest way, therefore, was to throw across the plateau and the beach two bridges, from twenty to twenty-five feet long, of which a few trees, merely squared with the axe, formed the entire framework. It was the work of a few days. The bridges built, Neb and Pencroff took advantage of them to go as far as the oyster-bed which had been discovered off the downs. They dragged with them a sort of rude cart, which replaced the old hurdle, really too inconvenient, and they brought back some thousands of oysters, whose acclimatization took place rapidly amid those rocks which formed so many natural preserves at the mouth of the Mercy. These mollusks were of excellent quality, and the colonists made an almost daily consumption of them.

As we see, Lincoln Island, although its inhabitants had explored only a very small portion of it, already supplied almost all their wants. And it was probable that, searched to its most secret recesses, throughout all that wooded part which extended from the Mercy to Reptile Promontory, it would lavish new treasures upon them.

A single privation still tried the colonists of Lincoln Island. Azotized food was not wanting to them, nor the vegetable products which should temper its use; the woody roots of the dragon-trees, subjected to fermentation, gave them an acidulated beverage, a kind of beer far preferable to pure water; they had even made sugar, without cane or beets, by collecting that liquor which the *acer saccharinum* distills—a species of maple of the family of *acerineæ*, which thrives in all temperate zones, and of which the island possessed a great number; they made a very agreeable tea by using the monardas brought from the warren; finally, they had in abundance salt, the only mineral product which enters into food—but bread was wanting.

Perhaps, later on, the colonists might replace this food by some equivalent, sago-flour or bread-fruit starch, and it was possible, indeed, that the southern forests counted these precious trees among their species, but hitherto they had not been met with.

However, Providence was, in this circumstance, to come directly to the aid of the colonists, in an infinitesimal proportion, it is true, but, after all, Cyrus Smith, with all his intelligence, all his ingenuity, could never have produced what, by the greatest chance, Herbert found one day in the lining of his jacket, which he was engaged in mending.

That day—it was raining in torrents—the colonists were assembled in the great hall of Granite House, when the boy suddenly cried out:—

“Look, Mr. Cyrus! A grain of wheat!”

And he showed his companions a grain—a single grain—which, from a hole in his pocket, had slipped into the lining of his jacket.

The presence of this grain was explained by the habit Herbert had, when at Richmond, of feeding some ring-doves of which Pencroff had made him a present.

“A grain of wheat?” answered the engineer quickly.

“Yes, Mr. Cyrus, but only one—nothing but one!”

“Well, my boy,” cried Pencroff, smiling, “we are nicely advanced, upon my word! What could we possibly do with a single grain of wheat?”

“We will make bread of it,” answered Cyrus Smith.

“Bread, cakes, tarts!” replied the sailor. “Come! the bread that grain of wheat will furnish will not choke us very soon!”

Herbert, attaching but little importance to his discovery, was about to throw away the grain in question, but Cyrus Smith took it, examined it, saw that it was in good condition, and, looking the sailor full in the face:—

“Pencroff,” he asked him quietly, “do you know how many ears a single grain of wheat can produce?”

“One, I suppose!” answered the sailor, surprised at the question.

“Ten, Pencroff. And do you know how many grains an ear bears?”

“Faith, no.”

“Eighty on an average,” said Cyrus Smith. “Therefore, if we plant this grain, at the first harvest we shall reap eight hundred grains, which at the second will produce six hundred and forty thousand; at the third, five hundred and twelve millions; at the fourth, more than four hundred billions of grains. That is the proportion.”

Cyrus Smith’s companions listened to him without answering. These figures stupefied them. They were exact, however.

“Yes, my friends,” resumed the engineer. “Such are the arithmetical progressions of fruitful nature. And yet, what is this multiplication of the grain of wheat, whose ear bears only eight hundred grains, compared to those poppy plants which bear thirty-two thousand seeds, or those tobacco plants which produce three hundred and sixty thousand? In a few years, without the numerous causes of destruction which arrest their fecundity, these plants would overrun the whole earth.”

But the engineer had not finished his little examination.

“And now, Pencroff,” he resumed, “do you know how many bushels four hundred billions of grains represent?”

“No,” answered the sailor, “but what I do know is that I am only a blockhead!”

“Well, it would make more than three millions, at one hundred and thirty thousand to the bushel, Pencroff.”

“Three millions!” cried Pencroff.

“Three millions.”

“In four years?”

“In four years,” answered Cyrus Smith, “and even in two years, if, as I hope, we can, in this latitude, obtain two harvests a year.”

To this, according to his custom, Pencroff could think of no other reply than a tremendous hurrah.

“Thus, Herbert,” added the engineer, “you have made a discovery of extreme importance to us. Everything, my friends, everything can serve us in the condition in which we are. I beg you not to forget it.”

“No, Mr. Cyrus, no, we will not forget it,” answered Pencroff, “and if ever I find one of those tobacco seeds which multiply by three hundred and sixty thousand, I assure you I will not throw it to the winds! And now, do you know what remains for us to do?”

“It remains for us to plant this grain,” answered Herbert.

“Yes,” added Gideon Spilett, “and with all the respect due to it, for it bears within it our future harvests.”

“Provided it grows!” cried the sailor.

“It will grow,” answered Cyrus Smith.

It was the 20th of June. The time was therefore favorable for sowing this single and precious grain of wheat. At first there was some talk of planting it in a pot; but, upon reflection, they resolved to trust more frankly to nature, and to commit it to the earth. This was done the very same day, and it is needless to add that every precaution was taken that the operation might succeed.

The weather having cleared slightly, the colonists climbed the heights of Granite House. There, on the plateau, they chose a spot well sheltered from the wind, and upon which the midday sun would pour all its heat. The spot was cleared, carefully weeded, even dug over, to drive out insects or worms; they put down a layer of good soil enriched with a little lime; they surrounded it with a palisade; then the grain was pressed into the moist bed.

Did it not seem as if these colonists were laying the first stone of an edifice? It reminded Pencroff of the day when he had lighted his single match, and all the care he had brought to that operation. But this time the matter was more serious. In fact, the castaways would always have succeeded in procuring fire, by one process or another, but no human power could ever have remade that grain of wheat for them, if, by misfortune, it had perished!
