# Measuring the Cliff and the Longitude (draft 1)

CHAPTER XIV.

THE MEASUREMENT OF THE GRANITE WALL—AN APPLICATION OF THE THEOREM OF SIMILAR TRIANGLES—THE LATITUDE OF THE ISLAND—AN EXCURSION TO THE NORTH—AN OYSTER BED—PROJECTS FOR THE FUTURE—THE PASSAGE OF THE SUN OVER THE MERIDIAN—THE COORDINATES OF LINCOLN ISLAND.

The next day, the 16th of April, Easter Sunday, the colonists left the Chimneys at daybreak, and set about washing their linen and cleaning their clothes. The engineer intended to make soap as soon as he had procured the raw materials necessary for saponification, soda or potash, fat or oil. The very important question of renewing their wardrobe would also be dealt with in due time and place. In any case, their clothes would last quite six months longer, for they were strong, and could resist the wear of manual labor. But all would depend on the situation of the island with regard to inhabited lands. This would be determined that very day, if the weather permitted.

Now the sun, rising upon a clear horizon, announced a magnificent day, one of those fine autumn days which are like the last farewells of the warm season.

It was a question, then, of completing the elements of the observations of the day before, by measuring the height of Prospect Plateau above the level of the sea.

“Will you not need an instrument like the one which served you yesterday?” asked Herbert of the engineer.

“No, my boy,” replied the latter; “we are going to proceed in another way, and in a manner almost as precise.”

Herbert, who liked to learn about everything, followed the engineer, who moved away from the foot of the granite wall and went down to the edge of the beach. Meanwhile Pencroff, Neb, and the reporter busied themselves with various work.

Cyrus Smith had provided himself with a sort of straight pole, about twelve feet long, which he had measured as exactly as possible by comparing it with his own height, which he knew to within a line. Herbert carried a plumb line which Cyrus Smith had given him, that is to say, a simple stone fastened to the end of a flexible fibre.

Having arrived about twenty feet from the edge of the beach, and about five hundred feet from the granite wall, which rose perpendicularly, Cyrus Smith drove the pole two feet into the sand; and, wedging it with care, he succeeded, by means of the plumb line, in setting it up perpendicular to the plane of the horizon.

This done, he moved back the distance necessary for the visual ray from his eye, as he lay upon the sand, to graze at once both the top of the pole and the crest of the wall. Then he carefully marked this point with a stake.

Then, addressing Herbert,—

“You know the first principles of geometry?” he asked him.

“A little, Mr. Smith,” replied Herbert, who did not wish to put himself too far forward.

“You remember well what are the properties of two similar triangles?”

“Yes,” replied Herbert. “Their homologous sides are proportional.”

“Well, my boy, I have just constructed two similar triangles, both of them right-angled. The first, the smaller, has for its sides the perpendicular pole and the distance which separates the stake from the foot of the pole, and my visual ray for its hypothenuse; the second has for its sides the perpendicular wall, the height of which we wish to measure, and the distance which separates the stake from the foot of that wall, with my visual ray likewise forming its hypothenuse,—which happens to be the prolongation of that of the first triangle.”

“Ah, Mr. Smith, I understand!” cried Herbert. “Just as the distance from the stake to the pole is proportional to the distance from the stake to the base of the wall, so the height of the pole is proportional to the height of the wall.”

“That is just it, Herbert,” replied the engineer; “and when we have measured the first two distances, knowing the height of the pole, we shall have nothing more than a sum in proportion to work, which will give us the height of the wall, and will save us the trouble of measuring it directly.”

The two horizontal distances were taken by means of the pole itself, the length of which above the sand was exactly ten feet.

The first distance was fifteen feet, between the stake and the point where the pole was driven into the sand.

The second distance, between the stake and the base of the wall, was five hundred feet.

These measurements finished, Cyrus Smith and the lad returned to the Chimneys.

There the engineer took a flat stone which he had brought back from one of his former excursions, a sort of slaty schist, upon which it was easy to trace figures with a sharp shell. He then set out the following proportion:—

15 : 500 :: 10 : x

500 × 10 = 5000

5000 / 15 = 333.33

From which it was established that the granite wall measured three hundred and thirty-three feet in height.(1)

(1) The English foot is meant, which is equal to 30 centimetres.

Cyrus Smith then took up again the instrument which he had made the evening before, the two little boards of which, by their opening, had given him the angular distance of the star alpha from the horizon. He measured very exactly the opening of this angle upon a circle which he divided into three hundred and sixty equal parts. Now this angle, by adding to it the twenty-seven degrees which separate alpha from the antarctic pole, and by reducing to the level of the sea the height of the plateau from which the observation had been made, was found to be fifty-three degrees. These fifty-three degrees being subtracted from ninety degrees,—the distance from the pole to the equator,—there remained thirty-seven degrees. Cyrus Smith concluded, therefore, that Lincoln Island was situated on the thirty-seventh degree of south latitude; or, allowing, on account of the imperfection of his operations, a difference of five degrees, that it must lie between the thirty-fifth and the fortieth parallels.

There remained the longitude to be obtained, in order to complete the coordinates of the island. This the engineer would attempt to determine that very day, at noon, that is to say, at the moment when the sun passed the meridian.

It was decided that this Sunday should be spent in a walk, or rather in an exploration of that part of the island lying between the north of the lake and Shark Gulf; and, if time permitted, they would push the reconnoissance as far as the northern side of South Mandible Cape. They were to breakfast among the dunes, and not to return until evening.

At half-past eight in the morning the little band was following the edge of the channel. On the other side, upon Safety Islet, numerous birds were gravely walking about. They were divers, of the penguin species, easily recognized by their disagreeable cry, which recalls the braying of an ass. Pencroff considered them only from an eatable point of view, and learned, not without a certain satisfaction, that their flesh, though blackish, is very good eating.

Large amphibious creatures could also be seen crawling upon the sand, seals, no doubt, which appeared to have chosen the islet for a refuge. It was hardly possible to consider these animals as food, for their oily flesh is detestable; nevertheless, Cyrus Smith observed them attentively, and, without making known his idea, he announced to his companions that very shortly they would pay a visit to the islet.

The shore which the colonists followed was strewn with innumerable shells, some of which would have delighted a lover of malacology. There were, among others, phasianellae, terebratulae, trigoniae, etc. But what was to be of more use was a vast oyster bed, uncovered at low tide, which Neb pointed out among the rocks, about four miles from the Chimneys.

“Neb will not have lost his day,” cried Pencroff, as he looked at the bank of oysters which stretched out to sea.

“It is a lucky discovery, indeed,” said the reporter; “and if, as is said, each oyster produces from fifty to sixty thousand eggs a year, we shall have there an inexhaustible store.”

“Only I believe that the oyster is not very nourishing,” said Herbert.

“No,” replied Cyrus Smith. “The oyster contains very little nitrogenous matter, and a man who lived on them exclusively would need no less than fifteen or sixteen dozen a day.”

“Good!” replied Pencroff. “We shall be able to swallow dozens of dozens before we have exhausted the bed. Suppose we take some for our breakfast?”

And without waiting for an answer to his proposal, knowing well that it was approved beforehand, the sailor and Neb detached a certain quantity of these mollusks. They were put into a sort of net of hibiscus fibre, which Neb had made, and which already held the materials of the meal; then they continued to ascend the coast between the dunes and the sea.

From time to time Cyrus Smith consulted his watch, so as to be ready in good time for the solar observation, which was to be made exactly at noon.

All this part of the island was very barren, as far as the point which closed Union Bay, and which had received the name of South Mandible Cape. Nothing was to be seen there but sand and shells, mingled with fragments of lava. A few sea birds frequented this desolate coast, gulls, great albatrosses, and also wild ducks, which, with good reason, excited the covetousness of Pencroff. He tried indeed to bring them down with arrows, but without result, for they scarcely ever settled, and it would have been necessary to hit them on the wing.

This led the sailor to repeat to the engineer,—

“You see, Mr. Smith, so long as we have not one or two fowling-pieces, our outfit will leave something to be desired!”

“No doubt, Pencroff,” replied the reporter, “but that rests only with you! Procure us iron for the barrels, steel for the locks, saltpetre, charcoal, and sulphur for the powder, mercury and nitric acid for the fulminate, and lastly lead for the balls, and Cyrus will make us guns of the first quality.”

“Oh!” replied the engineer, “all these substances we may no doubt find on the island; but a firearm is a delicate instrument, and requires tools of great precision. However, we shall see later.”

“Why,” cried Pencroff, “why must we have thrown overboard all those weapons which the car carried with us, and our utensils, and even our pocket-knives!”

“But if we had not thrown them overboard, Pencroff, it is we whom the balloon would have thrown to the bottom of the sea!” said Herbert.

“What you say there is true, all the same, my boy!” replied the sailor.

Then, passing to another idea,—

“But, now I think of it,” he added, “what must have been the amazement of Jonathan Forster and his companions, when, the next morning, they found the place empty and the machine flown!”

“The last of my cares is to know what they may have thought!” said the reporter.

“Yet it was I who had that idea!” said Pencroff, with a satisfied air.

“A fine idea, Pencroff,” replied Spilett, laughing, “and one which has put us where we are!”

“I would rather be here than in the hands of the Southerners!” cried the sailor, “especially since Mr. Smith has had the kindness to come and join us!”

“And so would I, in truth!” replied the reporter. “Besides, what do we want? Nothing!”

“Unless it is—everything!” replied Pencroff, bursting out laughing, and shaking his broad shoulders. “But one day or other we shall find the means of getting away!”

“And sooner, perhaps, than you imagine, my friends,” then said the engineer, “if Lincoln Island is only at a moderate distance from an inhabited archipelago or from a continent. Within an hour we shall know. I have no chart of the Pacific, but my memory has kept a very clear recollection of its southern part. The latitude which I obtained yesterday places Lincoln Island abreast of New Zealand on the west, and of the coast of Chili on the east. But between these two lands the distance is at least six thousand miles. It remains, then, to determine what point the island occupies in this wide space of sea; and that the longitude will give us presently, with sufficient approximation, I hope.”

“Is it not the Low Archipelago,” asked Herbert, “which is nearest to us in latitude?”

“Yes,” replied the engineer, “but the distance which separates us from it is more than twelve hundred miles.”

“And that way?” said Neb, who was following the conversation with extreme interest, and whose hand pointed towards the south.

“That way, nothing,” replied Pencroff.

“Nothing, indeed,” added the engineer.

“Well, Cyrus,” asked the reporter, “and if Lincoln Island is only two or three hundred miles from New Zealand or from Chili?”

“Well,” replied the engineer, “instead of making a house, we will make a boat, and Pencroff shall take charge of sailing her——”

“Why, Mr. Smith,” cried the sailor, “I am quite ready to turn captain—as soon as you have found the means of building a craft fit to keep the sea!”

“We will do it, if it is necessary!” replied Cyrus Smith.

But while these men, who truly doubted nothing, were talking, the hour was approaching at which the observation was to be made. How would Cyrus Smith set about ascertaining the passage of the sun over the meridian of the island, without any instrument? This was what Herbert could not guess.

The observers were then at a distance of six miles from the Chimneys, not far from that part of the dunes in which the engineer had been found after his enigmatic rescue. They halted at this spot, and everything was made ready for breakfast, for it was half-past eleven. Herbert went for fresh water to the stream which flowed near by, and brought it back in a jug with which Neb had provided himself.

During these preparations Cyrus Smith arranged everything for his astronomical observation. He chose upon the shore a clean place, which the retreating sea had levelled perfectly. This bed of very fine sand was as smooth as a mirror, without one grain standing above another. It mattered little, however, whether this bed was horizontal or not, and it mattered no more whether the rod, six feet high, which was planted in it, stood perpendicular. On the contrary, indeed, the engineer inclined it towards the south, that is to say, to the side opposite the sun; for it must not be forgotten that the colonists of Lincoln Island, by the very fact that the island was situated in the southern hemisphere, saw the radiant orb describe its diurnal arc above the northern horizon, and not above the southern horizon.

Herbert then understood how the engineer was going to proceed in order to ascertain the culmination of the sun, that is to say, its passage over the meridian of the island, or, in other words, the noon of the place. It was by means of the shadow cast upon the sand by the rod, a means which, for want of an instrument, would give him a fitting approximation for the result which he wished to obtain.

In fact, the moment when this shadow reached its least length would be exact noon, and it would be enough to follow the end of this shadow in order to recognize the instant when, after having successively diminished, it began to lengthen again. By inclining his rod to the side opposite the sun, Cyrus Smith made the shadow longer, and consequently its changes would be easier to ascertain. Indeed, the longer the hand of a dial, the more easily can the movement of its point be followed. The shadow of the rod was nothing else than the hand of a dial.

When he thought that the moment had come, Cyrus Smith knelt upon the sand, and, by means of little wooden pegs which he stuck into the sand, he began to mark the successive decreases of the shadow of the rod. His companions, bending over him, followed the operation with extreme interest.

The reporter held his chronometer in his hand, ready to note the hour which it would mark when the shadow was at its shortest. Moreover, as Cyrus Smith was operating on the 16th of April, a day on which true time and mean time coincide, the hour given by Spilett would be the true hour at Washington at that moment, which would simplify the calculation.

Meanwhile the sun advanced slowly; the shadow of the rod diminished little by little; and when it appeared to Cyrus Smith that it was beginning to lengthen again,—

“What o’clock?” said he.

“One minute past five,” replied Spilett at once.

There was nothing more to do but to work out the operation. Nothing was easier. There was, as will be seen, in round numbers, a difference of five hours between the meridian of Washington and that of Lincoln Island; that is to say, it was noon at Lincoln Island when it was already five o’clock in the evening at Washington. Now the sun, in its apparent movement round the earth, passes over one degree in four minutes, or fifteen degrees an hour. Fifteen degrees multiplied by five hours gave seventy-five degrees.

Therefore, since Washington is in 77° 3′ 11″, as much as to say seventy-seven degrees counted from the meridian of Greenwich,—which the Americans, together with the English, take as the starting point of longitudes,—it followed that the island was situated seventy-seven degrees plus seventy-five degrees west of the meridian of Greenwich, that is to say, on the hundred and fifty-second degree of west longitude.

Cyrus Smith announced this result to his companions; and, taking into account errors of observation, as he had done for the latitude, he believed that he could affirm that the position of Lincoln Island was between the thirty-fifth and the thirty-seventh parallels, and between the hundred and fiftieth and the hundred and fifty-fifth meridians west of the meridian of Greenwich.

The possible deviation which he attributed to errors of observation was, as will be seen, five degrees in both directions, which, at sixty miles to the degree, might give an error of three hundred miles in latitude or in longitude for the exact bearing.

But this error could not influence the course which it would be proper to take. It was very evident that Lincoln Island was at such a distance from any land or archipelago that they could not risk crossing that distance in a simple and frail canoe.

In fact, his reckoning placed it at least twelve hundred miles from Tahiti and the islands of the Low Archipelago, more than eighteen hundred miles from New Zealand, and more than four thousand five hundred miles from the American coast!

And when Cyrus Smith consulted his memory, he could not in any way recall that any island whatever occupied, in that part of the Pacific, the situation assigned to Lincoln Island.
