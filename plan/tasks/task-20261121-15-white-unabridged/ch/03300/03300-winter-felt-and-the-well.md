# Winter, Felt and the Well (draft 1)

CHAPTER XXXIII.

WINTER—THE FULLING OF THE WOOL—THE MILL—A FIXED IDEA OF PENCROFF’S—THE WHALEBONES—WHAT AN ALBATROSS MAY BE USED FOR—THE FUEL OF THE FUTURE—TOP AND JUP—TEMPESTS—DAMAGE AT THE POULTRY-YARD—AN EXCURSION TO THE MARSH—SMITH ALONE—EXPLORATION OF THE WELL.

Meanwhile winter was coming on with this month of June, which is the December of the northern zones, and the great occupation was the making of warm and strong clothing.

The mouflons of the corral had been stripped of their wool, and it only remained, therefore, to transform this precious textile material into cloth.

It is needless to say that Smith, having at his disposal neither carding-machines, nor combing-machines, nor smoothing-machines, nor drawing-machines, nor twisting-machines, nor “mule-jenny,” nor “self-acting,” to spin the wool, nor loom to weave it, was obliged to proceed in a simpler way, so as to save both the spinning and the weaving. And, in fact, he proposed quite simply to make use of the property which the filaments of wool possess, when they are pressed in all directions, of tangling together, and of forming, by their mere interlacing, that cloth which is called felt. This felt could therefore be obtained by a simple fulling, an operation which, if it lessens the suppleness of the cloth, notably increases its power of keeping in heat. Now, precisely, the wool furnished by the mouflons was made of very short strands, and that is a good condition for felting.

The engineer, aided by his companions, Pencroff included—he was obliged once more to abandon his boat!—began the preliminary operations, the object of which was to rid the wool of that oily and greasy substance with which it is impregnated, and which is called the suint. This scouring was done in vats filled with water, which were raised to a temperature of seventy degrees, and in which the wool was steeped for twenty-four hours. It was afterwards washed thoroughly by means of baths of soda. Then the wool, when it had been sufficiently dried by pressure, was in a state to be fulled, that is to say, to produce a strong cloth. It was coarse, no doubt, and would have had no value in a manufacturing centre of Europe or America; but it was to be held in extreme esteem in the “markets of Lincoln Island.”

It will be understood that this kind of cloth must have been known from the most remote ages; and, in fact, the first woollen cloths were manufactured by the process which Smith was about to employ.

Where his quality of engineer served him well was in the construction of the machine intended to full the wool; for he cleverly took advantage of the mechanical force, unused till then, which the waterfall on the beach possessed, to drive a fulling-mill.

Nothing could be more rudimentary. A shaft furnished with cams, which lifted vertical pestles and let them fall again in turn; troughs intended to receive the wool, into which these pestles fell; a strong timber frame, containing and binding together the whole system: such was the machine in question. And such it had been for centuries, until the moment when the idea came of replacing the pestles by compressing cylinders, and of subjecting the material, no longer to a beating, but to a true rolling.

The operation, well directed by Smith, succeeded to a wish. The wool had first been soaked in a solution of soap, intended, on the one hand, to make easier its sliding, its drawing together, its compression and its softening, and, on the other, to keep it from being damaged by the beating; and it came out of the mill in the form of a thick sheet of felt. The ridges and roughnesses with which the strand of wool is naturally furnished had caught and tangled one in another so well, that they formed a cloth equally fit for making garments or blankets. It was certainly neither merino, nor muslin, nor Scotch cashmere, nor stuff, nor rep, nor China satin, nor Orleans, nor alpaca, nor broadcloth, nor flannel! It was “Lincolnian felt,” and Lincoln Island counted one industry the more.

The colonists had therefore, with good garments, thick blankets, and they could see the winter of 1866-67 coming on without fear.

The great cold began really to make itself felt about the 20th of June, and, to his great regret, Pencroff was obliged to suspend the building of the boat, which, besides, could not fail to be finished by the next spring.

The sailor’s fixed idea was to make a voyage of reconnaissance to Tabor Island, although Smith did not approve of this voyage, which was one of pure curiosity, for there was evidently no help to be found on that desert and half-barren rock. A voyage of a hundred and fifty miles, in a boat comparatively small, in the midst of unknown seas, did not fail to cause him some apprehension. Suppose that the craft, once out at sea, should be unable to reach Tabor, and could not return to Lincoln Island, what would become of her in the midst of this Pacific, so fruitful in disasters?

Smith often talked of this project with Pencroff, and he found in the sailor a rather strange obstinacy in wishing to accomplish this voyage, an obstinacy of which perhaps the sailor himself was not well aware.

“For, after all,” said the engineer to him one day, “I would have you observe, my friend, that after having said so much good of Lincoln Island, after having so often shown the regret you would feel if you were obliged to abandon it, you are the first to wish to leave it.”

“To leave it for a few days only,” replied Pencroff, “for a few days only, Mr. Smith! Time to go and come back, to see what this islet is!”

“But it cannot be worth Lincoln Island!”

“I am sure of that beforehand!”

“Then why venture there?”

“To know what is going on at Tabor Island!”

“But nothing is going on there! Nothing can be going on there!”

“Who knows?”

“And if you are caught in some storm?”

“That is not to be feared in the fine season,” replied Pencroff. “But, Mr. Smith, as one must foresee everything, I shall ask your permission to take only Herbert with me on this voyage.”

“Pencroff,” replied the engineer, laying his hand on the sailor’s shoulder, “if misfortune should happen to you and to this child, whom chance has made our son, do you think that we could ever be consoled?”

“Mr. Smith,” replied Pencroff, with unshaken confidence, “we shall not cause you that grief. Besides, we will speak of this voyage again when the time has come to make it. Then, I fancy that when you have seen our boat well rigged and well fitted, when you have observed how she behaves at sea, when we have made the tour of our island—for we shall make it together—I fancy, I say, that you will no longer hesitate to let me go! I don’t hide from you that she will be a masterpiece, your boat!”

“Say at least our boat, Pencroff!” replied the engineer, disarmed for the moment.

The conversation ended thus, to begin again later, without convincing either the sailor or the engineer.

The first snows fell towards the end of the month of June. The corral had been amply provisioned beforehand, and no longer needed daily visits; but it was decided that a week should never be let pass without going there.

The traps were set again, and trial was made of the engines manufactured by Smith. The bent whalebones, imprisoned in a case of ice and covered with a thick coat of fat, were placed on the edge of the forest, at the spot where the animals usually passed on their way to the lake.

To the engineer’s great satisfaction, this invention, borrowed from the Aleutian fishermen, succeeded perfectly. A dozen foxes, a few wild boars, and even a jaguar, let themselves be taken by it; and these animals were found dead, their stomachs pierced by the unbent whalebones.

Here comes in an attempt which ought to be related, for it was the first trial made by the colonists to communicate with their fellow-creatures.

Spilett had already thought several times either of throwing into the sea a notice shut up in a bottle, which the currents might perhaps carry to an inhabited coast, or of entrusting it to pigeons. But how could they seriously hope that pigeons or bottles could cross the distance which separated the island from any land, and which was twelve hundred miles? It would have been pure folly.

But on the 30th of June an albatross was captured, not without trouble, which a shot from Herbert’s gun had slightly wounded in the foot. It was a magnificent bird of the family of those great sailers whose wings, spread out, measure ten feet from tip to tip, and which can cross seas as wide as the Pacific.

Herbert would have liked to keep this superb bird, whose wound quickly healed, and which he claimed he could tame; but Spilett made him understand that they could not neglect this opportunity of trying to correspond by this courier with the lands of the Pacific. Herbert was obliged to yield, for if the albatross had come from some inhabited region, it would not fail to return there as soon as it was free.

Perhaps, at bottom, Spilett, in whom the chronicler sometimes appeared again, was not sorry to launch at a venture a stirring article relating the adventures of the colonists of Lincoln Island! What a success for the accredited reporter of the New York Herald, and for the number which should contain the chronicle, if ever it reached the address of its director, the honorable John Bennett!

Spilett therefore drew up a concise notice, which was put into a bag of strong gummed canvas, with an urgent request to whoever should find it to forward it to the offices of the New York Herald. This little bag was fastened to the neck of the albatross, and not to its foot, for these birds are in the habit of resting on the surface of the sea. Then liberty was given to this swift courier of the air, and it was not without some emotion that the colonists watched it disappear in the distance among the mists of the west.

“Where is he going to?” asked Pencroff.

“Towards New Zealand,” replied Herbert.

“A good voyage to him!” cried the sailor, who, for his part, did not expect any great result from this mode of correspondence.

With the winter, work had been resumed in the interior of Granite House: the mending of clothes, various manufactures, and among others the sails of the boat, which were cut from the inexhaustible envelope of the balloon...

During the month of July the cold was intense, but neither wood nor coal was spared. Smith had set up a second fireplace in the great hall, and it was there that the long evenings were spent. There was talk while they worked, reading when their hands were idle, and the time passed with profit for every one.

It was a real enjoyment for the colonists when, in this hall well lighted with candles and well warmed with coal, after a comforting dinner, with the elder-berry coffee smoking in the cup and the pipes sending up their plumes of fragrant smoke, they heard the tempest roaring without! They would have known complete well-being, if well-being could ever exist for those who are far from their fellow-creatures and without any possible communication with them! They talked always of their country, of the friends whom they had left, of the greatness of the American republic, whose influence could only increase; and Smith, who had been much mixed up in the affairs of the Union, greatly interested his hearers by his narratives, his views, and his forecasts.

It happened one day that Spilett was led to say to him:—

“But now, my dear Cyrus, all this industrial and commercial movement, for which you predict a constant advance, does it not run the danger of being completely stopped sooner or later?”

“Stopped! And by what?”

“Why, by the want of that coal, which may justly be called the most precious of minerals!”

“Yes, the most precious indeed,” replied the engineer; “and it seems that nature wished to prove that it was so, by making the diamond, which is nothing but pure carbon crystallized.”

“You don’t mean to say, Mr. Smith,” returned Pencroff, “that diamonds will be burned in place of coal in the furnaces of boilers?”

“No, my friend,” replied Smith.

“However, I insist,” resumed Spilett. “You do not deny that some day the coal will be entirely consumed?”

“Oh! the beds of coal are still considerable, and the hundred thousand workmen who tear from them annually a hundred million metric quintals are not near having exhausted them!”

“With the increasing proportion of the consumption of coal,” replied Spilett, “one may foresee that these hundred thousand workmen will soon be two hundred thousand, and that the yield will be doubled?”

“No doubt; but after the beds of Europe, which new machines will soon allow to be worked more thoroughly, the coal-mines of America and Australia will for a long time yet supply the consumption of industry.”

“For how long?” asked the reporter.

“For two hundred and fifty or three hundred years at least.”

“That is reassuring for us,” replied Pencroff, “but alarming for our great-grand-cousins!”

“Something else will be found,” said Herbert.

“It is to be hoped so,” replied Spilett, “for, after all, without coal, no more machines; and without machines, no more railways, no more steamers, no more factories, nothing more of all that the progress of modern life requires!”

“But what will be found?” asked Pencroff. “Can you imagine it, Mr. Smith?”

“Very nearly, my friend.”

“And what will they burn instead of coal?”

“Water,” replied Smith.

“Water!” cried Pencroff; “water to heat steamers and locomotives! water to heat water!”

“Yes, but water decomposed into its constituent elements,” replied Smith, “and decomposed, no doubt, by electricity, which will then have become a powerful and manageable force; for all great discoveries, by some inexplicable law, appear to agree and to complete each other at the same moment. Yes, my friends, I believe that water will one day be employed as fuel, and that hydrogen and oxygen, which constitute it, used singly or together, will furnish an inexhaustible source of heat and light, of an intensity of which coal is not capable. Some day the bunkers of steamers and the tenders of locomotives will be loaded, instead of with coal, with these two gases compressed, which will burn in the furnaces with enormous calorific power. So, then, there is nothing to fear. As long as this earth is inhabited it will supply the wants of its inhabitants, and they will want neither light nor heat, any more than they will want the productions of the vegetable, mineral, or animal kingdoms. I believe, then, that when the beds of coal are exhausted, we shall heat, and warm ourselves, with water. Water is the coal of the future.”

“I should like to see that,” said the sailor.

“You got up too early, Pencroff,” replied Neb, who took part in the discussion only by these words.

However, it was not Neb’s words that ended the conversation, but the barking of Top, which broke out again with that strange intonation which had already given the engineer concern. At the same time Top began again to run round the mouth of the well, which opened at the end of the inner passage.

“What is Top barking like that for again?” asked Pencroff.

“And Jup growling in that way?” added Herbert.

In fact the orang, joining the dog, was giving unequivocal signs of agitation; and, a singular detail, these two animals appeared to be uneasy rather than irritated.

“It is evident,” said Spilett, “that this well is in direct communication with the sea, and that some marine animal comes from time to time to breathe at the bottom.”

“That is evident,” replied the sailor, “and there is no other explanation to give.... Come, silence, Top!” added Pencroff, turning to the dog; “and you, Jup, off to your room!”

The ape and the dog were silent. Jup went back to his bed, but Top remained in the sitting-room, and went on uttering low growls all the evening.

There was no more question of the incident, which, however, clouded the engineer’s brow.

During the rest of the month of July there were alternations of rain and cold. The temperature did not fall so low as during the preceding winter, and its maximum did not pass eight degrees Fahrenheit (13.33° centigrade below zero). But if this winter was less cold, at least it was more troubled by tempests and gales. There were again violent assaults of the sea, which more than once endangered the Chimneys. One would have believed that a tidal wave, raised by some submarine shock, was lifting those monstrous billows and hurling them against the wall of Granite House.

When the colonists, leaning from their windows, watched those enormous masses of water breaking beneath their eyes, they could not but admire the magnificent spectacle of this impotent fury of the ocean. The waves rebounded in dazzling foam; the whole beach disappeared under this raging flood; and the granite mass seemed to rise out of the sea itself, whose spray mounted to a height of more than a hundred feet.

During these tempests it was difficult, dangerous even, to venture upon the roads of the island, for falls of trees were frequent there. Nevertheless the colonists never let a week pass without going to visit the corral. Happily this enclosure, sheltered by the south-east spur of Mount Franklin, did not suffer much from the violence of the hurricane, which spared its trees, its sheds, and its palisade. But the poultry-yard, established on Prospect Plateau, and consequently exposed directly to the blasts of the east wind, had to bear rather considerable damage. The dovecote was twice unroofed, and the fence was blown down as well. All this needed to be made again in a more solid fashion, for, as was plainly seen, Lincoln Island was situated in the worst parts of the Pacific. It really seemed as if it formed the central point of vast cyclones, which lashed it as the whip lashes the top. Only here it was the top that stood still, and the whip that went round.

During the first week of the month of August the squalls abated little by little, and the atmosphere recovered a calm which it seemed to have lost for ever. With the calm the temperature fell, the cold again became very keen, and the column of the thermometer fell to eight degrees Fahrenheit below zero (22° centigrade below freezing).

On the 3rd of August an excursion, which had been planned some days before, was made into the south-east of the island, towards Tadorn’s Fen. The hunters were tempted by all the water-game which had established its winter quarters there. Wild ducks, snipe, pintails, teal, and grebes abounded, and it was decided that a day should be devoted to an expedition against these birds.

Not only Spilett and Herbert, but Pencroff and Neb also, took part in the expedition. Smith alone, alleging some work, did not join them, and remained at Granite House.

The hunters therefore took the road to Balloon Harbor, to go to the marsh, after promising to be back in the evening. Top and Jup accompanied them. As soon as they had crossed the bridge over the Mercy, the engineer raised it and returned, with the intention of carrying out a project for which he wished to be alone.

Now this project was to explore minutely that interior well, the mouth of which opened on a level with the passage of Granite House, and which communicated with the sea, since it had formerly served as an outlet for the waters of the lake.

Why did Top so often run round this opening? Why did he utter such strange barks, when a sort of uneasiness drew him back to this well? Why did Jup join Top in a sort of common anxiety? Had this well other branches than the vertical communication with the sea? Did it ramify towards other parts of the island? That was what Smith wished to know, and, in the first place, to be alone in knowing. He had therefore resolved to attempt the exploration of the well during an absence of his companions, and here was the opportunity of doing it.

It was easy to descend to the bottom of the well by using the rope-ladder, which had not served since the elevator was set up, and the length of which was sufficient. This the engineer did. He dragged the ladder to the hole, which measured about six feet in diameter, and let it unroll itself, after having firmly fastened its upper end. Then, having lighted a lantern, taken a revolver, and passed a cutlass through his belt, he began to descend the first rungs.

Everywhere the wall was solid; but some projections of the rock stood out at intervals, and by means of these projections it would really have been possible for an agile creature to climb up to the mouth of the well.

This was a remark which the engineer made; but, carefully passing his lantern over these projections, he found no impression, no fracture, which could give reason to think that they had served for any climbing, either ancient or recent.

Smith descended deeper, throwing light on every point of the wall.

He saw nothing suspicious there.

When the engineer had reached the last rungs he felt the surface of the water, which was then perfectly calm. Neither at its level, nor in any other part of the well, did any lateral passage open which could branch into the interior of the granite mass. The wall, which Smith struck with the handle of his cutlass, sounded solid. It was a compact granite, through which no living being could force a way. To reach the bottom of the well and then climb up to its mouth, it was necessary to pass through that channel, always under water, which connected it with the sea through the rocky subsoil of the beach; and this was possible only for marine animals. As to the question of where this channel ended, at what point of the shore and at what depth beneath the waves, it could not be answered.

So Smith, having finished his exploration, climbed up again, drew up the ladder, covered the mouth of the well, and returned, very thoughtful, to the great hall of Granite House, saying to himself:—

“I have seen nothing, and yet there is something!”
