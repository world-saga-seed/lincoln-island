# Herbert Wounded and Top Sent to Neb (final)

CHAPTER XLIX.

THE REPORTER AND PENCROFF IN THE CORRAL—HERBERT CARRIED IN—DESPAIR OF THE SAILOR—CONSULTATION OF THE REPORTER AND THE ENGINEER—MODE OF TREATMENT—HOPE REVIVES—HOW TO WARN NEB?—A SURE AND FAITHFUL MESSENGER—NEB’S ANSWER.

At Herbert’s cry, Pencroff, letting his weapon fall, had rushed towards him.

“They have killed him!” he cried. “Him, my child! They have killed him!”

Smith and Spilett had run to Herbert. The reporter listened to learn whether the poor boy’s heart still beat.

“He lives,” said he. “But he must be carried—”

“To Granite House? That is impossible!” answered the engineer.

“To the corral, then!” cried Pencroff.

“One moment,” said Smith.

And he rushed off to the left, so as to go round the enclosure. There he found himself face to face with a convict, who, taking aim at him, sent a ball through his hat. A few seconds after, before the man had even had time to fire his second shot, he fell, struck to the heart by the dagger of Smith, surer even than his gun.

Meanwhile, Spilett and the sailor hoisted themselves up at the angles of the palisade, they strode over the top, they leaped into the enclosure, they threw down the props which held the door on the inside, they rushed into the house, which was empty, and soon poor Herbert was lying upon Ayrton’s bed.

A few moments after, Smith was beside him.

At the sight of Herbert lifeless, the grief of the sailor was terrible. He sobbed, he wept, he wished to dash his head against the wall. Neither the engineer nor the reporter could calm him. Their own emotion choked them, and they could not speak.

However, they did all that lay in their power to dispute with death the poor boy who was dying under their eyes. Spilett, after the many incidents with which his life had been strewn, was not without some practice in ordinary medicine. He knew a little of everything, and many times already he had been called upon to tend wounds made either by a blade or by a firearm. Aided by Smith, he therefore proceeded to the care which the state of Herbert required.

First of all, the reporter was struck by the general stupor which overwhelmed him, a stupor due either to the hemorrhage, or even to the shock, if the ball had struck a bone with force enough to cause a violent concussion.

Herbert was extremely pale, and his pulse so feeble that Spilett felt it beat only at long intervals, as if it were on the point of stopping. At the same time, there was an almost complete prostration of the senses and of the intelligence. These symptoms were very grave.

Herbert’s chest was laid bare, and, the blood having been stanched with handkerchiefs, it was washed with cold water.

The contusion, or rather the contused wound, appeared. An oval hole was in the chest, between the third and fourth ribs. It was there that the ball had struck Herbert.

Smith and Spilett then turned the poor boy over, and he uttered a groan so feeble that it might have been thought his last sigh.

Another contused wound bloodied Herbert’s back, and the ball which had struck him came out of it at once.

“God be praised!” said the reporter; “the ball has not remained in the body, and we shall not have to extract it.”

“But the heart?” asked Smith.

“The heart has not been touched; otherwise Herbert would be dead!”

“Dead!” cried Pencroff, who gave a roar.

The sailor had heard only the last words spoken by the reporter.

“No, Pencroff,” answered Smith, “no! He is not dead. His pulse still beats! He has even uttered a groan. But, for the sake of your child himself, be calm. We have need of all our self-possession. Do not make us lose it, my friend.”

Pencroff was silent; but, a reaction taking place in him, great tears flooded his face.

Meanwhile, Spilett tried to collect his recollections and to proceed with method. From what he had observed, there was no doubt in his mind that the ball, entering in front, had come out behind. But what ravages had this ball made in its passage? What essential organs had been reached? That was what a professional surgeon could hardly have said at that moment, and, still less, the reporter.

However, he knew one thing: that he would have to prevent the inflammatory strangulation of the injured parts, then to combat the local inflammation and the fever which would follow from this wound,—a mortal wound, perhaps! Now, what topics, what antiphlogistics was he to employ? By what means was he to turn aside this inflammation?

In any case, the important thing was that the two wounds should be dressed without delay. It did not appear necessary to Spilett to bring on a fresh flow of blood, by washing them in warm water and pressing their lips together. The hemorrhage had been very abundant, and Herbert was already only too much weakened by the loss of his blood.

The reporter, therefore, thought it right to content himself with washing the two wounds with cold water.

Herbert was placed on his left side, and he was kept in that position.

“He must not move,” said Spilett. “He is in the most favorable position for the wounds in his back and chest to suppurate freely, and absolute rest is necessary.”

“What! We cannot carry him to Granite House?” asked Pencroff.

“No, Pencroff,” answered the reporter.

“Curse it!” cried the sailor, and his fist was raised towards heaven.

“Pencroff!” said Smith.

Spilett had begun again to examine the wounded boy with extreme attention. Herbert was still so frightfully pale that the reporter felt troubled.

“Cyrus,” said he, “I am not a doctor—I am in a terrible perplexity—you must help me with your advice, with your experience!”

“Recover your calm, my friend,” answered the engineer, pressing the reporter’s hand; “judge coolly—think only of this: Herbert must be saved!”

These words restored to Spilett that self-possession which, in a moment of discouragement, the keen sense of his responsibility had made him lose. He sat down near the bed, while Smith remained standing. Pencroff had torn up his shirt, and was mechanically making lint.

Spilett then explained to Smith that he thought it his duty, first of all, to stop the hemorrhage, but neither to close the two wounds nor to bring about their immediate cicatrization, because there had been an internal perforation, and the suppuration must not be allowed to accumulate in the chest.

Smith approved entirely, and it was decided that the two wounds should be dressed without attempting to close them by immediate coaptation. Very fortunately, it did not seem that they needed to be laid open.

And now, to act against the inflammation which would come on, did the colonists possess an efficacious agent?

Yes! They had one, for nature has lavished it generously. They had cold water, that is to say, the most powerful sedative that can be used against the inflammation of wounds, the most efficacious therapeutic agent in grave cases, and one which is now adopted by all physicians. Cold water has, besides, the advantage of leaving the wound in absolute rest, and of preserving it from all premature dressing; a considerable advantage, since it has been shown by experience that contact with the air is fatal during the first days.

Spilett and Smith reasoned thus with their plain good sense, and they acted as the best surgeon would have done. Linen compresses were applied to the two wounds of poor Herbert, and were to be kept constantly soaked with cold water.

The sailor had, first of all, lighted a fire in the fireplace of the dwelling, which did not lack the things necessary to life. Maple sugar and medicinal plants—the very ones which the lad had gathered on the banks of Lake Grant—allowed them to make some cooling infusions, and they made him take them without his being aware of it. His fever was extremely high, and all that day and the night passed thus without his having recovered consciousness. Herbert’s life now hung only by a thread, and that thread might break at any moment.

The next day, the 12th of November, Smith and his companions regained some hope. Herbert had come out of his long stupor. He opened his eyes, he recognized Smith, the reporter, Pencroff. He uttered two or three words. He did not know what had happened. They told him, and Spilett begged him to keep in absolute rest, telling him that his life was not in danger, and that his wounds would heal in a few days. Besides, Herbert suffered hardly at all, and the cold water, with which they were bathed without ceasing, prevented all inflammation of the wounds. The suppuration was established in a regular manner, the fever showed no tendency to increase, and it might be hoped that this terrible wound would bring on no catastrophe. Pencroff felt the swelling of his heart go down little by little. He was like a sister of charity, like a mother at the bedside of her child.

Herbert dozed again, but his sleep appeared to be better.

“Tell me again that you have hope, Mr. Spilett!” said Pencroff. “Tell me again that you will save Herbert!”

“Yes, we will save him!” answered the reporter. “The wound is serious, and perhaps the ball has even passed through the lung; but the perforation of that organ is not mortal.”

“God hear you!” repeated Pencroff.

As may well be supposed, during the twenty-four hours that they had been at the corral, the colonists had had no other thought than to nurse Herbert. They had troubled themselves neither about the danger which might threaten them if the convicts came back, nor about the precautions to be taken for the future.

But that day, while Pencroff watched by the bed of the sick boy, Smith and the reporter talked over what it was fitting to do.

First of all, they went over the corral. There was no trace of Ayrton. Had the unhappy man been dragged away by his old accomplices? Had he been surprised by them in the corral? Had he fought, and fallen in the struggle? This last supposition was only too probable. Spilett, at the moment when he was scaling the palisade, had plainly seen one of the convicts fleeing by the south spur of Mount Franklin, towards whom Top had rushed. He was one of those whose boat had broken on the rocks at the mouth of the Mercy. Besides, the man whom Smith had killed, and whose body was found outside the enclosure, certainly belonged to the band of Bob Harvey.

As to the corral, it had as yet suffered no devastation. The gates were shut, and the domestic animals had not been able to scatter in the forest. Nor was there any trace of a struggle, any damage, either to the house or to the palisade. Only the ammunition with which Ayrton had been supplied had disappeared with him.

“The unhappy man must have been surprised,” said Smith, “and, as he was a man to defend himself, he must have fallen.”

“Yes! that is to be feared!” answered the reporter. “Then, no doubt, the convicts installed themselves in the corral, where they found everything in abundance, and they only took flight when they saw us coming. It is very evident, too, that at that moment Ayrton, dead or alive, was no longer here.”

“We shall have to beat the forest,” said the engineer, “and rid the island of these wretches. Pencroff’s presentiments did not deceive him, when he wished them to be hunted like wild beasts. That would have spared us many misfortunes!”

“Yes,” answered the reporter; “but now we have the right to be without pity!”

“In any case,” said the engineer, “we are obliged to wait some time, and to remain at the corral until Herbert can be carried to Granite House without danger.”

“But Neb?” asked the reporter.

“Neb is in safety.”

“And if, uneasy at our absence, he should venture to come?”

“He must not come!” answered Smith quickly. “He would be murdered on the way!”

“But it is very probable that he will try to join us!”

“Ah! if the telegraph still worked, we could warn him! But that is impossible now! As for leaving Pencroff and Herbert alone here, we cannot do it! Well, I will go alone to Granite House.”

“No, no, Cyrus!” answered the reporter; “you must not expose yourself! Your courage could do nothing there. These wretches are evidently watching the corral; they are in ambush in the thick woods which surround it; and, if you went, we should soon have to lament two misfortunes instead of one!”

“But Neb?” repeated the engineer. “It is now twenty-four hours since he has had news of us! He will want to come!”

“And as he will be even less on his guard than we should be ourselves,” answered Spilett, “he will be struck down!”

“Is there, then, no way of warning him?”

While the engineer was reflecting, his eyes fell upon Top, who, going and coming, seemed to say, “And I, am I not here?”

“Top!” cried Smith.

The animal bounded at the call of his master.

“Yes, Top shall go!” said the reporter, who had understood the engineer. “Top will pass where we could not! He will carry to Granite House the news of the corral, and he will bring back to us that of Granite House!”

“Quick!” answered Smith. “Quick!”

Spilett had rapidly torn a page from his note-book, and on it he wrote these lines:—

“Herbert wounded. We are at the corral. Be on your guard. Do not leave Granite House. Have the convicts appeared in the neighborhood? Answer by Top.”

This laconic note contained all that Neb ought to learn, and at the same time asked him all that the colonists were concerned to know. It was folded and fastened to Top’s collar, in a very conspicuous manner.

“Top! my dog,” then said the engineer, caressing the animal, “Neb, Top! Neb! Go! go!”

Top bounded at these words. He understood, he divined what was required of him. The road to the corral was familiar to him. In less than half an hour he could have cleared it, and it was permitted to hope that there, where neither Smith nor the reporter could have ventured without danger, Top, running in the grass or under the edge of the wood, would pass unperceived.

The engineer went to the gate of the corral, and pushed back one of its leaves.

“Neb! Top, Neb!” repeated the engineer once more, stretching out his hand in the direction of Granite House.

Top sprang outside, and disappeared almost immediately.

“He will get there!” said the reporter.

“Yes, and he will come back, the faithful animal!”

“What time is it?” asked Spilett.

“Ten o’clock.”

“In an hour he may be here. We will watch for his return.”

The gate of the corral was closed again. The engineer and the reporter went back into the house. Herbert was then sound asleep. Pencroff kept his compresses in a permanent state of moisture.

Spilett, seeing that there was nothing to be done at the moment, busied himself in preparing some food, all the while watching with care that part of the enclosure backed against the spur, by which an attack might be made.

The colonists awaited the return of Top, not without anxiety. A little before eleven o’clock, Smith and the reporter, carbine in hand, were behind the gate, ready to open it at the first bark of their dog. They did not doubt that, if Top had been able to reach Granite House in safety, Neb would have sent him back at once.

They had both been there for about ten minutes, when a report rang out, and was immediately followed by repeated barking.

The engineer opened the gate, and, seeing still a remnant of smoke a hundred paces off in the wood, he fired in that direction.

Almost at the same moment Top bounded into the corral, the gate of which was quickly shut again.

“Top, Top!” cried the engineer, taking the dog’s great good head between his arms.

A note was fastened to his neck, and Smith read these words, traced in the large writing of Neb:—

“No pirates in the neighborhood of Granite House. I shall not stir. Poor Mr. Herbert!”
