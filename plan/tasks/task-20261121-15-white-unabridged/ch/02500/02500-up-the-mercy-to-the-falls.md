# Up the Mercy to the Falls (draft 1)

CHAPTER XXV.

THE DEPARTURE—THE RISING TIDE—ELMS AND NETTLE TREES—VARIOUS PLANTS—THE JACAMAR—ASPECT OF THE FOREST—THE GIGANTIC EUCALYPTUS—WHY THEY ARE CALLED “FEVER TREES”—TROOPS OF MONKEYS—THE WATERFALL—ENCAMPMENT FOR THE NIGHT.

The next day, the 30th of October, all was ready for the projected exploration, which the late events had rendered so urgent. Indeed, things had so turned out that the colonists of Lincoln Island might fancy themselves no longer in the position of asking for help, but rather of being able to bring it.

It was therefore agreed that they should ascend the Mercy as far as the current of the river was practicable. A great part of the way would thus be made without fatigue, and the explorers could carry their provisions and their arms to a point far advanced in the west of the island.

It had been necessary, in fact, to think not only of the things which they were taking with them, but also of those which chance might perhaps permit them to bring back to Granite House. If there had been a shipwreck on the coast, as everything led them to presume, wreckage would not be wanting, and would be good prize. With this in view, the cart would no doubt have suited better than the fragile canoe; but this cart, heavy and rough, had to be dragged, which made it less easy to employ. This led Pencroff to express his regret that the chest had not contained, at the same time as “his half-pound of tobacco,” a pair of those vigorous New Jersey horses, which would have been very useful to the colony!

The provisions, already embarked by Neb, consisted of preserved meat and a few gallons of beer and fermented liquor, that is to say, enough to sustain them for three days, the longest time which Cyrus Smith assigned to the exploration. Besides, they counted on replenishing their stores on the way if need be, and Neb took good care not to forget the little portable stove.

As to tools, the colonists took the two woodmen’s axes, which were to serve for clearing a road through the thick forest; and, as to instruments, the telescope and the pocket compass.

For arms they chose the two flint-lock guns, which on this island were more useful than guns of the new system would have been; for the first used only flints, which were easy to replace, and the second required fulminating caps, which frequent use would soon have exhausted. However, they took also one of the carbines and some cartridges. As to the powder, of which the barrels held about fifty pounds, they were obliged to carry a certain supply of it; but the engineer counted on manufacturing an explosive substance which would allow them to spare it. To the firearms they added the five cutlasses, well sheathed in leather; and in these conditions the colonists could venture into this vast forest with some chance of getting safely out of it.

It is needless to add that Pencroff, Herbert, and Neb, thus armed, were at the height of their wishes, although Cyrus Smith had made them promise not to fire a shot without necessity.

At six o’clock in the morning the canoe was pushed into the sea. All embarked, including Top, and made for the mouth of the Mercy.

The tide had been rising for half an hour only. There were, therefore, still some hours of flood, which it was well to profit by, for later the ebb would make the ascent of the river difficult. The flood was already strong, for the moon was to be full three days after; and the canoe, which needed only to be kept in the current, went rapidly between the two high banks, without its being necessary to increase its speed with the help of the oars.

In a few minutes the explorers had arrived at the elbow formed by the Mercy, and exactly at the angle where, seven months before, Pencroff had made his first raft of wood.

After this rather sharp angle the river, curving round, bent towards the southwest, and its course stretched away under the shade of great evergreen conifers.

The aspect of the banks of the Mercy was magnificent. Cyrus Smith and his companions could not but admire without reserve these fine effects which nature obtains so easily with water and trees. As they advanced, the forest trees changed in kind. On the right bank of the river rose in tiers magnificent specimens of the ulmaceae, those precious elms so much sought after by builders, which have the property of lasting a long time in water. Then came numerous groups belonging to the same family, among others nettle trees, whose kernel yields a very useful oil. Farther on, Herbert remarked some lardizabalas, whose flexible twigs, steeped in water, furnish excellent cordage, and two or three trunks of the ebenaceae, which showed a fine black color crossed by capricious veins.

From time to time, at certain places where landing was easy, the canoe stopped. Then Spilett, Herbert, and Pencroff, gun in hand and preceded by Top, beat the bank. Without counting the game, some useful plant might be met with which was not to be despised; and the young naturalist was served to his heart’s content. He discovered a sort of wild spinach of the family of the chenopodiaceae, and numerous specimens of cruciferae belonging to the cabbage genus, which it would certainly be possible to “civilize” by transplantation. There were cress, horseradish, and turnips; and lastly some little branching stems, slightly hairy, a metre high, which bore seeds almost brown.

“Do you know what that plant is?” asked Herbert of the sailor.

“Tobacco!” cried Pencroff, who evidently had never seen his favorite plant except in the bowl of his pipe.

“No, Pencroff!” answered Herbert; “it is not tobacco, it is mustard.”

“Mustard be it!” replied the sailor; “but if by chance a tobacco plant should turn up, my boy, be so good as not to despise it.”

“We shall find one some day!” said Spilett.

“Truly!” cried Pencroff. “Well, on that day I really do not know what our island will want!”

These various plants, which had been carefully uprooted, were carried to the canoe, which Cyrus Smith did not leave, still absorbed in his reflections.

The reporter, Herbert, and Pencroff thus landed several times, now on the right bank of the Mercy, now on the left. The latter was less abrupt, but the former more wooded. The engineer could ascertain, by consulting his pocket compass, that the direction of the river from the first elbow was sensibly southwest and northeast, and almost straight for a length of about three miles. But it was to be supposed that this direction changed farther on, and that the Mercy went up to the northwest, towards the spurs of Mount Franklin, which must feed it with their waters.

During one of these excursions Spilett succeeded in capturing two couples of living gallinaceae. They were birds with long and slender beaks, lengthened necks, and short wings, and with no appearance of a tail. Herbert rightly gave them the name of “tinamous,” and it was resolved that they should be the first guests of the future poultry yard.

But till then the guns had not spoken, and the first report which resounded in this forest of the Far West was provoked by the appearance of a beautiful bird which resembled a kingfisher anatomically.

“I recognize it!” cried Pencroff; and it may be said that his gun went off in spite of him.

“What do you recognize?” asked the reporter.

“The bird which escaped us on our first excursion, and whose name we gave to that part of the forest.”

“A jacamar!” cried Herbert.

It was a jacamar, indeed, a beautiful bird, whose rather harsh plumage is clothed with a metallic lustre. A few pellets had brought it to the ground, and Top carried it back to the canoe, together with a dozen “touraco-lories,” a sort of climbing bird of the size of a pigeon, all daubed with green, with part of the wings of a crimson color, and an upright crest festooned with a white edging. To the lad fell the honor of this fine shot, and he showed himself rather proud of it. The lories made better game than the jacamar, whose flesh is somewhat tough; but it would have been difficult to persuade Pencroff that he had not killed the king of eatable birds.

It was ten o’clock in the morning when the canoe reached a second elbow of the Mercy, about five miles from its mouth. Here they halted for breakfast, and this halt, under the shelter of fine tall trees, lasted half an hour.

The river still measured from sixty to seventy feet in breadth, and its bed from five to six feet in depth. The engineer had observed that numerous affluents swelled its course, but they were only simple rios, not navigable. As to the forest, whether under the name of Jacamar Wood or under that of the forests of the Far West, it extended out of sight. Nowhere, neither under the high forest trees nor under the trees on the banks of the Mercy, was the presence of man revealed.

The explorers could find no suspicious trace. It was evident that the woodman’s axe had never notched these trees, and that the pioneer’s knife had never severed these creepers, stretched from one trunk to another in the midst of the tufted brushwood and the long grass. If any castaways had landed on the island, they had not yet left its shore; and it was not under this thick cover that the survivors of the supposed shipwreck must be sought.

The engineer, therefore, showed a certain haste to reach the western coast of Lincoln Island, distant, by his estimate, at least five miles. The navigation was resumed. Although, by its present direction, the Mercy seemed to run towards Mount Franklin rather than towards the shore, it was decided that they should make use of the canoe as long as it found enough water under its keel to float. It was at once much fatigue spared and time gained, for it would have been necessary to cut a way with the axe through the thick underwood.

But soon the flood failed altogether, either because the tide was going down, and indeed it must be falling at this hour, or because it was no longer felt at this distance from the mouth of the Mercy. They were therefore obliged to ship the oars. Neb and Herbert took their places on their seat, Pencroff at the scull, and the ascent of the river was continued.

It seemed then that the forest tended to grow thinner on the side of the Far West. The trees there were less crowded, and often stood alone. But, precisely because they were more widely spaced, they profited more largely by the free, pure air which circulated around them, and they were magnificent.

What splendid specimens of the flora of this latitude! Certainly their presence would have sufficed a botanist to name without hesitation the parallel which Lincoln Island crossed!

“Eucalyptus!” Herbert had cried.

They were, in fact, those superb trees, the last giants of the extra-tropical zone, congeners of the eucalyptus of Australia and New Zealand, both of which are situated in the same latitude as Lincoln Island. Some of them rose to a height of two hundred feet. Their trunks measured twenty feet round at the base, and their bark, furrowed by a network of fragrant resin, was as much as five inches thick. Nothing could be more wonderful, and at the same time more singular, than these enormous specimens of the family of the myrtaceae, whose foliage presented its profile to the light and let the rays of the sun reach the ground!

At the foot of these eucalyptus a fresh grass carpeted the ground, and from the midst of the tufts flew flocks of little birds, which glittered in the streams of light like winged carbuncles.

“Those are trees!” cried Neb; “but are they good for anything?”

“Pooh!” answered Pencroff. “It must be with giant plants as it is with giant men. They are good for nothing but to show at fairs!”

“I think you are mistaken, Pencroff,” replied Spilett, “and that eucalyptus wood is beginning to be employed very advantageously in cabinet-making.”

“And I will add,” said the lad, “that these eucalyptus belong to a family which includes many useful members: the guava tree, which gives guavas; the clove tree, which produces cloves; the pomegranate, which bears pomegranates; the ‘eugenia cauliflora,’ whose fruits serve for the making of a passable wine; the myrtle ‘ugni,’ which contains an excellent alcoholic liquor; the myrtle ‘caryophyllus,’ whose bark forms an esteemed cinnamon; the ‘eugenia pimenta,’ from which comes Jamaica pepper; the common myrtle, whose berries can take the place of pepper; the ‘eucalyptus robusta,’ which produces a sort of excellent manna; the ‘eucalyptus gunei,’ whose sap is turned into beer by fermentation; and lastly, all those trees known by the name of ‘trees of life’ or ‘iron-wood,’ which belong to this family of the myrtaceae, of which forty-six genera and thirteen hundred species are counted!”

They let the lad run on, who was reciting his little lesson in botany with great spirit. Cyrus Smith listened to him smiling, and Pencroff with a feeling of pride which it would be impossible to express.

“Very good, Herbert,” replied Pencroff; “but I would dare swear that all those useful specimens you have just named are not giants like these!”

“That is true, Pencroff.”

“That supports what I said, then,” returned the sailor; “namely, that giants are good for nothing!”

“That is where you are wrong, Pencroff,” said the engineer then; “and these very gigantic eucalyptus which shelter us are good for something.”

“And what is that?”

“To make the country which they inhabit healthy. Do you know what they are called in Australia and New Zealand?”

“No, Mr. Smith.”

“They are called ‘fever trees.’”

“Because they give it?”

“No, because they prevent it!”

“Good. I will note that,” said the reporter.

“Note it, then, my dear Spilett; for it appears to be proved that the presence of the eucalyptus is enough to neutralize the miasmas of marshes. This natural preservative has been tried in certain countries of the south of Europe and the north of Africa, where the soil was absolutely unhealthy, and the health of their inhabitants has been seen to improve little by little. No more intermittent fevers in the regions which forests of these myrtaceae cover. This fact is now beyond doubt, and it is a happy circumstance for us colonists of Lincoln Island.”

“Ah! what an island! what a blessed island!” cried Pencroff. “I tell you, it wants nothing—unless it be—”

“That will come, Pencroff, that will be found,” answered the engineer; “but let us resume our navigation, and push on as far as the river will carry our canoe!”

The exploration therefore continued, for two miles at least, through a country covered with eucalyptus, which dominated all the woods of this portion of the island. The space which they covered extended beyond the limits of sight on each side of the Mercy, whose bed, rather winding, now ran hollowed between high green banks. This bed was often obstructed by tall grasses, and even by sharp rocks, which made the navigation rather laborious. The action of the oars was hindered by them, and Pencroff had to push with a pole. They felt, too, that the bottom was rising little by little, and that the moment was not far off when the canoe, for want of water, would be obliged to stop. Already the sun was declining towards the horizon, and threw upon the ground the immeasurable shadows of the trees. Cyrus Smith, seeing that he could not reach the western coast of the island that day, resolved to camp at the very place where, for want of water, the navigation would perforce be stopped. He reckoned that he must still be five or six miles from the coast, and this distance was too great for him to attempt to cross it during the night in the midst of these unknown woods.

The boat was therefore pushed on without respite through the forest, which little by little grew thicker again, and seemed more inhabited too; for, if the sailor’s eyes did not deceive him, he thought he saw troops of monkeys running under the copses. Sometimes even two or three of these animals stopped at some distance from the canoe and looked at the colonists without showing any terror, as if, seeing men for the first time, they had not yet learned to fear them. It would have been easy to bring down these quadrumana with gunshots, but Cyrus Smith opposed this useless massacre, which somewhat tempted the eager Pencroff. Besides, it was prudent; for these monkeys, vigorous and gifted with extreme agility, might be formidable, and it was better not to provoke them by an aggression altogether inopportune.

It is true that the sailor considered the monkey from a purely alimentary point of view; and, in fact, these animals, which are entirely herbivorous, make excellent game. But, since the provisions were abundant, it was useless to spend the ammunition to no purpose.

Towards four o’clock the navigation of the Mercy became very difficult, for its course was obstructed by water plants and rocks. The banks rose higher and higher, and the bed of the river was already hollowed between the first spurs of Mount Franklin. Its sources, then, could not be far off, since they were fed by all the waters of the southern slopes of the mountain.

“Within a quarter of an hour,” said the sailor, “we shall be forced to stop, Mr. Smith.”

“Well, then, we will stop, Pencroff, and we will make an encampment for the night.”

“How far can we be from Granite House?” asked Herbert.

“About seven miles,” answered the engineer, “taking into account, however, the windings of the river, which have carried us to the northwest.”

“Shall we go on?” asked the reporter.

“Yes, and as long as we are able,” answered Cyrus Smith. “To-morrow, at daybreak, we will leave the canoe. We shall cross in two hours, I hope, the distance which separates us from the coast, and we shall have almost the whole day for exploring the shore.”

“Forward!” answered Pencroff.

But soon the canoe grated on the stony bottom of the river, whose breadth did not then exceed twenty feet. A thick arch of verdure curved over its bed and wrapped it in half darkness. They heard, too, the rather loud noise of a waterfall, which showed, some hundreds of paces upstream, the presence of a natural dam.

And, indeed, at a last bend of the river, a cascade appeared through the trees. The canoe struck the bottom of the bed, and a few moments after it was moored to a trunk near the right bank.

It was about five o’clock. The last rays of the sun glided under the thick boughs and struck obliquely upon the little fall, whose damp spray glittered with the colors of the prism. Beyond, the bed of the Mercy disappeared under the copses, where it was fed from some hidden spring. The various rios which flowed into it along its course made of it, lower down, a true river; but here it was no more than a limpid brook without depth.

They camped at this very spot, which was charming. The colonists landed, and a fire was lighted under a clump of large nettle trees, among whose branches Cyrus Smith and his companions could, if need be, have found a refuge for the night.

The supper was soon devoured, for they were hungry, and after that the only thought was of sleep. But, some roars of a suspicious nature having made themselves heard at nightfall, the fire was fed for the night, so as to protect the sleepers with its crackling flames. Neb and Pencroff even watched by turns, and were lavish with the fuel. Perhaps they were not mistaken when they thought they saw the shadows of animals wandering round the camp, either under the copse or among the branches; but the night passed without accident, and the next day, the 31st of October, at five o’clock in the morning, all were on foot, ready to start.
