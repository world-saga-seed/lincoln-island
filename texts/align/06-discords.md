# I.6: White against the French (discords)

*Labeller d. Beads corrected: none.*

### 1. cut: the comparison with Defoe, Wyss, Selkirk and Raynal (I.6 ¶3; White ¶1)
- The French sets the castaways beside the imaginary heroes and the real castaways, who all had a wreck, seed, stock, tools or drift; White drops the whole comparison and keeps only a closing cry. *(Drafted.)*
  - quote: Les héros imaginaires de Daniel de Foé ou de Wyss
  - white: Every necessary of life was wanting!

### 2. changed: "seemed uninhabited" becomes "desert" (I.6 ¶1; White ¶1)
- The French says the coast only seemed uninhabited, which is the open question of ¶5; White states it as a desert coast. *(Drafted.)*
  - quote: sur une côte qui paraissait être inhabitée
  - white: Thrown upon a desert coast

### 3. changed: "perhaps all hope would not have been lost" becomes "would have saved them" (I.6 ¶4; White ¶2)
- The French hedges: with Smith, perhaps hope would not be lost; White says his science and genius would have saved them from despair. *(Drafted.)*
  - quote: peut-être tout espoir n’eût-il pas été perdu
  - white: would have saved them from despair

### 4. softened: Providence "that never abandons those whose faith is sincere" (I.6 ¶4; White ¶2)
- The French adds that Providence never abandons those whose faith is sincere; White keeps Providence and drops the condition of faith. *(My reading, to confirm.)*
  - quote: cette Providence qui n’abandonne jamais ceux dont la foi est sincère
  - white: The castaways could rely on Providence only, and on their own right hands.

### 5. addition: strength "exhausted by fatigue and by the inclemency of the weather" (I.6 ¶6; White ¶3)
- The French gives the reason for waiting as the long fatigues of exploring without shelter; White says their strength is already exhausted by fatigue and weather, which the French does not say. *(Drafted.)*
  - quote: devaient, avant tout, refaire leurs forces
  - white: repair their strength, exhausted by fatigue and by the inclemency of the weather

### 6. changed: the pigeons fly "in hundreds at the crest of the plateau" (I.6 ¶7; White ¶3)
- The French places the pigeons by the hundred on the crest of the plateau; White has "numerous" pigeons fluttering among the rocks. *(Drafted.)*
  - quote: ces pigeons qui volaient par centaines à la crête du plateau
  - white: the numerous pigeons which fluttered among the rocks

### 7. cut: Pencroff's own hopelessness (I.6 ¶8; White ¶4)
- The French says that for the sailor there was no hope left and the engineer had really perished, though he would not argue with Nab; White has only the illusion he has not the heart to destroy. *(Drafted.)*
  - quote: Pour lui, il n’était plus d’espoir, et l’ingénieur avait bien réellement péri dans les flots
  - white: —

### 8. changed: the place where his master fell becomes "his master’s tomb" (I.6 ¶8; White ¶4)
- The French compares Nab to the dog that cannot leave the place where its master fell; White has the dog that will not leave its master's tomb. *(Drafted.)*
  - quote: C’était comme le chien qui ne peut quitter la place où est tombé son maître
  - white: He was like the dog who would not leave his master’s tomb

### 9. condensed: Pencroff's invitation to the reporter (I.6 ¶11; White ¶6)
- The French has Pencroff ask Spilett to join the hunt and Spilett reconsider; White says only that they divided forces. *(Drafted.)*
  - quote: Pencroff demanda au reporter si celui-ci voulait les accompagner dans la forêt
  - white: When the meal was over they divided forces.

### 10. softened: the "formidable" wild beasts one would have to reckon with (I.6 ¶20; White ¶11)
- The French is sure ("très certainement", and Herbert agrees) that some tracks were left by formidable beasts to be reckoned with; White says only that some seemed to be the tracks of dangerous beasts. *(My reading, to confirm.)*
  - quote: quelques-unes avaient été laissées par des fauves formidables avec lesquels il y aurait à compter sans doute
  - white: Some seemed to them the tracks of dangerous wild beasts

### 11. changed: the hedges on man's presence, "perhaps" dropped (I.6 ¶20; White ¶11)
- The French says "peut-être" twice (they should perhaps be glad; man's presence perhaps more to fear than to wish); White has "no doubt" and a flat "more to be dreaded than desired". *(Drafted.)*
  - quote: eût été peut-être plus à craindre qu’à désirer
  - white: the presence of man was a thing more to be dreaded than desired

### 12. changed: the palm's limits in latitude (I.6 ¶27; White ¶17)
- The French gives palms to the fortieth parallel in the northern hemisphere and only to the thirty-fifth in the southern; White makes it a single range from 40° north to 35° south. *(Drafted.)*
  - quote: jusqu’au quarantième parallèle dans l’hémisphère boréal et jusqu’au trente-cinquième seulement dans l’hémisphère austral
  - white: grow from 40° north latitude to 35° south

### 13. changed: the Douglas pines of the north-west coast become the north-east (I.6 ¶27; White ¶17)
- The French places the Douglas firs on the north-west coast of America; White says the northeast coast. *(Drafted.)*
  - quote: la côte nord-ouest de l’Amérique
  - white: the northeast coast of America

### 14. changed: "a hundred" couroucous becomes 105 (I.6 ¶33; White ¶21)
- The French says about a hundred lay on the ground when the rest fled; White gives the exact number 105. *(Drafted.)*
  - quote: Une centaine jonchait déjà le sol
  - white: 105 were knocked over

### 15. cut: the river's source in the mountain, fed by snow on the central cone (I.6 ¶35; White ¶23)
- The French says the river must rise in the mountain and be fed by the melting snow on the flanks of the central cone, and that it curves slightly south; White has nothing of it. *(Drafted.)*
  - quote: la rivière devait prendre sa source dans la montagne et s’alimenter de la fonte des neiges qui tapissaient les flancs du cône central
  - white: —

### 16. changed: birds seen "through certain trees, among them junipers" becomes a wood full of junipers (I.6 ¶37; White ¶24)
- The French has the birds pecking berries on certain trees, juniper among them; White has the hunters enter a wood full of juniper. *(Drafted.)*
  - quote: dont ils becquetaient les baies aromatiques, entre autres des genévriers
  - white: they entered a wood full of juniper trees

### 17. cut: the males told by their two pointed wing tufts (I.6 ¶37; White ¶24)
- The French has Harbert recognise the male tétras by two pointed tufts of raised feathers on the neck; White omits it. *(Drafted.)*
  - quote: Harbert reconnut les mâles aux deux ailerons pointus
  - white: —

### 18. changed: the hazel-hen becomes a pullet (I.6 ¶37; White ¶24)
- The French likens the tétras' flesh to the gélinotte (hazel-hen); White says a pullet. *(Drafted.)*
  - quote: dont la chair vaut celle de la gélinotte
  - white: their meat as delicious as a pullet

### 19. changed: "expect to see many more" becomes "hope for many such contrivances" (I.6 ¶50; White ¶33)
- The French has Pencroff say that in their situation they must expect to see many more such things; White has him hope for many such contrivances. *(My reading, to confirm.)*
  - quote: il faut nous attendre à en voir bien d’autres
  - white: we must hope for many such contrivances

### 20. cut: footnote n1, Isaac Walton (I.6 ¶41 n1; White —)
- The French footnote glosses Isaac Walton as the author of a treatise on angling; White names Walton in the text with no note. *(Drafted.)*
  - quote: Célèbre auteur d’un traité sur la pêche à la ligne.
  - white: —
