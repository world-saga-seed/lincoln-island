# The Bridge and the Onaggas (draft 1)

CHAPTER XXIX.

PROJECTS TO BE CARRIED OUT—A BRIDGE OVER THE MERCY—TO MAKE AN ISLAND OF PROSPECT PLATEAU—THE DRAW-BRIDGE—THE CORN HARVEST—THE STREAM—THE CAUSEWAY—THE POULTRY YARD—THE PIGEON-HOUSE—THE TWO ONAGGAS—HARNESSED TO THE WAGON—EXCURSION TO BALLOON HARBOR.

The colonists of Lincoln Island had now reconquered their domicile without having been obliged to follow the old weir, which spared them the work of masonry. It was, indeed, fortunate, that at the moment they were preparing to do so, the band of monkeys, struck by a terror not less sudden than inexplicable, had rushed from Granite House. Had these animals a presentiment that a serious attack was about to be made on them from another direction? This was almost the only way to account for their retreat.

The rest of the day was occupied in carrying the dead monkeys to the wood and burying them there, and in repairing the disorder made by the intruders,—disorder and not damage, as, though they had upset the furniture in the rooms, they had broken nothing. Neb rekindled the range, and the supply in the pantry furnished a substantial repast to which all did ample honor.

Jup was not forgotten, and he ate with avidity the pine kernels and the rhizome roots, with which he saw himself abundantly provided. Pencroff had unfastened his arms, although he thought it best to keep the monkey’s legs bound until he could count on his resignation.

Seated at the table, before going to bed, Smith and his companions discussed several projects, the execution of which was urgent. The most important and the most pressing was the establishment of a bridge across the Mercy, so as to put the southern part of the island in communication with Granite House, then the building of a corral, designed for the accommodation of moufflons or other woolly animals which it would be well to capture. It will be seen that these two plans tended to solve the question of clothing, which was then the most serious question. In fact, the bridge would make easy the carriage of the envelope of the balloon, which would furnish linen, and the corral would yield the crop of wool, which would furnish winter clothing.

As to this corral, it was Smith’s intention to establish it at the very sources of Red Creek, where the ruminants would find pastures that would give them fresh and abundant food. Already the path between there and Prospect Plateau was partially cleared, and with a cart better constructed than the first, carriage would be easier, especially if they should capture some animal that could draw it.

But while it would not be inconvenient to have the corral some distance from Granite House, it was different with the poultry-yard, to which Neb called attention. It was necessary that the fowls should be at the hand of the cook, and no place seemed more favorable for an establishment of this kind than that portion of the lake shore bordering on the former weir. The aquatic birds also would thrive there, and the pair of tinamons, taken in the last excursion, would serve for a first attempt at domestication.

The next day—the 3d of November—the new works were begun with the building of the bridge, and all hands were required on the important undertaking. Saws, hatchets, chisels, and hammers were loaded on the shoulders of the colonists, who, now turned carpenters, descended to the shore.

Here Pencroff reflected as follows:—

“Supposing while we are away Master Jup takes the notion of hauling up the ladder, which he so gallantly sent back to us yesterday?”

“Let us make fast its lower end,” answered Smith.

The ladder was therefore made fast to two stakes driven firmly into the sand. Then the colonists, ascending the left bank of the Mercy, soon arrived at the bend formed by the river, where they halted to examine whether the bridge should not be thrown across at this place. The situation appeared suitable, as from this point to Balloon Harbor, discovered the day before on the southern coast, the distance was only three miles and a half, and from the bridge to the harbor a wagon road could easily be constructed, which would make communication easy between Granite House and the south of the island.

Then Smith communicated to his companions a project, at once very simple to carry out and very advantageous, which he had had in view for some time. This was to completely isolate Prospect Plateau, so as to protect it from all attacks of quadrupeds or quadrumana. By this means Granite House, the Chimneys, the poultry yard, and all the upper part of the plateau destined for sowing would be protected against the depredations of animals.

Nothing could be easier than to do this, and the engineer proposed to accomplish it as follows:—The plateau was already protected on three sides by either natural or artificial water courses. On the northwest, by the bank of Lake Grant, extending from the angle resting against the mouth of the former weir to the cut made in the east bank to draw off the water. On the north, from this cut to the sea, by the new water course which had worn itself a bed across the plateau and the beach, both above and below the fall; and it would suffice to dig out the bed of this creek to render the passage impracticable to animals. Along the whole of the east, by the sea itself, from the mouth of this new creek to the mouth of the Mercy. And on the south, finally, from this mouth to the bend of the Mercy, where the bridge was to be built. Therefore the only part remaining open was the western part of the plateau included between the bend in the river and the southern angle of the lake, a distance of less than a mile. But nothing could be easier than to dig a ditch, wide and deep, which would be filled from the lake, and whose overflow would be carried by a second fall into the bed of the Mercy. Doubtless the level of the lake would be lowered somewhat by this new drain on its resources, but Smith had assured himself that the flow of Red Creek was sufficient for his purpose.

“Thus,” added the engineer, “Prospect Plateau will be a veritable island, being surrounded with water on every side, and it will communicate with the rest of our domain only by the bridge which we will throw over the Mercy, by the two causeways already built above and below the fall, and by the two others which are to be constructed, one over the proposed ditch, and the other over the left bank of the Mercy. Now if this bridge and the causeways can be raised at will, Prospect Plateau will be secured from all surprise.”

Smith, in order to make his companions comprehend clearly his plans, had made a plot of the plateau, and his project was at once understood in all its bearings. It met with unanimous approval; and Pencroff, brandishing his carpenter’s hatchet, exclaimed:—

“And first, for the bridge!”

This work was the most urgent. Trees were selected, felled, lopped, and cut into beams, planks, and boards. The bridge was to be stationary on the right bank of the Mercy, but on the left it was to be so constructed as to raise by means of counterweights, as some lock bridges are.

It will be understood that this was a considerable work, and if it was ably conducted, it at least required some time, as the Mercy was about eighty feet wide at this point. It was first necessary to drive piles in the bed of the river, to sustain the fixed flooring of the bridge, and to set up a pile-driver to drive the piles, so as to form two arches capable of supporting heavy weights.

Very fortunately they lacked neither the necessary tools for preparing the timber, nor the iron work, to bind it together, nor the ingenuity of a man who was an adept at this sort of work, nor, finally, the zeal of his companions who in these seven months had necessarily acquired great manual skill. And it should be added that Spilett was not the most awkward among them, and vied in skill with the sailor himself “who would never have expected so much from a newspaper man!”

It took three weeks of very steady work to build this bridge. And as the weather was then magnificent they lunched on the ground, and only returned to Granite House for supper.

During this period it was observed that Master Jup took kindly to and familiarized himself with his new masters, whom he always watched with an extremely curious eye. Nevertheless, as a measure of precaution, Pencroff did not yet give him complete liberty, wishing, with reason, to wait until the limits of the plateau had been rendered impassible by the projected works. Top and he were the best possible friends, and got on capitally together although Jup took everything gravely.

The bridge was finished on the 20th of November. The movable part, balanced by the counterpoise, swung easily, and needed but a slight effort to raise it; between the hinge and crossbeam on which the draw rested when closed, the distance was twenty feet, a gap sufficiently wide to prevent animals from getting across.

It was next proposed to go for the envelope of the balloon, which the colonists were anxious to place in complete safety; but in order to bring it, the cart would have to be dragged to Balloon Harbor, necessitating the breaking of a road through the dense underwood of the Far West, all of which would take time. Therefore Neb and Pencroff first made an excursion to the harbor, and as they found that the “stock of cloth” was in no way suffering in the cave where it had been stored, it was decided that the works about the plateau should not be discontinued.

“This,” said Pencroff, “will enable us to establish the poultry-yard under better conditions, since we need have no fear of the visits of foxes or other noxious animals.”

“And also,” added Neb, “we can clear the plateau, and transplant wild plants”—

“And make ready our second corn-field!” cried the sailor with a triumphant air.

Indeed the first corn-field, sowed with a single grain, had prospered admirably, thanks to the care of Pencroff. It had produced the ten ears foretold by the engineer, and as each ear had eighty grains, the colonists found themselves possessed of 800 grains—in six months—which promised them a double harvest each year. These 800 grains, excepting some fifty which were prudently reserved, were now about to be sowed in a new field with as much care as the first solitary specimen.

The field was prepared, and inclosed with a strong palisade, high and sharp-pointed, which quadrupeds would have found very difficult to surmount. As to the birds, the noisy whirligigs and frightful scarecrows, the product of Pencroff’s fantastic imagination, were enough to keep them at a distance. Then the 750 grains were laid in little furrows, very regularly drawn, and Nature was left to do the rest.

On the 21st of November, Smith began laying out the ditch which was to enclose the plateau on the west, from the southern angle of Lake Grant to the bend of the Mercy. There were two or three feet of vegetable earth, and beneath that the granite. It was, therefore, necessary to manufacture some more nitro-glycerine, and the nitro-glycerine had its accustomed effect. In less than a fortnight a ditch, twelve feet wide and six feet deep was excavated in the hard ground of the plateau. A new outlet was in like manner made in the rocky border of the lake, and the waters rushed into this new channel, forming a small stream, to which they gave the name of Glycerine Creek, and which became a tributary of the Mercy. As the engineer had foreseen the level of the lake was lowered, but almost imperceptibly. Finally, for completing the enclosure, the bed of the stream across the beach was considerably enlarged, and the sand was kept up by a double palisade.

By the first half of December all these works were finally completed, and Prospect Plateau, shaped something like an irregular pentagon, having a perimeter of about four miles, was encircled with a liquid belt, making it absolutely safe from all aggression.

During this month the heat was very great. Nevertheless, the colonists would not suspend the execution of their projects, and as it was becoming urgent to organize the poultry-yard, they proceeded to construct it. It is needless to say that, since the complete enclosing of the plateau, Master Jup had been given his liberty. He never quitted his masters nor manifested the least desire to escape. He was a gentle beast, though very vigorous, and of surprising agility. Ah! when it came to climbing the ladder to Granite House, no one could rival him. Already he was given employment; he dragged loads of wood and carted the stones which had been taken from the bed of Glycerine Creek.

“Although he’s not yet a mason, he is already a ‘monkey!’” said Herbert, making a joking allusion to the nickname masons give their apprentices. And if ever a name was well applied, it was so in this instance!

The poultry-yard occupied an area of 200 square yards on the southeast bank of the lake. It was enclosed with a palisade, and various shelters were built for the animals which were to people it. These were huts of branches divided into compartments, which soon awaited only their occupants.

The first was the pair of tinamons, who were not long in breeding numerous little ones. They had for companions half-a-dozen ducks, who were always by the water-side. Some of these belonged to that Chinese variety whose wings open like a fan, and whose plumage rivals in brilliance that of the golden pheasant. Some days later, Herbert caught a pair of magnificent curassows, birds of the gallinaceæ family, with long rounding tails. These soon grew tame; and as to the pellicans, the kingfishers, the moorhens, they came of themselves to the poultry-yard. And soon, all this little world, after some disputing, cooing, scolding, clucking, ended by agreeing and multiplying at a rate reassuring for the future food of the colony.

Smith, in order to complete his work, established a pigeon-house in a corner of the poultry-yard, and placed therein a dozen of the pigeons which frequented the high rocks of the plateau. These birds readily habituated themselves to their new abode, and returned there each evening, showing a greater propensity to domestication than the wood pigeons, their congeners, which do not breed except in a savage state.

And now the time was come to make use of the envelope in the manufacture of linen, for to keep it intact in order to attempt to leave the island by risking themselves in a balloon filled with heated air over a sea, which might be called limitless, was only to be thought of by men deprived of all other resources, and Smith, being of a practical mind, did not dream of such a thing.

It was necessary to bring the envelope to Granite House, and the colonists busied themselves in making their heavy cart less unwieldy and lighter. But though the vehicle was provided, the motor was still to be found! Did not there exist in the island some ruminant of indigenous species which could replace the horse, ass, ox, or cow? That was the question.

“Indeed,” said Pencroff, “a draught animal would be very useful to us, while we are waiting until Mr. Smith is ready to build a steam-wagon, or even a locomotive, for certainly, some day we will have a railway from Granite House to Balloon Harbor, with a branch road up Mount Franklin!”

And the honest sailor, in talking thus, believed what he said. Such is the power of imagination combined with faith!

But, not to exaggerate, a simple quadruped capable of being harnessed would have just suited Pencroff, and as Providence had a weakness for him, she did not keep him waiting.

One day, the 23d of December, the colonists, busy at the Chimneys, heard Neb crying and Top barking in such emulation, that fearing some untoward accident, they ran to them.

What did they see? Two large, beautiful animals, which had imprudently ventured upon the plateau, the causeways not having been closed. They seemed like two horses, or at least two asses, male and female, finely shaped, of an isabella color, striped with black on the head, neck, and body, and with white legs and tail. They advanced tranquilly, without showing any fear, and looked with lively eyes on these men in whom they had not yet recognized their masters.

“They are onaggas!” cried Herbert. “Quadrupeds of a kind between the zebra and the quagga!”

“Why aren’t they asses?” asked Neb.

“Because they have not long ears, and their forms are more graceful!”

“Asses or horses,” retorted Pencroff—“they are what Mr. Smith would call ‘motors,’ and it will be well to capture them!”

The sailor, without startling the animals, slid through the grass to the causeway over Glycerine Creek, raised it, and the onaggas were prisoners. Should they be taken by violence, and made to submit to a forced domestication? No. It was decided that for some days they would let these animals wander at will over the plateau where the grass was abundant, and the engineer at once had a stable constructed near to the poultry-yard in which the onaggas would find a good bedding, and a refuge for the night.

The fine pair were thus left entirely at liberty, and the colonists even avoided frightening them by approaching. Several times, however, the onaggas seemed to feel the need of quitting the plateau, which was too confined for them, accustomed to wide ranges and deep forests. The colonists saw them following around the belt of water impossible to cross, uttering some shrill brays, then galloping over the grass; and, when calm had returned, they would remain whole hours gazing at the great woods which were shut against them for ever!

In the meantime, harness and traces had been made from vegetable fibres, and some days after the capture of the onaggas, not only was the cart ready to be harnessed, but a straight road, or rather a cut, had been made through the forest of the Far West all the way from the bend in the Mercy to Balloon Harbor. They could therefore get to this latter place with the cart, and towards the end of the month the onaggas were tried for the first time.

Pencroff had already coaxed these animals so that they ate from his hand, and he could approach them without difficulty, but, once harnessed, they reared, and were with great difficulty held in. However, it was not long before they submitted to this new service, for the onagga, less rebellious than the zebra, is frequently harnessed in the mountainous parts of South Africa, and it has even been acclimatized in Europe in relatively cold zones.

This day, every one except Pencroff, who walked at the head of his team, rode in the cart to Balloon Harbor. That they were jolted about over this road, scarcely yet laid out, goes without saying; but the cart arrived without accident, and they were able to load it, the same day, with the envelope and the appurtenances to the balloon.

By 8 o’clock in the evening, the cart, having recrossed the bridge, followed down the left bank of the Mercy and stopped on the beach. The onaggas were unharnessed, placed in the stable, and Pencroff, before sleeping, gave a sigh of satisfaction that made the echoes of Granite House ring noisily.
