# The Boat, the Whale and the Tobacco (draft 1)

CHAPTER XXXII.

BUILDING THE BOAT—THE SECOND HARVEST OF WHEAT—HUNTING KOULAS—A NEW PLANT, MORE AGREEABLE THAN USEFUL—A WHALE IN SIGHT—THE HARPOON FROM THE VINEYARD—CUTTING UP THE WHALE—USE OF THE WHALEBONE—THE END OF MAY—PENCROFF HAS NOTHING MORE TO WISH FOR.

When Pencroff had once got a project into his head, he gave neither himself nor others any rest until it had been carried out. Now he wished to visit Tabor Island, and, as a boat of some size was necessary for the crossing, the said boat must be built.

This is the plan which was settled by the engineer, in agreement with the sailor.

The boat was to measure thirty-five feet in the keel and nine feet in the beam,—which would make her a good sailer, if her bottom and her water-lines came out well,—and she was not to draw more than six feet, a draught of water sufficient to hold her against leeway. She was to be decked her whole length, and pierced with two hatchways giving access to two cabins separated by a partition. She was to be rigged as a sloop, with mainsail, staysail, square-sail, gaff-topsail and jib, a rig very easily handled, lowering well in case of squalls, and very favorable for lying close to the wind. Lastly, her hull was to be carvel-built, that is to say, the planks were to lie flush instead of overlapping; and as to her frames, they would be put in hot, after the fitting of the planks, which were to be mounted on false frames.

What wood should be employed in building this boat? The elm or the fir, both of which abounded in the island? They decided for the fir, a wood a little “shaky,” in the carpenters' phrase, but easy to work, and which bears immersion in water as well as the elm.

These details settled, it was agreed that, since the fine season would not return for six months, Smith and Pencroff should work at the boat alone. Spilett and Herbert were to continue hunting, and neither Neb nor Master Jup, his assistant, would abandon the domestic work which had fallen to their share.

As soon as the trees were chosen, they were felled, cut up, and sawn into planks, as pit-sawyers might have done it. Eight days later, in the recess which lay between the Chimneys and the cliff, a ship-yard was prepared, and a keel thirty-five feet long, furnished with a stern-post aft and a stem forward, stretched along the sand.

Smith had not gone blindly to work at this new task. He understood ship-building as he understood nearly everything else, and it was on paper that he had first worked out the model of his boat. Besides, he was well served by Pencroff, who, having worked for some years in a ship-yard at Brooklyn, knew the practice of the trade. It was therefore only after rigorous calculation and mature reflection that the false frames were fitted upon the keel.

Pencroff, as may well be believed, was all fire to carry his new enterprise through, and he would not have left it for an instant.

One operation alone had the privilege of drawing him from his ship-yard, and that for one day only. This was the second harvest of wheat, which was gathered on the 15th of April. It had succeeded as well as the first, and gave the proportion of grain which had been foretold.

“Five bushels, Mr. Smith!” said Pencroff, after having scrupulously measured his riches.

“Five bushels,” replied the engineer; “and, at a hundred and thirty thousand grains to the bushel, that makes six hundred and fifty thousand grains.”

“Well, we will sow it all this time,” said the sailor, “all but a small reserve, though!”

“Yes, Pencroff; and if the next harvest gives a proportionate yield, we shall have four thousand bushels.”

“And we shall eat bread?”

“We shall eat bread.”

“But we shall have to make a mill?”

“We will make a mill.”

The third field of wheat was therefore incomparably larger than the first two, and the soil, prepared with extreme care, received the precious seed. This done, Pencroff returned to his work.

During this time Spilett and Herbert hunted in the neighborhood, and they ventured rather deep into the still unknown parts of the Far West, their guns loaded with ball, ready for any bad encounter. It was an inextricable tangle of magnificent trees, pressed one against another as if space had failed them. The exploration of these wooded masses was extremely difficult, and the reporter never risked himself there without carrying the pocket compass; for the sun hardly pierced the thick boughs, and it would have been difficult to find the way back. It naturally happened that game was rarer in these parts, where it would not have had sufficient freedom of movement. Three large herbivorous animals were, however, killed during the last fortnight of April. These were koulas, of which the colonists had already seen a specimen to the north of the lake, and which stupidly let themselves be killed among the thick branches of the trees in which they had sought refuge. Their skins were brought back to Granite House, and, with the help of sulphuric acid, they were subjected to a sort of tanning which made them fit for use.

A discovery, precious from another point of view, was also made during one of these excursions, and for this one they were indebted to Spilett.

It was the 30th of April. The two hunters had gone deep into the south-west of the Far West, when the reporter, who was some fifty paces ahead of Herbert, came into a sort of clearing, where the trees, standing farther apart, let in a few rays.

Spilett was at first surprised by the odor given off by certain plants with straight stems, cylindrical and branching, which bore flowers arranged in clusters and very small seeds. The reporter pulled up one or two of these stems and went back to the lad, to whom he said:—

“Just see what this is, Herbert?”

“And where did you find this plant, Mr. Spilett?”

“There, in a clearing, where it grows very abundantly.”

“Well, Mr. Spilett,” said Herbert, “here is a find which gives you every right to Pencroff's gratitude!”

“It is tobacco, then?”

“Yes, and if it is not of the first quality, it is tobacco none the less!”

“Ah! good Pencroff! How pleased he will be! But, devil take it, he will not smoke it all! he will surely leave us our share!”

“Ah! an idea, Mr. Spilett,” replied Herbert. “Let us say nothing to Pencroff; let us take the time to prepare these leaves, and one fine day we will present him with a pipe ready filled!”

“Agreed, Herbert; and on that day our worthy companion will have nothing more to wish for in this world!”

The reporter and the lad laid in a good store of the precious plant, and they returned to Granite House, where they brought it in “as contraband,” and with as much precaution as if Pencroff had been the strictest of custom-house officers.

Smith and Neb were taken into the secret, and the sailor suspected nothing during all the time, a rather long one, which was needed to dry the thin leaves, to chop them, and to subject them to a certain roasting upon hot stones. This took two months; but all these manipulations could be carried on without Pencroff's knowledge, for, busy with the building of the boat, he came up to Granite House only at the hour of rest.

Once again, however, and in spite of himself, his favorite work was interrupted, on the 1st of May, by a fishing adventure in which all the colonists were obliged to take part.

For some days there had been seen at sea, two or three miles out, an enormous animal swimming in the waters of Lincoln Island. It was a whale of the largest size, which, in all probability, belonged to the southern species called the “Cape whale.”

“What a piece of luck it would be to get hold of it!” cried the sailor. “Ah! if we only had a proper boat and a harpoon in good order, how I would say, ‘After the beast, for she is well worth the taking!’”

“Eh! Pencroff,” said Spilett, “I should have liked to see you handle the harpoon. It must be curious!”

“Very curious, and not without danger,” said the engineer; “but since we have not the means of attacking this animal, it is useless to trouble ourselves about it.”

“I am astonished,” said the reporter, “to see a whale in this comparatively high latitude.”

“Why so, Mr. Spilett?” replied Herbert. “We are exactly in that part of the Pacific which English and American fishermen call the ‘whale-field,’ and it is here, between New Zealand and South America, that the whales of the southern hemisphere are met with in the greatest numbers.”


“Nothing is more true,” replied Pencroff; “and what surprises me is that we have not seen more of them. After all, since we cannot get near them, it matters little!”

And Pencroff went back to his work, not without heaving a sigh of regret; for in every sailor there is a fisherman, and if the pleasure of fishing is in direct ratio to the size of the animal, one may judge what a whaler feels in the presence of a whale!

And if it had only been the pleasure! But they could not hide from themselves that such a prey would have been very profitable to the colony, for the oil, the fat, and the whalebone might be put to many uses!

Now it so happened that the whale which had been sighted seemed unwilling to leave the waters of the island. So, either from the windows of Granite House or from Prospect Plateau, Herbert and Spilett, when they were not out hunting, and Neb, while minding his ovens, never left the telescope, and they observed every movement of the animal. The cetacean, having come far into the vast Union Bay, furrowed it rapidly from Mandible Cape to Claw Cape, driven by its prodigiously powerful tail-fin, on which it supported itself, moving by jerks with a speed which sometimes reached twelve miles an hour. Sometimes, too, it came so near the islet that it could be completely distinguished. It was indeed the southern whale, which is entirely black, and whose head is more depressed than that of the whales of the north.

It could be seen also throwing out from its blow-holes, to a great height, a cloud of vapor ... or of water; for—strange as the fact may appear—naturalists and whalers are not yet agreed on this subject. Is it air, or is it water, which is thus driven out? It is generally admitted to be vapor, which, condensing suddenly on contact with the cold air, falls again as rain.

Meanwhile the presence of this marine mammal occupied the colonists' minds. It vexed Pencroff above all, and distracted him at his work. He ended by longing for this whale as a child longs for a thing which is forbidden it. At night he dreamed of it aloud; and certainly, if he had had the means of attacking it, if the launch had been fit to keep the sea, he would not have hesitated to set out in pursuit of it.

But what the colonists could not do, chance did for them. On the 3rd of May shouts from Neb, posted at his kitchen window, announced that the whale was stranded on the shore of the island.

Herbert and Spilett, who were about to set out for the chase, laid down their guns, Pencroff threw down his axe, Smith and Neb joined their companions, and all made rapidly for the place where it had stranded.

The stranding had taken place on the beach of Jetsam Point, three miles from Granite House, and at high tide. It was probable, therefore, that the cetacean would not easily get off. In any case they must make haste, so as to cut off its retreat if need be. They ran with picks and iron-pointed spears, crossed the bridge over the Mercy, went down the right bank of the river, took to the beach, and in less than twenty minutes the colonists were beside the enormous animal, above which a world of birds was already swarming.

“What a monster!” cried Neb.

And the word was just, for it was a southern whale, eighty feet long, a giant of the species, which could not weigh less than a hundred and fifty thousand pounds!

Yet the monster, stranded thus, did not stir, and made no effort, by struggling, to float itself again while the sea was still high.

The colonists soon had the explanation of its stillness when, at low tide, they had walked round the animal.

It was dead, and a harpoon was sticking out of its left side.

“There are whalers in these waters, then?” said Spilett at once.

“Why so?” asked the sailor.

“Since this harpoon is still there—”

“Eh! Mr. Spilett, that proves nothing,” replied Pencroff. “Whales have been seen to travel thousands of miles with a harpoon in their side; and if this one had been struck in the north of the Atlantic and had come to die in the south of the Pacific, there would be nothing to be astonished at!”

“Nevertheless—” said Spilett, whom Pencroff's assertion did not satisfy.

“That is perfectly possible,” replied Smith; “but let us examine this harpoon. Perhaps, following a fairly common custom, the whalers have engraved on it the name of their ship?”

And indeed Pencroff, having pulled out the harpoon which the animal had in its side, read on it this inscription:—

MARIA-STELLA. VINEYARD.

“A ship of the Vineyard! A ship of my own country!” he cried. “The Maria-Stella! a fine whaler, upon my word! and one I know well! Ah! my friends, a vessel of the Vineyard, a whaler of the Vineyard!”(1)

(1) A port of the State of New York.

And the sailor, brandishing the harpoon, repeated, not without emotion, that name which was dear to his heart, the name of his native place!

But, as they could not wait for the Maria-Stella to come and claim the animal she had harpooned, they resolved to proceed to the cutting up before decomposition set in. The birds of prey, which for some days had been watching this rich prey, wished without further delay to take possession, and it was necessary to drive them off with gunshots.

This whale was a female, whose teats furnished a great quantity of milk which, in accordance with the opinion of the naturalist Dieffenbach, might pass for cow's milk; and, in fact, it differs from it neither in taste, nor in color, nor in density.

Pencroff had formerly served on board a whaling ship, and he was able to direct methodically the operation of cutting up,—a rather disagreeable operation, which lasted three days, but from which none of the colonists shrank, not even Spilett, who, as the sailor said, would end by making “a very good castaway.”

The blubber, cut in parallel slices two feet and a half thick, then divided into pieces which might weigh a thousand pounds each, was melted down in large earthen vessels brought to the very place of the cutting up,—for they did not wish to taint the approaches to Prospect Plateau,—and in this melting it lost about a third of its weight. But there was abundance of it: the tongue alone gave six thousand pounds of oil, and the lower lip four thousand. Then, besides this fat, which was to secure for a long time their store of stearine and glycerine, there was the whalebone as well, which would doubtless find its use, although neither umbrellas nor corsets were worn at Granite House. The upper part of the cetacean's mouth was, in fact, furnished on both sides with eight hundred horny blades, very elastic, of fibrous texture, and fringed at their edges like two great combs, whose teeth, six feet long, serve to retain the thousands of animalcules, small fish, and mollusks on which the whale feeds.

The operation ended, to the great satisfaction of those who had carried it out, the remains of the animal were abandoned to the birds, which would make every last vestige of it disappear, and the daily work was resumed at Granite House.

However, before returning to the ship-yard, Smith had the idea of making certain contrivances which strongly excited the curiosity of his companions. He took a dozen whalebones, which he cut into six equal parts, and sharpened at their ends.

“And these, Mr. Smith,” asked Herbert, when the work was finished, “these will serve—?”

“To kill wolves, foxes, and even jaguars,” replied the engineer.

“Now?”

“No, this winter, when we shall have ice at our disposal.”

“I do not understand—” replied Herbert.

“You shall understand, my boy,” replied the engineer. “This contrivance is not of my invention; it is frequently employed by the Aleutian hunters in Russian America. These whalebones which you see, my friends,—well! when it freezes, I shall bend them, and I shall water them until they are entirely coated with a layer of ice which will keep them bent; and I shall sow them on the snow, having first hidden them under a layer of fat. Now, what will happen if a hungry animal swallows one of these baits? Why, the heat of its stomach will melt the ice, and the whalebone, springing straight, will pierce it with its sharpened ends.”

“That is ingenious!” said Pencroff.

“And it will save powder and ball,” replied Smith.

“That is better than the traps!” added Neb.

“Let us wait for the winter, then!”

“Let us wait for the winter.”

Meanwhile the building of the boat was going forward, and towards the end of the month she was half planked. It could already be seen that her shape would be excellent for keeping the sea well.

Pencroff worked with an ardor beyond compare, and it needed his robust nature to withstand such fatigues. But his companions were secretly preparing for him a reward for so much toil, and on the 31st of May he was to experience one of the greatest joys of his life.

That day, at the end of dinner, just as he was about to leave the table, Pencroff felt a hand laid on his shoulder.

It was the hand of Spilett, who said to him:—

“One moment, Pencroff; one does not go off in that way! And the dessert, which you are forgetting?”

“Thank you, Mr. Spilett,” replied the sailor; “I am going back to work.”

“Well, a cup of coffee, my friend?”

“Not that either.”

“A pipe, then?”

Pencroff had risen suddenly, and his good broad face turned pale when he saw the reporter holding out to him a pipe ready filled, and Herbert a glowing coal.

The sailor tried to utter a word, without being able to do so; but, seizing the pipe, he carried it to his lips; then, putting the coal to it, he drew five or six whiffs one after another.

A bluish and fragrant cloud spread out, and from the depths of this cloud a delirious voice was heard, repeating:—

“Tobacco! real tobacco!”

“Yes, Pencroff,” replied Smith, “and excellent tobacco too!”

“Oh! Divine Providence! sacred Author of all things!” cried the sailor. “Then there is nothing more wanting to our island!”

And Pencroff smoked, and smoked, and smoked!

“And who made this discovery?” he asked at last. “You, no doubt, Herbert?”

“No, Pencroff, it was Mr. Spilett.”

“Mr. Spilett!” cried the sailor, clasping to his breast the reporter, who had never undergone such an embrace.

“Phew! Pencroff,” replied Spilett, recovering his breath, which had been for a moment in danger. “Give a share of your gratitude to Herbert, who recognized the plant, to Cyrus, who prepared it, and to Neb, who had a great deal of trouble to keep our secret!”

“Well, my friends, I will repay you for it some day!” replied the sailor. “Now it is for life and for death!”
