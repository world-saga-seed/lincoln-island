# Craft principles the method grew

These are the craft rules that came out of the checks. They are not universal laws. They are what a long, carefully caused book needed. Take the ones that fit yours, and write them into your `plan/README.md` or voice sheet.

## Causes, not convenience

- **Every big event has a cause on the page or in the notes.** Help that arrives exactly when needed, from someone the story never set up, is a setup fault. So is a power used once that would solve every later problem. The setup critic looks for both.
- **Chance needs a cause too.** A coincidence is allowed if the notes give a reason it was likely (the stranger was there because the market day brings people from the hills).
- **The world is no fool.** If something in the setting is backward, there is a reason it is the right choice under the setting's rules. Never "they never thought of it."
- **Echoes of real history need real causes.** If an outcome rhymes with something from history (a price collapse, a guild war), list what caused it there and find what causes it here. An outcome with a missing cause reads as the author reaching for history (`plan/workflows/echo-audit.md`).

## Show the parts; let one word name the whole

When the reader must understand a large system (a works, a market, a city's water), the narrator walks it part by part, concrete and unexplained, and at most one line from a character names the whole. Never a lecture; never the narrator summarizing the mechanism. The reasons live in the notes.

## The page does not explain how things work

A scene that stops to explain the machinery turns the story into a manual. The mechanism critic flags narration that states a cause the narrator could not know, a character stating a truth they could not know, and a scene that pauses for exposition. A local giving the common explanation is not a finding: that is the setting talking, and it may be wrong.

## Puzzles and clues

- **Never trim a clue.** A line that lets a reader work something out (a name, an order, a number, a detail that rereads) is never removed by a trim, cut or revision. A skim-driven trim sees only a dull list; it cannot see the clue in it. Clues are listed in a register (`plan/reread-hooks.md`) and in each chapter's notes. A clue in a dull passage is trimmed around, never through.
- **Clues outrank interest.** Where a skimmed passage holds a clue, make it livelier around the clue, or leave it to the writer.
- **Test the hooks.** A blind reader who guesses the payoff early means the hook is too plain. An informed reader who cannot find the plant means it is too faint, or was cut (`plan/workflows/series-critic.md`).

## Lulls and payoffs

- **A quiet stretch is fine if it sets something up.** The lull rule (read the spread; flag a lull only when nothing it sets up pays off, or when two run together) is in [the interest check](../../plan/workflows/interest-check.md#reading-the-scores).
- **Give a weak quiet chapter an outside pressure, not more incident.** Ask whether a low stretch lacks outside pressure or a widening cast; a quiet stretch holds on one of the two. Look for an existing thread it can share (the unanswered letter, the visitor who never left), carry one beat of it per weak chapter, escalate without a fight, and leave the main plot alone.
- **Small embarrassments, set to a beat.** The best slice-of-life moments are timed: plant an innocent exchange, delay, an adult who understands, a flat answer that covers it, one visible reaction the narrator reports without reading. Place them as relief in heavy stretches.

## Variety by who does it

The same task done by different people should be done differently. A fight, a hunt, a negotiation, a repair: ask first whose method it is, and what that group has too much or too little of (money, numbers, training, time). Vary the method by the people, not by making the problem bigger. That is how a long series avoids sameness.

## Voice

- **Voice a character from the whole person, never from their job.** A knight's rank is not her personality. The further a revision moves a character from their center, the more they become a stock type.
- **Revise paired tellings together.** Two accounts of one scene from two people are revised side by side, never one alone.
- **Habits come from the person's trade or life.** When a habit is needed, derive it from what they do all day. Drafters copy tics between characters; the habits critic catches it. Give each person only their own. Never sweep away the writer's own sample lines.
- **No hindsight in a told-as-it-happens past tense.** No "later I learned", no "I never saw her again".
- **Restraint is not a fault.** A narrator who withholds feelings and mysteries can read as strange to a reader who has not met the convention. Keep the withholding in feeling and mystery. Never withhold orientation: who, where, when, and what a term means.
- **Mark every shift.** A change of narrator or a jump in time is marked on the page (a heading, a date, a framing line). Readers who struggle to reorient, and many do, put a book down at an unmarked jump.

## Mechanize the expensive

Some devices cost a human author too much bookkeeping: a narrator whose voice ages over ten years, a viewpoint that knows only what it has lived, a dozen offstage timelines. With ledgers, critics and greps, those can be carried. Do not reject a craft choice because it is hard to keep consistent. Ask what it would take to mechanize it (a sheet, a ledger, a critic question, a grep), and propose that with the choice.

## Judge the drafted text, not the outline

An outline can read as something the drafted scene is not. A scene designed to cut against a genre's expectations may look, in outline, exactly like the thing it subverts. Judge taste and register on the drafted text.

## Small thoughts, held for scene time

A good detail often arrives far from the scene that will use it. Take it when it comes, record it where that scene's drafter will find it (the outline beat, the sheet), and never drop it because the scene is far off.
