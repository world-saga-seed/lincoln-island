# The Seal Hunt and the First Iron (draft 1)

CHAPTER XV.

WINTERING ABSOLUTELY DECIDED UPON—THE METALLURGIC QUESTION—THE EXPLORATION OF SAFETY ISLAND—A SEAL HUNT—CAPTURE OF AN ECHIDNA—THE KOULA—WHAT IS CALLED THE CATALONIAN METHOD—MAKING IRON—HOW STEEL IS OBTAINED.

The first words of the sailor, on the next day, the 17th of April, were addressed to Gideon Spilett:—

“Well, sir,” he asked, “what shall we be to-day?”

“Whatever Mr. Smith chooses,” answered the reporter.

The companions of the engineer, having been until then brickmakers and potters, were about to become metal-workers.

The previous day, after lunch, the party had explored as far as the extremity of Mandible Cape, nearly seven miles from the Chimneys. The extensive downs here came to an end and the soil appeared volcanic. There were no longer high walls, as at Prospect Plateau, but the narrow gulf between the two capes was enframed by a fantastic border of the mineral matter discharged from the volcano. Having reached this point, the colonists retraced their steps, and at nightfall they re-entered the Chimneys, but they did not go to sleep until the question whether they should look forwards to leaving Lincoln Island had been definitely settled.

The 1,200 miles to the Low Archipelago was a long distance. A small boat would not be able to accomplish it, especially at the approach of the bad season. Pencroff had formally declared as much. Now, the building of a simple boat, even when the proper tools are provided, is a difficult task, and as the colonists had none of these, the first thing to do was to make hammers, hatchets, adzes, saws, augers, planes, etc., which would take some time. It was therefore decided to winter on Lincoln Island, and to search for a more comfortable dwelling than the Chimneys in which to pass the winter months.

The first thing was to utilize the iron ore, of which the engineer had observed some deposits in the northwest part of the island, and to transform it either into iron or into steel.

Metals are not generally found in the soil in a pure state; for the most part they are found in combination with oxygen or sulphur. And it was so in this instance, as of the two specimens brought back by Cyrus Smith one was magnetic iron, not carbonated, and the other pyrites or sulphuret of iron. Of these, it was the first kind, the oxide of iron, which must be reduced by coal, that is to say, freed from the oxygen, in order to obtain the pure metal. This reduction is performed by submitting the ore, in the presence of coal, to a great heat, either by the rapid and easy Catalonian method, which has the advantage of transforming the ore directly into iron at one operation, or by means of blast furnaces which first change the ore into cast iron, and then the cast iron into iron, carrying off the 3 or 4 per cent of coal combined with it.

Now, what did Cyrus Smith want? Iron, and not cast iron; and he must seek the most rapid method of reduction. Besides, the ore he had found was in itself very pure and very rich. It was the oxydulated ore, which, found in confused masses of a dark grey, yields a black dust, crystallizes in regular octahedrons, furnishes natural magnets, and serves in Europe for the making of those irons of the first quality with which Sweden and Norway are so abundantly provided. Not far from this vein was the coal field previously worked by the colonists, so that every facility existed for the treatment of the ore, since the elements of the manufacture were close at hand. It is this, indeed, which makes the prodigious wealth of the works of the United Kingdom, where the coal serves to make the metal extracted from the same soil and at the same time as itself.

“Then, sir, are we going to work the iron ore?” questioned Pencroff.

“Yes, my friend,” answered the engineer, “and for that—which will not displease you—we shall begin with a seal hunt on the islet.”

“A seal hunt!” cried the sailor, turning towards Spilett. “Do we need seals, then, to make iron?”

“Since Cyrus says so!” replied the reporter.

But as the engineer had already left the Chimneys, Pencroff prepared for the chase without gaining any other explanation.

Soon Cyrus Smith, Herbert, Gideon Spilett, Neb, and the sailor were gathered upon the beach at a point where the channel could be forded at low water. The tide was at its lowest ebb, and the hunters were able to cross the channel without wading deeper than the knee.

This was Smith’s first visit to the islet, and his companions’ second, since it was there that the balloon had first thrown them. On their landing, some hundreds of penguins looked at them with candid eyes, and the colonists armed with clubs could easily have killed them, but they did not think of giving themselves up to this doubly useless slaughter, for it would not do to frighten the seals which were lying on the sand some cable lengths away. They respected also certain very innocent spheniscus, whose wings, mere stumps, were flattened in the form of fins, and covered with scale-like feathers.

The colonists marched stealthily forward towards the north point, over ground riddled with little holes which formed so many nests of aquatic birds. Towards the end of the islet, large black objects appeared, swimming on the surface of the water; they looked like the heads of rocks in motion. They were the seals the hunters wished to capture.

It was necessary to allow them to land, as, with their narrow pelvis, their short close hair, and their spindle-shaped form, these animals, capital swimmers, are difficult to seize in the sea, while on the shore their short webbed feet allow them only a slow, crawling movement. Pencroff, who knew their habits, counselled waiting until the seals were stretched on the sand, in the rays of the sun, which would soon plunge them into a deep sleep. Then the party could manage so as to cut off their retreat and despatch them with a blow on the muzzle.

The hunters therefore hid themselves behind the rocks of the shore and waited quietly.

An hour passed before the seals came to sport upon the sand; there were half a dozen of them. Pencroff and Herbert then went off round the point of the islet, so as to take them in the rear and cut off their retreat, while Cyrus Smith, Gideon Spilett, and Neb, crawling along the rocks, crept forward to the future scene of the encounter.

Suddenly the tall form of the sailor was seen. He gave a shout, and the engineer and his two companions hurriedly threw themselves between the seals and the sea. Two of the animals, vigorously struck, lay dead on the sand, but the others regained the sea and made off.

“Here are your seals, Mr. Smith,” cried the sailor, coming forward.

“Good,” replied the engineer. “We will make forge-bellows of them!”

“Forge-bellows!” exclaimed the sailor. “Well! these seals are in luck!”

It was, in effect, a blowing machine, necessary in the reduction of the ore, which the engineer expected to make from the skins of the seals. They were medium-sized, for their length did not exceed six feet, and had heads resembling those of dogs.

As it was useless to burden themselves with so considerable a weight as that of the two animals, Neb and Pencroff resolved to skin them on the spot, while Smith and the reporter finished the exploration of the islet.

The sailor and the negro acquitted themselves well, and three hours later Smith had at his disposal two seal skins, which he intended to use just as they were, without tanning.

The colonists, waiting until low water, re-crossed the channel and returned to the Chimneys.

It was no easy matter to stretch the skins upon wooden frames intended to keep them distended, and to sew them with fibres, so that they might hold the air without too much leakage. It had to be done over again several times. Smith had nothing but the two steel blades from Top’s collar to work with, yet he was so ingenious and his companions aided him so intelligently, that, three days later, the number of implements of the little colony was increased by a bellows intended to inject air into the midst of the ore during its treatment by heat—a condition indispensable to the success of the operation.

It was on the morning of the 20th of April that what the reporter called in his notes the “metallurgic period” began. The engineer had decided, as is known, to work upon the very deposits of coal and iron, which, according to his observations, were situated at the base of the northeasterly spurs of Mount Franklin, six miles from the Chimneys. And as it would not be possible to go back and forth each day, it was agreed that the little colony should camp upon the ground in a hut of branches, so that they could attend to the important work night and day.

This settled, they left in the morning, Neb and Pencroff dragging the bellows on a hurdle, with a stock of vegetable and animal provisions, which latter they would add to on the way.

The road led obliquely through the thickest part of Jacamar Wood, from southeast to northwest. It was necessary to break a path which would henceforth be the most direct route between Prospect Plateau and Mount Franklin. The trees belonging to the species already recognized were magnificent, and Herbert pointed out some new ones, among others the dragon trees, which Pencroff designated as “pretentious leeks,” for, notwithstanding their height, they belong to the same family of liliaceous plants as the onion, the civet, the shallot, or the asparagus. These dragon trees have ligneous roots which, cooked, are excellent, and which, fermented, yield a very agreeable liquor. They therefore gathered some.

It took the entire day to traverse the wood, but the party were thus able to observe its fauna and flora. Top, specially charged to look after the fauna, ran about in the grass and bushes, flushing all kinds of game. Herbert and Spilett killed two kangaroos with arrows, and besides, an animal which was very like a hedge-hog, in that it rolled itself into a ball and erected its quills, and like an ant-eater, in that it was provided with claws for digging, a long and thin snout terminating in a bird’s beak, and an extensile tongue furnished with little points, which enabled it to hold insects.

“And when it is in the pot,” asked Pencroff, naturally, “what will it be like?”

“Like an excellent piece of beef,” answered Herbert.

“We won’t ask any more of it than that,” said the sailor.

During the march they saw some wild boars, but they did not attempt to attack the little troop, and it seemed that they were not going to have any encounter with savage beasts, when the reporter thought he saw in a dense thicket, a few paces from him, among the lower branches of a tree, an animal which he took to be a bear, and which he began tranquilly to sketch. Very fortunately for Spilett, the animal in question did not belong to that redoubtable family of plantigrades. It was only a “koula,” better known as a sloth, which was the size of a large dog, with bristling hair of a dirty color, and feet armed with strong claws, which enabled it to climb trees and feed upon the leaves. Having identified the animal without disturbing it, Spilett struck out “bear” under his drawing and wrote “koula” in its place, and the route was resumed.

At 5 o’clock in the evening Smith called a halt. They were past the forest and at the beginning of the massive spurs which buttressed Mount Franklin towards the east. A few hundred paces distant was Red Creek; so drinking water was not far off.

The camp was immediately organized. In less than an hour a hut of branches interlaced with creepers, and stopped with clay, was erected under the trees on the edge of the forest, and afforded a sufficient shelter. They deferred the geological work until the next day. Supper was prepared, a good fire blazed before the hut, the spit turned, and at 8 o’clock, while one of the party kept the fire going, in case some dangerous beast should prowl around, the others slept soundly.

The next day, the 21st of April, Smith, accompanied by Herbert, went to look for the soil of ancient formation upon which he had already found a specimen of ore. He found the deposit on the surface, almost at the very sources of the creek, at the foot of the lateral base of one of the northeast buttresses. This mineral, very rich in iron, enclosed in its fusible vein-stone, was perfectly suited to the method of reduction which the engineer intended to employ, which was the simplified Catalonian process practised in Corsica.

In fact, the Catalonian method, properly so called, requires the construction of ovens and crucibles in which the ore and the coal, placed in alternate layers, are transformed and reduced. But Smith proposed to dispense with these constructions, and simply to make a cube of coal and ore, into the centre of which the air from his bellows would be introduced. This was, probably, what Tubal Cain and the first metallurgists of the inhabited world did. And a process which had given such good results to the grandsons of Adam, and which still gave good results in countries rich in ore and fuel, could not fail to succeed with the colonists of Lincoln Island in the circumstances in which they were placed.

The coal, like the ore, was collected without trouble, not far off, on the surface of the ground. The ore was first broken into little pieces, and the impurities which soiled its surface were picked from it by hand. Then the coal and ore were heaped together in successive layers—just as a charcoal-burner arranges the wood which he wishes to carbonize. Thus arranged, under the influence of the air from the bellows, the coal would change into carbonic acid, then into oxide of carbon, which would reduce the oxide of iron, that is to say, release the oxygen from it.

The engineer proceeded in this manner. The sealskin bellows, furnished with a pipe of refractory earth, which had previously been prepared at the pottery, was set up close to the heap of ore. And, moved by a mechanism consisting of a frame, fibre-cords, and balance-weight, it injected into the mass a supply of air, which, by raising the temperature, assisted the chemical transformation which would give the pure metal.

The operation was difficult. It took all the patience and ingenuity of the colonists to conduct it properly; but finally it succeeded, and the final result was a bloom of iron, reduced to a spongy state, which must be shingled and wrought, that is to say, forged, in order to expel the liquified gangue. It was evident that these self-constituted smiths wanted the first hammer, but they were no worse off than the first metallurgist, and they did as he must have done.

The first bloom, fastened to a wooden handle, served as a hammer with which to forge the second upon an anvil of granite, and they thus obtained a coarse metal, but one which could be utilized.

At length, after much trouble and labor, on the 25th of April, several bars of iron had been forged and turned into tools, pincers, tongs, pickaxes, mattocks, etc., which Pencroff and Neb declared to be real jewels.

But it was not as pure iron that it could render great services; it was above all as steel. Now steel, which is a combination of iron and carbon, is made in two ways: either from cast iron, by taking from it its excess of carbon, or from iron, by adding to it the carbon which it lacks. The first, obtained by decarburetting the cast iron, gives natural or puddled steel; the second, produced by carburetting the iron, gives the steel of cementation.

It was therefore the latter which the engineer must seek to make by preference, as he had iron in a pure state; and he succeeded by heating the metal with powdered charcoal in a crucible made from refractory earth.

Then this steel, which is malleable hot and cold, he worked with the hammer. And Neb and Pencroff, skillfully directed, made axe-heads, which, heated red-hot and quickly plunged in cold water, took an excellent temper.

Other instruments were thus manufactured, rudely fashioned, it need hardly be said: plane-irons, axes, hatchets, bands of steel which were to be made into saws, carpenters’ chisels; and then the irons of mattocks, shovels, and pickaxes, hammers, nails, etc.

By the 5th of May the first metallurgic period was ended, the smiths returned to the Chimneys, and new work would soon authorize their assumption of a new title.
