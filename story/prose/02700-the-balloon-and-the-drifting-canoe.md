# The Balloon and the Drifting Canoe (final)

CHAPTER XXVII.

PROPOSAL TO RETURN BY THE SOUTH COAST—ITS CONFIGURATION—SEARCH FOR THE SUPPOSED WRECK—A WAIF IN THE AIR—DISCOVERY OF A SMALL NATURAL HARBOR—MIDNIGHT ON THE MERCY—A DRIFTING CANOE.

Smith and his companions slept like innocent marmots in the cavern which the jaguar had so politely vacated, and, by sunrise, all were on the shore, at the very extremity of the promontory, and again scrutinizing the horizon, which was visible over two thirds of its circumference. For the last time the engineer was able to ascertain that no sail, no hull of a ship, was to be seen upon the sea, and not even with the spy-glass could any suspicious object be discerned. It was the same along the shore, at least on that straight portion, three miles in length, which formed the south side of the promontory; as, beyond that, an indentation of the land concealed the rest of the coast, and even from the extremity of Serpentine Peninsula, Claw Cape was hidden by high rocks.

The southern bank of the island remained to be explored. Had they not better attempt this at once, and give up this day, the 2nd of November, to it? This procedure had not entered into their first calculations, as, when the canoe was left at the sources of the Mercy, it had been agreed that, having explored the west coast, they would return for it, and go back to Granite House by way of the Mercy; Smith having then believed that this west coast might offer refuge either to a vessel in distress or to a ship on her regular course. But as soon as this shore disclosed no landing place, it became necessary to search the south side of the island for what they had failed to discover on the west.

It was Spilett who proposed continuing the exploration so as to settle definitely the question of the supposed shipwreck, and he inquired how far it would be from the end of the peninsula to Claw Cape.

“About thirty miles,” answered the engineer, “if we allow for the irregularity of the shore.”

“Thirty miles!” exclaimed Spilett, “that will be a hard day’s march. Nevertheless, I think we should return to Granite House by the south coast.”

“But,” observed Herbert, “from Claw Cape to Granite House is at least ten miles further.”

“Call it forty miles altogether,” answered the reporter, “and do not let us hesitate to do it. At least we will have seen this unknown shore, and will not have it to explore over again.”

“Very true,” said Pencroff. “But how about the canoe?”

“The canoe has stayed alone for a day at the sources of the Mercy,” replied Spilett; “it may well stay there two! Up to the present we can hardly say that the island is infested with thieves!”

“Nevertheless,” said the sailor, “when I remember that affair of the turtle, I am not so confident.”

“The turtle! the turtle!” cried the reporter, “don’t you know that the sea turned it over?”

“Who can say?” murmured the engineer.

“But—,” began Neb, who, it was evident, wished to say something, for he opened his mouth to speak and did not speak.

“What is it, Neb?” questioned the engineer.

“If we do return by the shore to Claw Cape,” replied Neb, “after having gone round it, we will be stopped—”

“By the Mercy!” cried Herbert. “Indeed, and we will have neither bridge nor boat to cross it!”

“Oh, Mr. Smith,” answered Pencroff, “we can cross it readily enough with some floating logs!”

“Nevertheless,” said Spilett, “it would be well to build a bridge some time if we wish to have ready access to the Far West.”

“A bridge!” cried Pencroff. “Well, isn’t Mr. Smith an engineer by trade? He will build us a bridge when we want a bridge! As to carrying you over the Mercy to-night without wetting a thread of your clothes, I will look out for that. We still have a day’s provision, which is all that is necessary, and, besides, the game may not give out to-day as it did yesterday. So let us go.”

The proposal of the reporter, very warmly seconded by the sailor, obtained general approval, as every one wished to end their doubts, and by returning by Claw Cape the exploration would be complete. But there was not an hour to lose, for a tramp of forty miles was long, and they could not count on reaching Granite House before night. So by 6 o’clock the little party was on its way, the guns loaded with ball in case of bad encounters with animals of two legs or of four, and Top, who went ahead, ordered to search the edge of the forest.

From the extremity of the promontory which formed the tail of the peninsula, the coast curved round for a distance of five miles, which was rapidly traversed, without the most minute search revealing the slightest trace of a landing, old or recent, nor a waif, nor the remains of a camp, nor the ashes of an extinguished fire, nor a footprint! When the colonists arrived at the angle where the curve ended, and the coast turned north-east to form Washington Bay, they were able to take in at one view the whole extent of the southern coast. Twenty-five miles distant the shore was terminated by Claw Cape, which was faintly visible through the morning mists, and which a mirage raised, as if it had been suspended between land and water. Between the place occupied by the colonists and the upper end of the immense bay the shore began with a wide beach, very smooth and very flat, bordered in the background by a fringe of trees; following this, it became very irregular, and thrust sharp points into the sea, and finally a heap of black rocks, thrown together in picturesque disorder, completed the distance to Claw Cape.

Such was the outline of this part of the island, which the explorers now saw for the first time, and which they took in at a glance, after halting for a moment.

“A ship which ran aground here,” said Pencroff, “would inevitably be lost. Sand-banks stretching out to sea, and further on, reefs! It is poor quarters!”

“But at least a portion of her would be left,” observed the reporter.

“Some bits of wood would remain on the reefs, nothing on the sands,” answered the sailor.

“How is that?”

“Because the sands are even more dangerous than the rocks, and swallow up everything that is thrown upon them; a few days suffice to bury out of sight the hull of a ship of several hundred tons measurement.”

“Then, Pencroff,” questioned the engineer, “if a vessel had been lost on these banks, it would not be surprising if there was no trace left?”

“No, sir, with the help of time or of a tempest. Nevertheless, it would be surprising, even in that case, that no fragments of masts or spars were thrown upon the shore beyond the reach of the sea.”

“Let us continue our search,” replied Smith.

By 1 o’clock the party had accomplished twenty miles, having reached the upper end of Washington Bay, and they stopped to lunch.

Here began an irregular shore, oddly jagged, and fringed by a long line of those reefs which succeeded the sand banks, and which the tide, slack at that moment, would soon uncover. The supple undulations of the sea, breaking on the heads of the rocks, could be seen spreading over them in long streaks of foam. From this point to Claw Cape the beach was narrow and confined between the reef of rocks and the forest, and the march would therefore be more difficult, for innumerable fallen rocks encumbered the shore. The granite wall also tended to rise higher and higher, and of the trees which crowned it behind, only the green tops, undisturbed by a breath of air, could be seen.

After half an hour’s rest the colonists took up the march again, and their eyes left no point of the reefs or the beach unobserved. Pencroff and Neb even ventured among the reefs whenever any object attracted their attention. But of a waif there was none; they had been deceived by some odd formation of the rocks. They found out, however, that edible shell-fish were plenty on this beach, although the beach could not be profitably worked until a communication should have been established between the two banks of the Mercy, and the means of transport should have been perfected.

Thus nothing relating to the supposed shipwreck appeared on this shore, and yet an object of any importance, the hull of a vessel for instance, would then have been visible, or its fragments would have been carried ashore, as the box had been, which was found less than twenty miles from there. But there was nothing.

Towards 3 o’clock, Smith and his companions reached a narrow, well-enclosed inlet, unfed by any water-course. It formed a veritable little natural harbor, invisible from without, and approached by a narrow passage guarded by the reefs. At the upper end of this creek some violent convulsion had shattered the rock, and a cutting, hollowed out in a gentle slope, gave access to the upper plateau, which might be less than ten miles from Claw Cape, and therefore four miles in a direct line from Prospect Plateau.

Spilett proposed to his companions to halt here, and, as the march had sharpened their appetites, although it was not dinner time, no one objected to a bit of venison, and with this lunch they would be able to await supper at Granite House.

Soon the colonists, seated at the foot of a group of splendid maritime pines, were eating heartily of the provisions which Neb had brought out from his haversack. The place was some fifty or sixty feet above the sea, and the view, extending beyond the furthest rock of the cape, was lost in Union Bay. But the islet and Prospect Plateau were not visible, nor could they be at that time, as the high ground and the curtain of high trees abruptly shut out the horizon to the north. It is needless to add that, in spite of the extent of sea which the explorers could take in, and although the engineer’s glass had swept, point by point, the whole of that circular line where sky and water met, no ship was perceived.

In the same way, over all that part of the coast which it was still necessary to explore, the glass was passed with the same care from the beach to the reefs, and no waif appeared in the field of the instrument.

“Well,” said Spilett, “we must make the best of it, and console ourselves by thinking that no one will come to dispute with us the possession of Lincoln Island!”

“But how about the pellet?” said Herbert. “It is not imaginary, I suppose!”

“A thousand devils, no!” cried Pencroff, thinking of his missing tooth.

“Well, what are we to conclude?” asked the reporter.

“This,” said Smith, “that within three months a ship, voluntarily or otherwise, has touched—”

“What! You would admit, Cyrus, that it has been swallowed up without leaving any trace?” cried the reporter.

“No, my dear Spilett; but you must remember that while it is certain that a human being has been here, it seems just as certain that he has now left it.”

“Then, if I understand you sir,” said Herbert, “the ship has gone again?”

“Evidently.”

“And we have lost, beyond return, a chance to get home?” said Neb.

“Beyond return, I fear.”

“Well then, since the chance is lost, let us push on,” said Pencroff, already home-sick for Granite House.

But scarcely had he risen, when Top’s barking resounded loudly, and the dog burst from the wood, holding in his mouth a rag of cloth soiled with mud.

Neb snatched it from the dog’s mouth. It was a bit of strong cloth. Top, still barking, seemed by his motions to invite his master to follow into the wood.

“Here is something which may well explain my pellet!” cried Pencroff.

“A shipwrecked person!” answered Herbert.

“Wounded, perhaps!” exclaimed Neb.

“Or dead!” responded the reporter.

And all, holding their arms in readiness, hurried after the dog, among the tall pines which formed the first curtain of the forest. They advanced some distance into the wood, but, to their great disappointment, they saw no tracks. The underbrush and lianas were uninjured and had to be cut away with the hatchet, as in the depths of the forest. It was hard to imagine that any human creature had passed there, and yet Top went and came, not like a dog searching at random, but like a being endowed with a will, which follows an idea.

After seven or eight minutes’ walk the dog stopped. The colonists, who had arrived at a sort of glade surrounded by high trees, looked all about them, but neither in the underbrush or between the tree trunks could they discover a thing.

“What is it, Top?” said Smith.

Top, barking louder, leaped at the foot of a gigantic pine.

Suddenly Pencroff exclaimed:—

“Ah! good! Ah! capital!”

“What’s that?” asked Spilett.

“We’ve been hunting for some waif on the sea or land—”

“Well?”

“And here it is in the air!”

And the sailor pointed out a sort of great whitish rag, caught on the summit of the pine, a piece of which, fallen to the ground, Top had brought back.

“But that is no waif!” exclaimed Spilett.

“Indeed it is,” answered Pencroff.

“What! is it—?”

“It is all that is left of our air-boat, of our balloon, which has stranded up there, on the top of this tree!”

Pencroff was not mistaken, and he gave a magnificent hurrah, adding:—

“And there is good stuff in it which will keep us in linen for years. It will make us handkerchiefs and shirts. Aha, Mr. Spilett! what do you say of an island where shirts grow on the trees?”

It was, indeed, a fortunate thing for the colonists that the aerostat, after having made its last bound into the air, had fallen again on the island, and that they had had the luck to find it. They could either keep the envelope in its present shape, in case they might desire to attempt a new escape through the air, or, after having taken off the varnish, they could make use of its hundreds of ells of good cotton cloth. As may well be supposed, Pencroff’s joy was keenly shared by all.

But this envelope had to be taken down from the tree on which it hung, and put in a safe place, and it was no easy task. Neb, Herbert, and the sailor, having climbed to the top of the tree, had to perform prodigies of skill to free the enormous collapsed aerostat. The work lasted nearly two hours, and then not only the envelope, with its valve, springs, and copper fittings, but the net, that is to say a considerable quantity of cordage and ropes, together with the retaining ring and the anchor of the balloon, lay upon the ground. The envelope, excepting the rent, was in good order, and only its lower end had been torn away.

It was a fortune fallen from heaven.

“Nevertheless, Mr. Smith,” said the sailor, “if we ever do decide to leave the island it won’t be in a balloon, I hope. These air ships don’t go the way you want them to, as we have found out. If you will let me have my way, we will build a good boat of about twenty tons, and you will allow me to cut from this cloth a foresail and jib. The rest of it will do for clothes.”

“We will see, Pencroff,” answered Smith, “we will see.”

“And meanwhile it must all be put away carefully,” said Neb.

In truth, they could not think of carrying all this weight of cloth, ropes, and cordage to Granite House; and while waiting for a proper means of removing it, it was important not to leave these riches any longer at the mercy of the first hurricane. The colonists, uniting their efforts, succeeded in dragging it to the shore, where they discovered a tolerably large cave in the rocks, so situated that neither wind, rain, nor sea could get at it.

“We wanted a wardrobe, and we have a wardrobe,” said Pencroff; “but since it does not lock, it will be prudent to hide the opening. I do not say so on account of two-footed thieves, but of four-footed ones!”

By 6 o’clock everything was stored away, and after having given the little inlet the very appropriate name of Balloon Harbor, they took the road for Claw Cape. Pencroff and the engineer discussed several projects, which it would be well to attend to at once. The first thing was to build a bridge across the Mercy, so as to establish easy communication with the south of the island; then the cart would come back for the balloon, as the canoe would not have sufficed to carry it. Then to build a decked launch, which Pencroff would make cutter-rigged, and in which they could make voyages of circumnavigation—around the island; then, etc.

In the meantime the night approached, and it was already dark, when the colonists reached Jetsam Point, where they had discovered the precious box. But here, as elsewhere, there was nothing to indicate a shipwreck, and it became necessary to adopt the opinions expressed by Smith.

The four miles from Jetsam Point to Granite House were quickly traversed, but it was past midnight when, after having followed the shore as far as the mouth of the Mercy, the colonists arrived at the first bend formed by the river. There the river was eighty feet wide, and not easy to cross; but Pencroff had undertaken to overcome the difficulty, and he was now called upon to do so. It must be admitted that the colonists were exhausted. The tramp had been long, and the incident of the balloon had not rested their arms or legs. They were therefore anxious to get back to Granite House to supper and bed, and if they had only had the bridge, in a quarter of an hour they could have been at home.

The night was very dark. Pencroff then prepared to keep his promise, by making a sort of raft on which to cross the Mercy. He and Neb, armed with the hatchets, chose two trees near the bank, of which they meant to make the raft, and began cutting them down at the base. Smith and Spilett, seated on the bank, waited until the moment came to assist their companions, and Herbert went to and fro, without going far.

All at once the lad, who had gone up the stream, returned hurriedly, and, pointing up the Mercy, exclaimed:—

“What is that drifting there?”

Pencroff stopped work and perceived a moving object, dimly seen in the gloom.

“A canoe!” he exclaimed.

All came up and saw, to their extreme astonishment, a boat following the current.

“Canoe, ahoy!” shouted Pencroff from a remnant of professional habit, forgetting that it might be better to keep quiet.

There was no answer. The boat continued to drift, and it was not more than a dozen paces off, when the sailor exclaimed:—

“Why, it’s our canoe! She has broken away and drifted down with the current. Well, we must admit that she comes in the nick of time!”

“Our canoe!” murmured the engineer.

Pencroff was right. It was indeed their canoe, which had doubtless broken loose and drifted all the way from the headwaters of the Mercy! It was important to seize it in passing before it should be carried by the rapid current of the river beyond its mouth, and Pencroff and Neb, by the aid of a long pole, adroitly did this, and drew the canoe to the bank.

The engineer stepped in first, and, seizing the rope, assured himself, by feeling it, that it had been really worn in two against the rocks.

“This,” said the reporter in an undertone; “this is a coincidence—”

“Strange!” answered the engineer.

Strange or not, it was fortunate! Herbert, the reporter, Neb, and Pencroff embarked in their turn. They did not doubt that the rope had been worn through by friction; but the astonishing part of the affair was that the canoe had arrived at the moment when the colonists were there to seize it, for a quarter of an hour later, and it would have been carried out to sea. Had they lived in the days of genii, this incident would have given them the right to believe that the island was haunted by a supernatural being, who placed his power at the service of the castaways!

With a few strokes of the oars the party arrived at the mouth of the Mercy. The canoe was drawn on shore at the Chimneys, and all took their way to the ladder at Granite House.

But, just then, Top began barking furiously, and Neb, who was feeling for the lower rung, uttered a cry.

The ladder was gone.
