# Herbert Carried to Granite House (final)

CHAPTER LII.

HERBERT CARRIED TO GRANITE HOUSE—NEB RELATES WHAT HAS HAPPENED—SMITH VISITS THE PLATEAU—RUIN AND DEVASTATION—THE COLONISTS POWERLESS AGAINST DISEASE—THE WILLOW BARK—A MORTAL FEVER—TOP BARKS AGAIN!

Of the convicts, of the dangers which threatened Granite House, of the ruins with which the plateau was covered, there was no longer any question. Herbert's condition outweighed everything. Had the removal been fatal to him, by causing some internal injury? The reporter could not say, but he and his companions were in despair.

The cart was brought to the bend of the river. There, some branches, arranged in the form of a litter, received the mattresses on which Herbert lay unconscious. Ten minutes later, Smith, Spilett, and Pencroff were at the foot of the wall, leaving to Neb the care of taking the cart back to Prospect Plateau.

The elevator was set in motion, and Herbert was soon stretched on his bed in Granite House.

The care which was lavished upon him brought him back to life. He smiled for a moment on finding himself in his own room, but he could scarcely murmur a few words, so great was his weakness.

Spilett examined his wounds. He feared that they had reopened, being imperfectly healed... it was not so. Whence, then, came this prostration? Why had Herbert's state grown worse?

The lad then fell into a sort of feverish sleep, and the reporter and Pencroff remained near his bed.

Meanwhile, Smith told Neb what had happened at the corral, and Neb related to his master the events of which the plateau had just been the theatre.

It was only during the preceding night that the convicts had shown themselves at the edge of the forest, near Glycerine Creek. Neb, who was watching near the poultry-yard, had not hesitated to fire at one of these pirates, who was about to cross the stream. But in that rather dark night he had been unable to tell whether the wretch had been hit. At any rate, it had not been enough to drive off the band, and Neb had only had time to get back up to Granite House, where at least he found himself in safety.

But what was he to do then? How could he prevent the devastation with which the convicts threatened the plateau? Had Neb any means of warning his master? And besides, in what situation were the inmates of the corral themselves?

Smith and his companions had left on the 11th of November, and it was now the 29th. It was, therefore, nineteen days since Neb had had any other news than that which Top had brought him, and that news disastrous: Ayrton gone, Herbert severely wounded, the engineer, the reporter, and the sailor, so to speak, imprisoned in the corral!

What was to be done? poor Neb asked himself. For himself he had nothing to fear, for the convicts could not reach him in Granite House. But the buildings, the plantations, all their improvements at the mercy of the pirates! Was it not fitting to leave Smith to judge what he should do, and to warn him, at least, of the danger which threatened him?

Neb then thought of employing Jup, and of entrusting a note to him. He knew the extreme intelligence of the orang, which had often been put to the proof. Jup understood the word corral, which had often been spoken before him, and it will even be remembered that he had very often driven the cart there in company with Pencroff. Day had not yet appeared. The agile orang would know well how to pass unperceived through the woods, of which, besides, the convicts would take him for one of the natural inhabitants.

Neb did not hesitate. He wrote the note, he tied it round Jup's neck, he brought the ape to the door of Granite House, from which he let a long rope unroll down to the ground; then, several times over, he repeated these words:—

"Jup! Jup! corral! corral!"

The animal understood, seized the rope, slid rapidly down to the beach, and disappeared in the darkness, without the attention of the convicts having been in any way aroused.

"You did well, Neb," replied Smith; "but in not warning us, perhaps you would have done better still!"

And in speaking thus, Smith was thinking of Herbert, whose removal seemed to have so seriously endangered his recovery.

Neb finished his account. The convicts had not shown themselves on the beach. Not knowing the number of the inhabitants of the island, they might suppose that Granite House was defended by a considerable force. They must have remembered that, during the attack on the brig, numerous shots had greeted them from the lower rocks as well as from the upper, and doubtless they did not wish to expose themselves. But Prospect Plateau lay open to them, and was in no way swept by the fire of Granite House. There, therefore, they gave themselves up to their instinct of plunder, sacking, burning, doing mischief for mischief's sake, and they only withdrew half an hour before the arrival of the colonists, whom they must have believed still confined in the corral.

Neb had rushed out of his retreat. He had climbed up to the plateau, at the risk of receiving some bullet there; he had tried to put out the fire which was consuming the buildings of the poultry-yard, and he had struggled against the flames, but in vain, up to the moment when the cart appeared at the edge of the wood.

Such had been these grave events. The presence of the convicts was a permanent menace to the colonists of Lincoln Island, until then so happy, and who might expect still greater misfortunes!

Spilett remained in Granite House with Herbert and Pencroff, while Smith, accompanied by Neb, went to judge for himself of the extent of the disaster.

It was fortunate that the convicts had not advanced as far as the foot of Granite House. The workshops at the Chimneys would not have escaped devastation. But, after all, that harm would perhaps have been more easily repaired than the ruins heaped upon Prospect Plateau!

Smith and Neb proceeded towards the Mercy and went up its left bank, without meeting any trace of the passage of the convicts. On the other side of the river, in the depth of the wood, they perceived no suspicious sign either.

Besides, this is what might be admitted, in all probability: either the convicts knew of the return of the colonists to Granite House, for they might have seen them pass along the road from the corral; or, after the devastation of the plateau, they had gone into Jacamar Wood, following the course of the Mercy, and knew nothing of this return.

In the first case, they must have gone back towards the corral, now without defenders, and which contained resources precious to them.

In the second, they must have regained their encampment, to wait there for some opportunity of renewing the attack.

There would therefore be reason to forestall them; but every enterprise intended to rid the island of them was still subordinate to Herbert's condition. Indeed, Smith would have need of all his forces, and no one could, at that moment, leave Granite House.

The engineer and Neb arrived on the plateau. It was a scene of desolation. The fields had been trampled down, and the ears of the harvest, which was about to be gathered, lay upon the ground; the other plantations had suffered no less, and the kitchen garden was turned upside down. Happily, Granite House possessed a reserve of seed which would allow these damages to be repaired.

As to the mill and the buildings of the poultry-yard, and the stable of the onaggas, the fire had destroyed everything.

A few frightened animals were roaming over the plateau. The birds, which during the fire had taken refuge on the waters of the lake, were already returning to their usual place and dabbling along the banks. There, everything would have to be done again.

Smith's face, paler than usual, showed an inward anger which he did not master without difficulty, but he did not utter a word. For a last time he looked at his ravaged fields, at the smoke which still rose from the ruins; then he returned to Granite House.

The days which followed were the saddest that the colonists had yet passed upon the island! Herbert's weakness visibly increased. It seemed that a more serious illness, the consequence of the profound physiological disturbance which he had undergone, threatened to declare itself, and Spilett foresaw such an aggravation of his state that he would be powerless to combat it!

In fact, Herbert remained in a sort of almost continual drowsiness, and some symptoms of delirium began to show themselves. Cooling drinks were the only remedies at the disposal of the colonists. The fever was not yet very strong, but it soon appeared to be settling into regular fits.

Spilett recognized this on the 6th of December. The poor boy, whose fingers, nose, and ears became extremely pale, was first seized with slight shiverings, with horripilations, with tremblings. His pulse was small and irregular, his skin dry, his thirst intense. This stage was soon followed by a stage of heat; his face grew flushed, his skin reddened, his pulse quickened; then an abundant sweat broke out, after which the fever appeared to diminish. The fit had lasted about five hours.

Spilett had not left Herbert, who was now seized with an intermittent fever, that was only too certain. And this fever must at any cost be cut short before it became more serious.

"And to cut it short," said Spilett to Smith, "we need a febrifuge."

"A febrifuge!" replied the engineer. "We have neither cinchona nor sulphate of quinine!"

"No," said Spilett, "but there are willows on the border of the lake, and willow bark may sometimes take the place of quinine."

"Let us try it, then, without losing a moment!" replied Smith.

Willow bark, in fact, has been justly considered a substitute for cinchona, as well as the horse-chestnut, the leaf of the holly, the serpentaria, etc. It was evidently necessary to try this substance, although it was not worth so much as cinchona, and to employ it in its natural state, since the means were wanting to extract its alkaloid, that is to say, salicine.

Smith himself went to cut some pieces of bark from the trunk of a kind of black willow; he brought them back to Granite House, he reduced them to powder, and this powder was given to Herbert that same evening.

The night passed without grave incident. Herbert had some delirium, but the fever did not reappear in the night, nor did it return the following day.

Pencroff regained some hope. Spilett said nothing. It might be that the intermissions were not daily, that the fever was tertian, in a word, and that it would return on the morrow. That morrow, therefore, was awaited with the keenest anxiety.

It might be remarked, moreover, that during the apyretic period Herbert remained as if broken, his head heavy and subject to giddiness. Another symptom alarmed the reporter to the last degree: Herbert's liver was beginning to be congested, and soon a more intense delirium showed that his brain was also being affected.

Spilett was overwhelmed by this new complication. He took the engineer aside.

"It is a pernicious fever!" said he.

"A pernicious fever!" cried Smith. "You are mistaken, Spilett. A pernicious fever does not declare itself spontaneously. One must have had the germ of it!"

"I am not mistaken," replied the reporter. "Herbert has doubtless contracted the germ in the marshes of the island, and that is enough. He has already had one fit. If a second fit comes on, and if we do not succeed in preventing the third... he is lost!"

"But this willow bark?"

"It is insufficient," replied the reporter, "and a third fit of pernicious fever, which is not cut short by means of quinine, is always mortal!"

Happily, Pencroff had heard nothing of this conversation. He would have gone mad.

It may be understood in what anxiety the engineer and the reporter passed that day, the 7th of December, and the night which followed it.

Towards the middle of the day the second fit came on. The crisis was terrible. Herbert felt himself lost! He stretched out his arms towards Smith, towards Spilett, towards Pencroff! He did not wish to die!... The scene was heartrending. Pencroff had to be sent away.

The fit lasted five hours. It was evident that Herbert could not bear a third.

The night was dreadful. In his delirium Herbert said things which broke the hearts of his companions! He wandered, he fought against the convicts, he called for Ayrton! He implored that mysterious being, that protector, now vanished, whose image haunted him... then he fell back into a deep prostration which utterly overwhelmed him... Several times Spilett thought that the poor boy was dead!

The next day, the 8th of December, was nothing but a succession of fainting fits. Herbert's wasted hands clutched at his sheets. Fresh doses of pounded bark had been given him, but the reporter no longer expected any result from them.

"If before to-morrow morning we have not given him a more powerful febrifuge," said the reporter, "Herbert will be dead!"

Night came,—the last night, doubtless, of this courageous, good, and intelligent boy, so far above his age, and whom all loved as their own son! The only remedy which existed against this terrible pernicious fever, the only specific which could overcome it, was not to be found in Lincoln Island!

During this night of the 8th to the 9th of December, Herbert was again seized with a more intense delirium. His liver was horribly congested, his brain attacked, and already it was impossible for him to recognize any one.

Would he live until the morrow, until that third fit which must infallibly carry him off? It was no longer probable. His strength was exhausted, and in the intervals between the crises he lay as if lifeless.

Towards three o'clock in the morning Herbert uttered a fearful cry. He seemed to writhe in a final convulsion. Neb, who was near him, terrified, rushed into the next room, where his companions were watching!

Top, at that moment, barked in a strange manner...

All came in at once, and succeeded in holding down the dying boy, who was trying to throw himself out of his bed, while Spilett, taking his arm, felt his pulse rising little by little...

It was five o'clock in the morning. The rays of the rising sun were beginning to steal into the rooms of Granite House. A fine day was promised, and this day was to be poor Herbert's last!...

A ray stole as far as the table which stood near the bed.

Suddenly Pencroff, uttering a cry, pointed to an object placed upon that table...

It was a little oblong box, the lid of which bore these words:—

SULPHATE OF QUININE.
