# The Telegraph to the Corral (draft 1)

CHAPTER XL.

A TALK—SMITH AND SPILETT—THE ENGINEER’S IDEA—THE ELECTRIC TELEGRAPH—THE WIRES—THE BATTERY—THE ALPHABET—FINE WEATHER—THE PROSPERITY OF THE COLONY—PHOTOGRAPHY—A SNOW EFFECT—TWO YEARS ON LINCOLN ISLAND.

“The poor man!” said Herbert, who, having rushed to the door, returned from it, after having watched Ayrton slide down the rope of the elevator and disappear in the darkness.

“He will come back,” said Smith.

“Well, Mr. Smith,” exclaimed Pencroff, “what does it mean? What! it was not Ayrton who threw this bottle into the sea? Then who did it?”

Certainly, if ever there was a question to be asked, it was that one!

“He did it,” replied Neb; “only the poor fellow was half out of his senses at the time.”

“Yes,” said Herbert, “and he had no knowledge of what he was doing.”

“It can be explained in no other way, my friends,” responded Smith, hurriedly, “and I understand, now, how Ayrton was able to give the exact situation of Tabor Island, since the very events prior to his abandonment on the island had made it known to him.”

“Nevertheless,” observed Pencroff, “if he was not yet a brute when he wrote that paper, and if it is seven or eight years since he threw it into the sea, how is it that the paper has not been injured by moisture?”

“It proves,” said Smith, “that Ayrton retained possession of his faculties to a period much more recent than he imagines.”

“That must be it,” replied Pencroff, “for otherwise the thing would be inexplicable.”

“Inexplicable, indeed,” answered the engineer, who seemed not to wish to prolong this talk.

“Has Ayrton told the truth?” questioned the sailor.

“Yes,” answered the reporter, “the history he has related is true in every particular. I remember, perfectly well, that the papers reported Lord Glenarvan’s undertaking and its result.”

“Ayrton has told the truth,” added Smith, “without any doubt, Pencroff, since the truth was cruel enough to himself. A man does not lie when he accuses himself in this way!”

The next day—the 21st of December—the colonists went down to the beach, and then clambered up to the plateau, but they saw nothing of Ayrton. Ayrton had gone during the night to his house at the corral, and they judged it best not to intrude upon him. Time would, doubtless, effect what encouragement had failed to accomplish.

Herbert, Pencroff, and Neb resumed their accustomed occupations; and, as it happened, that very day the same work brought Smith and Spilett together in the workshop at the Chimneys.

“Do you know, my dear Cyrus,” said Spilett, “that your explanation of yesterday about the bottle does not satisfy me at all? It is impossible to suppose that this unhappy creature could have written that paper, and thrown the bottle into the sea, without remembering anything about it!”

“Consequently, it is not he who threw it there, my dear Spilett.”

“Then you believe—”

“I believe nothing, I know nothing!” replied Smith, interrupting the reporter. “I content myself with placing this incident among those others which I have not been able to explain up to this time!”

“In truth, Cyrus,” said Spilett, “these things are incredible. Your rescue, the box thrown up on the beach, Top’s adventures, and now this bottle. Will we never have an answer to these enigmas?”

“Yes,” answered the engineer, earnestly, “yes, though I should have to search this island to its very bowels!”

“Chance will, perhaps, give us the key to this mystery.”

“Chance, Spilett! I scarcely believe in chance, any more than I believe in mysteries in this world. There is a cause for everything, however inexplicable, which has happened here, and I will discover it. But, while waiting, let us watch and work.”

January arrived, and the year 1867 began. The summer work was pushed forward assiduously. During the days which followed, Herbert and Spilett, having gone in the direction of the corral, ascertained that Ayrton had taken possession of the abode which had been prepared for him. He occupied himself with the large herd confided to his care, and thus was to save his companions the fatigue of visiting the corral every two or three days. Nevertheless, in order not to leave Ayrton too long alone, they went there pretty often.

It was just as well—owing to certain suspicions shared by Smith and Spilett—that this part of the island should be under a certain supervision, and Ayrton, if anything happened, would not fail to let the inhabitants of Granite House know of it.

Possibly, some sudden event might happen, which it would be important to communicate to the engineer without delay. And, aside from whatever might be connected with the mystery of the island, other things, requiring the prompt intervention of the colonists, might occur, as, for example, the discovering of a ship in the offing and in sight of the west coast, a wreck on that shore, the possible arrival of pirates, etc.

So Smith determined to place the corral in instant communication with Granite House.

It was the 10th of January when he told his project to his companions.

“How are you going to do such a thing as that, Mr. Smith?” asked Pencroff. “Do you, by chance, think of setting up a telegraph?”

“Precisely,” replied the engineer.

“Electric?” exclaimed Herbert.

“Electric,” responded Smith. “We have everything necessary for making a battery, and the most difficult part will be to draw the iron wire, but by means of a drawing-plate I think we can succeed.”

“Well, after this,” replied the sailor, “I no longer despair of seeing us some day riding along on a railway!”

They entered upon the work at once, beginning with the most difficult part, that is to say, the manufacture of the wires, since, if that failed, it would be useless to make the battery and other accessories.

The iron of Lincoln Island was, as we know, of excellent quality, and, therefore, very well adapted to being drawn out. Smith began by making a drawing-plate, that is to say, a plate of steel, pierced with conical holes of different sizes, which would successively bring the wire to the desired fineness. This piece of steel, after having been tempered “through and through,” as they say in metallurgy, was fixed immovably to a solid frame-work sunk in the ground, only a few feet distant from the great waterfall, whose motive power the engineer was once more about to use.

And, indeed, there was the fulling-mill, not then in use, the main shaft of which turned with extreme power, and would serve to draw out the wire and roll it around itself.

The operation was delicate and required great care. The iron, previously made into long and thin bars, with ends tapered by the file, having been introduced into the largest hole of the drawing-plate, was drawn out by the main shaft of the mill, wound round it for a length of 25 or 30 feet, then unrolled, and pulled, in turn, through the smaller holes; and at length, the engineer obtained wires 40 or 50 feet long, which it was easy to join together and place along the five miles between the corral and Granite House.

It took but a few days to carry this work through, and, indeed, as soon as the machine had been got under way, Smith, leaving his companions to work as wire-drawers, busied himself in the construction of his battery.

It was necessary, in this case, to make a battery with a constant current. We know that the elements of modern batteries are usually retort carbon, zinc, and copper. Copper the engineer was entirely without, since, in spite of all his search, he had been unable to find a trace of it on the island, and he must do without it. The retort carbon, that is to say, that hard graphite which is found in the retorts of gas-works after the coal has been dehydrogenized, might have been produced, but it would have been necessary to set up a special apparatus—a heavy piece of work. As to the zinc, it will be remembered that the box found on Jetsam Point was lined with a sheet of that metal, which could not be better utilized than at present.

Smith, after deep reflection, resolved to make a very simple battery, something like that which Becquerel invented in 1820, in which zinc alone is used. The other substances, nitric-acid and potash, he had at hand.

The manner in which he made this battery, in which the current was produced by the action of the acid and the potash on each other, was as follows:—

A certain number of glass vessels were made and filled with nitric-acid. They were corked with perforated corks, containing glass tubes closed at their lower ends with clay plugs, held in place by a piece of linen, and reaching into the acid. Into these tubes, by their upper ends, the engineer poured a solution of potash—previously obtained by burning various plants—and thus the acid and the potash reacted on each other through the clay.

Then Smith plunged two plates of zinc, the one in the nitric acid, the other in the solution of potash. At once a current was produced, which passed from the plate in the jar to that in the tube, and as these plates had been connected by a bit of wire, the one in the tube became the positive and the other the negative pole of the apparatus. Each jar thus produced as many currents, which, together, would be sufficient to cause all the phenomena of the electric telegraph.

Such was the ingenious and very simple apparatus constructed by Smith, an apparatus which was to enable him to establish telegraphic communication between Granite House and the corral.

On the 6th of February they began to erect the poles, furnished with glass insulators, and intended to support the wire, which was to follow the road to the corral. Some days later the wire was stretched, ready to produce, with the speed of 100,000 kilometres a second, the electric current which the earth would undertake to bring back to its point of departure.

Two batteries had been made, one for Granite House, and the other for the corral, as, if the corral had to communicate with Granite House, it might, also, be needful for Granite House to communicate with the corral.

As to the receiver and manipulator, they were very simple. At both stations the wire was wrapped around an electro-magnet, that is to say, around a piece of soft iron surrounded with wire. When communication was established between the two poles, the current, leaving the positive pole, traversed the wire, passed into the electro-magnet, which was temporarily magnetized, and returned under ground to the negative pole. When the current was interrupted, the electro-magnet was at once demagnetized. It was, therefore, sufficient to place a plate of soft iron before the electro-magnet which, attracted while the current was passing, fell back when it was interrupted. The movement of the plate thus obtained, Smith very easily fastened to it a needle, pointing to a dial, which bore the letters of the alphabet upon its face; and, in this way, he could correspond from one station to the other.

Everything was finished by the 12th of February. On that day Smith, having turned on the current, asked if everything was all right at the corral, and received, in a few moments, a satisfactory reply from Ayrton.

Pencroff was beside himself with delight, and every morning and evening he sent a telegram to the corral, which never remained unanswered.

This method of communication presented two very real advantages, both in informing the colonists of Ayrton’s presence at the corral, and in preventing his complete isolation. Moreover, Smith never allowed a week to pass without visiting him, and Ayrton came occasionally to Granite House, where he always found a kind reception.

Continuing their accustomed work, the fine weather passed away, and the resources of the colony, particularly in vegetables and cereals, increased from day to day, and the plants brought from Tabor Island had been perfectly acclimated. Prospect Plateau presented a most reassuring appearance. The fourth crop of corn had been excellent, and, as may well be supposed, no one undertook to count the 400,000,000,000 grains produced in the harvest; although Pencroff had had the idea of doing so; but Smith having informed him that, even supposing he could count 300 grains a minute, or 9,000 an hour, it would take him about 5,500 years to accomplish his undertaking, the worthy sailor thought it best to give it up.

The weather was superb, and the temperature very hot during the day; but, in the evening, the sea-breeze sprung up, tempering the air and giving refreshing nights to the inhabitants of Granite House. Still there were some storms, which, if not long continued, at least fell upon Lincoln Island with extraordinary violence. For several hours at a time the lightning never ceased illuminating the heavens, and the thunder roared without cessation.

About this time the little colony was extremely prosperous. The denizens of the poultry-yard increased rapidly, and the colonists lived on this increase, as it was becoming urgent to bring the population down to a more moderate number. The pigs had already littered, and it may be understood that the care of these animals absorbed a great part of Pencroff and Neb’s time. There were two pretty young onaggas, and their parents were most often ridden by Spilett and Herbert, who had become an excellent horseman under the reporter’s direction, or hitched to the cart to drag wood and coal to Granite House, or bring the minerals which the engineer made use of.

Many explorations were made about this time into the depths of the forests of the Far West. The explorers did not suffer from the heat, as the sun’s rays could scarcely penetrate the thick leafy roof above them. Thus, they visited all the left bank of the Mercy, bordering on the route from the corral to the mouth of Fall River.

But during these excursions the colonists took care to be well armed, as they often encountered certain exceedingly savage and ferocious wild boars, against which they had to fight in earnest. During this season they also waged a terrible war against the jaguars, for which animals Spilett had a special hatred, and his pupil, Herbert, seconded him well. Armed as they were, the hunters scarcely feared an encounter with one of these beasts, and the courage of Herbert was superb, while the coolness of the reporter was astonishing. So some twenty magnificent skins already ornamented the great hall at Granite House, and if this continued, the race of jaguars would soon be extinct in the island, the end at which the hunters aimed.

Sometimes the engineer took part in explorations of the unknown portions of the island, which he observed with minute attention. There were other traces than those of animals which he sought for in the thickest places in the forests, but not once did anything suspicious appear. Top and Jup, who accompanied him, showed by their actions that there was nothing extraordinary there, and yet the dog had barked more than once again at the mouth of that pit which the engineer had explored without result.

During this season Spilett, assisted by Herbert, took several views of the most picturesque portions of the island, by means of the photographic apparatus which had been found in the box, and which had not been used until now.

This apparatus, furnished with a powerful lens, was very complete. All the substances necessary in photographic work were there; collodion for preparing the glass plate, nitrate of silver for sensitizing it, hyposulphate of soda for fixing the image obtained, chloride of ammonium for soaking the paper which was to give the positive proof, acetate of soda and chloride of gold for impregnating the latter; nothing was wanting. Even the paper was there, already prepared with chloride, so that all that was necessary, before laying it in the frame upon the negative proofs, was to steep it for a few minutes in nitrate of silver diluted with water.

The reporter and his assistant soon became expert operators, and they obtained some tolerably fine views of the landscape, such as a comprehensive view of the island taken from Prospect Plateau, with Mount Franklin on the horizon, the mouth of the Mercy so picturesquely framed between its high rocks, the glade and the corral, with the lower spurs of the mountain in the background, the curious outline of Claw Cape, Jetsam Point, etc. Neither did the photographers forget to take portraits of all the inhabitants of the island, without exception.

“That peoples the place,” as Pencroff expressed it.

And the sailor was charmed to see his likeness, faithfully reproduced, ornamenting the walls of Granite House, and he stood before this display as pleased as if he had been gazing in one of the richest show-windows on Broadway.

It must be confessed, however, that the portrait showing the finest execution was, beyond dispute, that of master Jup. Master Jup had posed with a gravity impossible to describe, and his picture was a speaking likeness!

“One would say he was going to make a grimace!” exclaimed Pencroff.

And if Jup had not been satisfied, he must have been hard to please. But he was satisfied, and he contemplated his image with such a sentimental air, that it was evident he was a little conceited.

The heat of the summer ended with March. The weather was sometimes rainy, but the air was still warm. This month of March, which corresponds to the month of September in northern latitudes, was not as pleasant as might have been hoped. Perhaps it foreboded an early and a rigorous winter.

One morning, the 21st, it might even have been believed that the first snows had made their appearance. Indeed, Herbert, having stationed himself early at one of the windows of Granite House, exclaimed:—

“Hullo, the islet is covered with snow!”

“Snow at this season?” replied the reporter, joining the lad.

Their companions were soon beside them, and every one saw that not only the islet, but that the entire beach below Granite House, was covered with the white mantle, uniformly spread over the ground.

“It is, indeed, snow,” said Pencroff.

“Or something very much like it,” replied Neb.

“But the thermometer stands at 58° (14° centigrade above zero)!” said Spilett.

Smith looked at the white covering without speaking, for he was, indeed, at a loss how to explain such a phenomenon in this season and in this temperature.

“A thousand devils!” cried the sailor; “our plantations will be frost-bitten!”

And he was about descending when the agile Jup sprang before him and slid down to the ground.

The orang had scarcely touched the earth before the immense body of snow rose and scattered itself through the air in such innumerable flocks as to veil the light of the sun for some minutes.

“They are birds!” cried Herbert.

The effect had, indeed, been produced by myriads of sea-birds, with plumage of brilliant whiteness. They had settled by hundreds of thousands on the islet and the coast, and they now disappeared in the distance, leaving the colonists as amazed as if they had witnessed a transformation scene, from winter to summer, in some fancy spectacle. Unfortunately, the change had been so sudden that neither the reporter nor the lad had succeeded in knocking over one of these birds, whose species they did not recognize.

A few days later, and it was the 26th of March. Two years had passed since the castaways of the air had been thrown upon Lincoln Island!
