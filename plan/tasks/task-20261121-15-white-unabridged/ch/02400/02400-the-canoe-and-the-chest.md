# The Canoe and the Chest (draft 1)

CHAPTER XXIV.

TRIAL OF THE CANOE—A WRECK ON THE SHORE—THE TOW—JETSAM POINT—INVENTORY OF THE BOX: TOOLS, ARMS, INSTRUMENTS, CLOTHING, BOOKS, UTENSILS—WHAT PENCROFF WANTED—THE GOSPEL—A VERSE FROM THE SACRED BOOK.

On the 29th of October the canoe was entirely finished. Pencroff had kept his word, and had built, in five days, a sort of bark shell, stiffened with flexible crejimba rods. A seat at either end, another midway to keep it open, a gunwale for the thole-pins of a pair of oars, and a paddle to steer with, completed this canoe, which was twelve feet in length, and did not weigh 200 pounds. As for launching it, that was extremely simple. The light canoe was carried down to the sand, at the edge of the shore, before Granite House, and the rising tide lifted it. Pencroff, leaping in at once, worked it with the paddle, and found that it was very well suited to the use they meant to make of it.

“Hurrah!” cried the sailor, quite ready to applaud his own success. “With this we can make the tour of—”

“Of the world?” suggested Spilett.

“No, but of the island. Some stones for ballast, a mast in the bow, with a sail which Mr. Smith will make some day, and we’ll go a long way! Well, Mr. Smith, and you, Mr. Spilett, and you, Herbert, and you, Neb, will you not come and try our new ship? The devil! we must see if it will carry all five of us!”

It was, indeed, an experiment to be made. Pencroff, by a stroke of the paddle, brought the canoe close to the shore by a narrow passage between the rocks, and it was agreed that they should make a trial trip of the craft that very day, by following the bank as far as the first point, where the rocks of the south ended.

As they were stepping in, Neb cried:—

“But your boat leaks a good deal, Pencroff!”

“Oh, that’s nothing, Neb,” answered the sailor. “The wood has to drink! But in two days it will not show, and there will be as little water in our canoe as in the stomach of a drunkard! Come, get in!”

They all embarked, and Pencroff pushed off. The weather was splendid, the sea was as calm as if its waters had been held within the narrow shores of a lake, and the canoe could venture upon it with as much security as upon the tranquil current of the Mercy.

Neb and Herbert took the oars, and Pencroff sat in the stern with the paddle as steersman.

The sailor first crossed the channel, and skirted the southern point of the islet. A gentle breeze was wafted from the south. There was no swell, either in the channel or out at sea. A few long undulations, which the canoe scarcely felt, for it was heavily laden, regularly swelled the surface of the sea. They rowed out about half a mile from the coast so as to get a view of the whole outline of Mount Franklin. Then, putting about, Pencroff returned towards the mouth of the river, and followed along the shore, which, rounding to the extreme point, hid the whole of the marshy plain of Tadorn’s Fen. The point, whose distance was made greater by the bend of the coast, was about three miles from the Mercy, and the colonists resolved to go to its end, and to pass it only so far as would be needed to obtain a hasty glance at the coast as far as Claw Cape.

The canoe followed along the shore, keeping off two cables’ length at most, so as to avoid the reefs with which these waters were strewn, and which the rising tide was beginning to cover. The cliff, beginning at the mouth of the river, lowered as it approached the promontory. It was an extremely savage-looking, unevenly-arranged heap of granite blocks, very different from the curtain which formed Prospect Plateau. One would have said that an enormous cart-load of rocks had been emptied out there. There was not a trace of vegetation on this very sharp point, which projected two miles beyond the forest, rather like a giant’s arm, thrust out from a green sleeve.

The canoe, driven by the two oars, sped easily along. Spilett, pencil in one hand and note-book in the other, sketched the outline of the coast in bold strokes, and Neb, Pencroff, and Herbert discussed this part of their domain, new to their eyes. As they moved southward the two Mandible Capes seemed to shift their places and to close in Union Bay more narrowly. As to Smith, he regarded everything in silence, and from his distrustful expression it seemed as if he was observing some strange country.

However, after three-quarters of an hour, the canoe had nearly reached the end of the point and was about doubling it, when Herbert rose, and pointing out a black object, said:—

“What is that down there on the sand?”

Every one looked in the direction indicated.

“There is something there, indeed,” said the reporter. “It looks like a wreck half buried in the sand.”

“Oh, I see what it is!” cried Pencroff.

“What?” asked Neb.

“Barrels! they are barrels, and, may be, they are full!”

“To shore, Pencroff!” said Smith.

And with a few strokes the canoe touched land at the head of a little cove, and the party leaped out upon the beach.

Pencroff was not mistaken. There were two barrels half buried in the sand; but still firmly fastened to them was a large box, which, borne up by them, had been floated on to the shore.

“Has there been a shipwreck, then, near the island?” asked Herbert.

“Evidently,” answered Spilett.

“But what is in this box?” exclaimed Pencroff, with a natural impatience. “What is in this box? It is closed, and we have nothing with which to break the lid. However, with a stone—”

And the sailor picked up a heavy rock, and was about to break one of the sides, when the engineer, stopping him, said:—

“Cannot you moderate your impatience for one hour only, Pencroff?”

“But, think, Mr. Smith! May be there is everything we want in it!”

“We will find out, Pencroff,” answered the engineer, “but believe me, do not break the box, as it may be useful to us. Let us transport it to Granite House, where we can readily open it without injuring it. It is all prepared for the voyage, and since it has floated here, it can float again to the river mouth.”

“You are right, sir, and I am wrong,” answered the sailor, “but one is not always his own master!”

The engineer’s advice was good. In fact, the canoe could not have carried the things probably enclosed in the box, which must have been heavy, since it had to be buoyed up by two empty barrels. It was, therefore, better to tow it in this condition to the shore at the Granite House.

And now the important question was, from whence came this jetsam? Smith and his companions looked attentively around them, and searched the beach for several hundred paces, but there was nothing else to be seen. They scanned the sea, Herbert and Neb climbing up a high rock, but the horizon was deserted. Nothing was in sight, neither a disabled ship nor a vessel under sail.

Nevertheless, there had been a shipwreck, there was no doubt of that. Perhaps, even, this incident was connected with the incident of the pellet. Perhaps strangers had landed upon another part of the island. Perhaps they were still there. But the natural conclusion of the colonists was that these strangers could not be Malay pirates, since the jetsam was evidently of European or American provenance.

They all went back to the box, which measured five feet by three. It was made of oak, very carefully closed, and covered with thick leather, held on by copper nails. The two large barrels, hermetically sealed, but which sounded empty, were fastened to its sides by means of strong ropes, tied in what Pencroff recognized to be “sailor’s knots.” It appeared to be in a perfect state of preservation, which was accounted for by the fact of its having been thrown upon the sand instead of the rocks. It could even be affirmed, on close examination, that it had not been long in the sea, and that it had but lately come upon the beach. It seemed probable, also, that the water had not penetrated, and that its contents would be found uninjured. It was evident that this box had been thrown overboard from a disabled ship making for the island. And, in the hope that it would reach the coast, where they would find it later, the passengers had taken the precaution to buoy it up.

“We will tow this box to Granite House,” said the engineer, “and take an inventory of its contents; then, if we discover any of the survivors of this supposed shipwreck, we will return them what is theirs. If we find no one—”

“We will keep the things ourselves!” cried the sailor. “But, for Heaven’s sake, what can there be in it!”

The tide was already beginning to reach the jetsam, which would doubtless float at high water. One of the ropes which was fastened to the barrels was partly untwisted and served to fasten the floating apparatus to the canoe. Then, Neb and Pencroff dug out the sand with their oars, so as to make it easier to move the box, and soon the canoe, with the jetsam in tow, began to round the promontory to which they gave the name of Jetsam Point. The box was so heavy that the barrels just sufficed to sustain it above the water; and Pencroff feared each moment that it would break loose and sink to the bottom. Fortunately his fears were groundless, and an hour and a half after starting—it had needed all that time to cover this distance of three miles—the canoe touched the bank before Granite House.

The boat and the jetsam were drawn upon the shore, and as the tide was beginning to fall, both soon rested on dry ground. Neb brought some tools so as to force the box with as little injury to it as possible, and the colonists forthwith proceeded to examine its contents.

Pencroff did not try to hide that he was extremely agitated. He began by unfastening the barrels, which, being in very good condition, could be made use of, it need hardly be said; then the locks were forced with a crowbar, and the cover at once fell back. A second envelope, of zinc, lined the inside of the case, which had evidently been so arranged that its contents should be, under all circumstances, impervious to moisture.

“Oh!” cried Neb, “what if it should be preserves that are inside!”

“I hope not,” answered the reporter.

“If it should turn out that there was—” muttered the sailor.

“What?” asked Neb, who heard him.

“Nothing!”

The zinc cover was split across its whole breadth, and turned back over the sides of the box, and, little by little, many different objects were lifted out on the sand. At each new discovery Pencroff cheered, Herbert clapped his hands, and Neb danced—like a negro. There were books which would have made the lad crazy with pleasure, and cooking implements which Neb would have covered with kisses!

In truth the colonists had reason to be extremely satisfied, for the box contained tools, arms, instruments, clothing, and books; and here is the exact inventory of them, as it was entered in Spilett’s note-book:—

TOOLS.—3 knives, with several blades, 2 wood-chopper’s hatchets, 2 carpenter’s hatchets, 3 planes, 2 adzes, 1 twibil, 6 cold chisels, 2 files, 3 hammers, 3 gimlets, 2 augers, 10 bags of nails and screws, 3 saws of different sizes, 2 boxes of needles.

ARMS.—2 flint-lock guns, 2 percussion guns, 2 central-fire carbines; 5 cutlasses, 4 boarding sabres, 2 barrels of powder, holding 25 pounds each, 12 boxes of caps.

INSTRUMENTS.—1 sextant, 1 opera-glass, 1 spyglass, 1 box of compasses, 1 pocket compass, 1 Fahrenheit thermometer, 1 aneroid barometer, 1 box containing a complete photographic apparatus, lens, plates, chemicals, etc.

CLOTHING.—2 dozen shirts of a peculiar material resembling wool, though evidently a vegetable substance; 3 dozen stockings of the same material.

UTENSILS.—1 iron pot, 6 tinned copper stewpans, 3 iron plates, 10 aluminium knives and forks, 2 kettles, 1 small portable stove, 6 table knives.

BOOKS.—1 Bible, containing the Old and New Testaments, 1 atlas, 1 dictionary of Polynesian languages, 1 dictionary of the Natural Sciences, in six volumes, 3 reams of blank paper, 2 blank books.

“Unquestionably,” said the reporter, after the inventory had been taken, “the owner of this box was a practical man! Tools, arms, instruments, clothing, utensils, books, nothing is wanting. One would really say that he had expected to be shipwrecked, and had made ready for it before-hand!”

“Nothing, indeed, is wanting,” murmured Smith, thoughtfully.

“And it is a sure thing,” added Herbert, “that the ship which carried this box and its owner was not a Malay pirate!”

“Unless its owner had been taken prisoner by pirates—” said Pencroff.

“That is not admissible,” answered the reporter. “It is more probable that an American or European ship has been driven to this neighborhood, and that the passengers, wishing to save what was, at least, necessary, have prepared this box and have thrown it overboard.”

“And do you think so, Mr. Smith?” asked Herbert.

“Yes, my boy,” answered the engineer, “that might have been the case. It is possible that, at the moment of a shipwreck, or in anticipation of one, divers articles of the first necessity have been collected in this chest, so that they might be found again at some point of the coast—”

“Even the photographic apparatus!” observed the sailor, rather incredulously.

“As to that,” answered the engineer, “I do not quite see its use; what we, as well as any other shipwrecked person, would have valued more, would have been a greater assortment of clothing and more ammunition!”

“But have none of these things any mark or address by which we can tell where they came from?” asked Spilett.

They looked to see. Each article was examined attentively, especially the books, instruments, and arms; but, contrary to custom, neither instruments nor arms bore any maker’s name or mark; moreover, they were in perfect order, and seemed never to have been used. So also with the tools and utensils; everything was new, and this went to prove, on the whole, that the things had not been hastily thrown together in the box, but that their selection had been made thoughtfully, and their arrangement with care. This, also, was evident from the zinc case which had kept everything watertight, and which could not have been soldered in a moment.

The dictionaries of the Natural Sciences and of the Polynesian languages were both in English, but they bore no publisher’s name, nor any date of publication.

So also with the Bible, printed in the English language, a quarto remarkable for its typography, and which seemed to have been often turned over.

The Atlas was a splendid work, containing maps of every part of the world, and several planispheres laid out on Mercator’s Projection. The nomenclature in this book was in French, but in it, as in the others, neither the date of publication nor the name of the editor or publisher appeared.

There was, therefore, nothing on these articles to show where they came from, and consequently the colonists were unable even to conjecture the nationality of the ship that must have so recently passed near them. But no matter where it came from, this box enriched the party on Lincoln Island. Until now, in transforming the products of nature, they had created everything for themselves, and had succeeded by their own intelligence. Did it not now seem as if Providence had intended to reward them by placing these divers products of human industry in their hands? Therefore, with one accord, they all rendered thanks to Heaven.

Nevertheless, Pencroff was not entirely satisfied. It appeared that the box did not contain something to which he attached an immense importance, and as its contents lessened, his cheers had become less hearty, and when the inventory was closed, he murmured:—

“That’s all very fine, but you will see that there will be nothing for me in this box!”

“Why, friend Pencroff, what did you expect?” said Neb.

“A half pound of tobacco,” answered the sailor seriously, “and then I would have been perfectly happy!”

No one could help laughing at the sailor’s remark.

The discovery of this jetsam made the thorough exploration of the island now and more than ever necessary. It was, therefore, agreed that they should set out at daybreak the next morning, proceeding to the western coast via the Mercy. If anyone had been shipwrecked on that part of the island, it was to be feared that they were without resources, and help must be given them at once.

During the day the contents of the box were carried to Granite House and arranged in order in the great hall.

That day—the 29th of October—happened to be a Sunday, and, before retiring, Herbert asked Mr. Smith if he would read them some passage from the Gospel.

“Gladly,” answered the engineer, taking the sacred book in his hands; and he was about to open it, when Pencroff, checking him, said:—

“Mr. Smith, I am superstitious. Open the book at random and read the first verse which you meet with. We will see if it applies to our situation.”

Smith smiled at the words of the sailor, but yielding to his wishes he opened the Gospel exactly where a marker lay between the leaves. Suddenly his eye was arrested by a red cross made with a crayon, opposite the 8th verse of the seventh chapter of St. Matthew.

He read these words:—

“For every one that asketh, receiveth; and he that seeketh, findeth.”
