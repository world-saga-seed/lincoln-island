# The Descent Into the Old Outlet and Granite-House (draft 1)

CHAPTER XVIII.

PENCROFF DOUBTS NO MORE—THE OLD OUTLET OF THE LAKE—A SUBTERRANEAN DESCENT—THE WAY THROUGH THE GRANITE—TOP HAS DISAPPEARED—THE CENTRAL CAVERN—THE LOWER WELL—MYSTERY—THE BLOWS WITH THE PICK—THE RETURN.

Smith’s project had succeeded; but, as was his manner, he stood motionless, his lips closed, his gaze fixed, giving no sign of satisfaction. Herbert was all enthusiasm; Neb jumped with joy; Pencroff, shaking his big head, murmured:—

“Indeed, our engineer does wonders!”

The nitro-glycerine had worked powerfully. The opening made in the lake was so great that at least a three times greater volume of water escaped by it than by the former outlet. In a little while, therefore, the level of the lake would be lowered two feet or more.

The colonists returned to the Chimneys, and collecting some picks, iron-tipped spears, ropes of fibre, a steel and tinder, returned to the plateau. Top went with them.

On the way the sailor could not resist saying to the engineer:—

“But do you know, Mr. Smith, that one could blow up our whole island with this beautiful liquid which you have made?”

“Doubtless,” replied the other, “island, continents, the world itself. It is only a question of quantity.”

“Couldn’t you use this nitro-glycerine to load firearms?” asked the sailor.

“No, Pencroff, because it is too shattering. But it would be easy to make gun-cotton, or even common powder, as we have nitric acid, saltpetre, sulphur, and charcoal. Unfortunately, the guns themselves are wanting.”

“Oh! Mr. Smith,” replied the sailor, “with a little good will!—”

Decidedly, Pencroff had erased the word “impossible” from the dictionary of Lincoln Island.

The colonists having reached Prospect Plateau, hastened at once towards the point of the lake, near which opened the mouth of the old outlet, which ought now to be uncovered. The outlet would thus have become practicable, since the water no longer poured through it, and it would, doubtless, be easy to explore its interior arrangement.

In a few moments they reached the lower angle of the lake, and a glance sufficed to show them that the result had been obtained.

There, in the granite wall of the lake, and now above the water-level, appeared the long-looked for opening. A narrow ledge, left bare by the subsidence of the water, gave them access to it. The opening was about twenty feet wide, though only two feet high. It was like the gutter-mouth in a pavement. It was not open enough to give the party an easy passage, but Neb and Pencroff, with their picks, in less than an hour had given it a sufficient height.

The engineer then approached, and saw that the walls of the outlet in its upper part showed a slope of not more than from 30° to 35°. They were, therefore, practicable, and, unless they became steeper, it would be easy to descend them to the very level of the sea. And if, as was very probable, some vast cavern existed in the interior of the massive granite, it was possible that they could make use of it.

“Well, Mr. Smith, what are we waiting for?” asked the sailor, all impatience to venture into the narrow corridor. “Top, you see, has gone ahead!”

“Good,” replied the engineer, “but we must have some light. Go, Neb, and cut some resinous branches.”

Neb and Herbert ran to some pine and evergreens growing upon the bank, and soon returned with branches which were made into torches. Having lit them with the steel, the colonists, with Smith leading, entered the dark passage, but recently filled with water.

Contrary to their expectation, the passage grew larger as they advanced, so that almost at once they were able to walk upright. The granite walls, worn by the water during countless ages, were slippery, and the party had to look out for falls. They, therefore, fastened themselves together with a cord, like mountain climbers. Fortunately, some projections of the granite, forming regular steps, made the descent less perilous. Drops of water, still clinging to the rocks, shone with the colors of the rainbow here and there in the light of the torches, and one might have thought that the walls were hung with innumerable stalactites. The engineer looked carefully at this black granite. He could not see a stratum or a flaw. The mass was compact and of an extremely fine grain, and the passage must have been coeval with the island. It had not been worn little by little by the constant action of water. Pluto, and not Neptune, had bored it with his own hand; and upon its surface could be distinguished the traces of igneous action, which the washing of the waters had not been able entirely to efface.

The colonists descended but slowly. They experienced some emotion in thus adventuring into the depths of this massive rock, of which they were evidently the first human visitants. No one spoke, but each was busied with his own reflections and the thought occurred to more than one, that perhaps some poulp or other gigantic cephalopod might inhabit the interior cavities which communicated with the sea. It was, therefore, necessary to advance cautiously.

Top was ahead of the little troop and they could rely on the dog’s sagacity to give the alarm on occasion. After having descended about 100 feet, by a rather winding route, Smith, who was walking in front, halted, and the others came up with him. They were standing in a cavern of moderate size. Drops of water fell from the roof, but they did not ooze through the rocks, they were simply the last traces of the torrent which had so long roared through this place, and the air, though slightly humid, emitted no mephitic vapor.

“Well, my dear Cyrus,” said Spilett, “here is a retreat truly unknown and hidden in these depths, but, after all, it is uninhabitable.”

“How, uninhabitable?” asked the sailor.

“Why, it is too small and too dark.”

“Cannot we make it bigger, hollow it out, and make openings for the light and air?” answered Pencroff, who now thought nothing impracticable.

“Let us push on,” said Smith, “let us go on with our exploration. Perhaps lower down, nature will have spared us this work.”

“We are only a third of the way down,” observed Herbert.

“About a third,” responded Cyrus, “for we have descended about 100 feet from the opening, and it is not impossible that 100 feet lower—”

“Where is Top?” asked Neb, interrupting his master.

They looked about the cavern. The dog was not there.

“He has probably gone on,” said Pencroff.

“Let us overtake him,” replied Smith.

The descent was resumed. The engineer noted carefully all the deviations of the route, and, in spite of so many windings, kept without much difficulty a general idea of their direction, which was towards the sea. The party had descended about fifty feet further, measured perpendicularly, when their attention was arrested by distant sounds coming from the depths of the rock. They stopped and listened. These sounds, borne along the passage, as the voice through an acoustic tube, were distinctly heard.

“It’s Top’s barking!” cried Herbert.

“Yes, and our brave dog is barking furiously,” added Pencroff.

“We have our iron-tipped spears,” said Smith. “Let us be on our guard, and forward!”

“It is becoming more and more interesting,” whispered Spilett to the sailor, who nodded assent.

They hurried to the rescue of the dog. His barks grew more distinct. They could hear a strange rage in his broken voice. Was he then struggling with some animal whose retreat he had disturbed? It may be said that, without thinking of the danger to which they exposed themselves, the colonists now felt themselves seized with an irresistible curiosity. They no longer walked down the passage, but let themselves slide, so to speak, down its wall, and in a few minutes, sixty feet lower, they came up with the dog.

There, the corridor opened out into a vast and magnificent cavern. Top, rushing about, was barking furiously. Pencroff and Neb, shaking their torches, lit up all the inequalities of the granite, and at the same time Smith, Spilett, and Herbert, with their spears raised, held themselves prepared for any emergency.

But the enormous cavern was empty. The colonists searched everywhere; there was nothing, not an animal, not a living being! Nevertheless, Top continued barking, and neither threats nor caresses could stop him.

“There must be some place where the water escaped to the sea,” said the engineer.

“Yes, and look out for a hole,” answered Pencroff.

“On, Top, on,” cried Smith, and the dog, encouraged by his master, ran towards the end of the cavern, and redoubled his barking.

Following him, they saw by the light of the torches the opening of a regular well in the granite. Here, undoubtedly, was the place where the water had found its way out of the granite, but this time, instead of being a corridor sloping and accessible, it was a perpendicular well, impossible to descend.

The torches were waved above the opening. They saw nothing. Smith broke off a burning branch and dropped it into the abyss. The blazing resin, whose light was increased still more by the rapidity of its fall, illuminated the interior of the pit, but still nothing appeared. Then the flame was extinguished with a slight hiss, which indicated that it had reached the water, that is to say, the sea level.

The engineer calculated, from the time taken in the fall, that the depth was about ninety feet. The floor of the cavern was therefore that distance above the sea.

“Here is our house,” said Smith.

“But it was occupied by some creature,” said Spilett, whose curiosity was unsatisfied.

“Well, the thing that had it, whether amphibious or not, has fled by this outlet and vacated in our favor,” replied the engineer.

“Any how, I should like to have been Top a quarter of an hour ago,” said the sailor, “for, after all, it was not for nothing that he barked.”

Smith looked at his dog, and any one of his companions who had come near him would have heard him murmur these words:—

“Yes, I quite believe that Top knows more than we do about many things!”

However, the wishes of the colonists had been in a great measure realized. Chance, aided by the marvellous acuteness of their chief, had done them good service. Here they had at their disposal a vast cavern, whose extent could not yet be estimated in the insufficient light of the torches, but which could certainly be easily partitioned off with bricks into chambers, and arranged, if not as a house, at least as a spacious suite of rooms. The water having left it, could not return. The place was free.

But two difficulties remained, first, the possibility of lighting this excavation hollowed out of a solid block; secondly, the necessity of rendering it easier of access. The first could not be done from above as the enormous mass of granite was over them; but, perhaps, they would be able to pierce the outer wall which faced the sea. Smith, who during the descent had judged the slope roughly enough, and therefore of the length of the passage, believed that this part of the wall could not be very thick. If light could be thus obtained, so could entrance, as it was as easy to pierce a door as windows, and to fix a ladder on the outside.

Smith communicated his ideas to his companions.

“Then, Mr. Smith, to work!” answered Pencroff; “I have my pick, and I will soon make daylight through this wall! Where shall I begin?”

“Here,” answered the engineer, showing the strong sailor a rather considerable hollow in the wall, which must lessen its thickness.

Pencroff attacked the granite, and for half an hour, by the light of the torches, made the splinters fly about him. The rock sparkled under his pick. Then Neb took his place, and Spilett after Neb. The work had already lasted two hours, and they began to fear that at this place the wall was thicker than the length of the pick, when, at a last stroke of Spilett the implement, passing through, fell on the outside.

“Hurrah forever!” cried Pencroff.

The wall was but three feet thick.

Smith looked through the opening, which was eighty feet above the ground. Before him extended the coast, the islet, and, beyond, the boundless sea.

But through this hole, which was fairly large, for the rock had crumbled away considerably, the light entered in floods, inundating the splendid cavern and producing a magical effect. While on the left hand it measured not more than thirty feet in height and breadth, and one hundred in length, to the right it was enormous, and its vault rose to a height of more than eighty feet. In some places, granite pillars, irregularly disposed, supported the arches as in the nave of a cathedral. Resting upon a sort of lateral piers, here, sinking into elliptic arches, there, rising in ogive mouldings, losing itself in the dark bays, half seen in the shadow through the fantastic arches, ornamented by a profusion of projections which seemed like pendants, this vaulted roof afforded a picturesque blending of all the architectures—Byzantine, Romanesque, Gothic—that the hand of man has produced. And yet here it was only the work of nature! She alone had constructed this magic Alhambra in a granite rock!

The colonists were overcome with admiration. Expecting to find but a narrow cavern, they found themselves in a sort of marvellous palace, and Neb had taken off his hat as if he had been transported into a temple!

Exclamations of admiration escaped from every lip, and the hurrahs echoed and reechoed to the depths of the dark naves.

“Ah! my friends,” cried Smith, “when we shall have well lighted the interior of this place, when we shall have arranged our chambers, our store-rooms, our offices in the left-hand portion, we will still have this splendid cavern, which shall be our study and our museum!”

“And we will call it—” asked Herbert.

“Granite House,(1)” answered Smith; and his companions again saluted the name with their hurrahs.

(1) Granite palace. The word “house” applies equally to palaces and to dwelling-houses, as Buckingham House, or the Mansion House, in London.

By this time the torches were nearly consumed, and as, in order to return, it was necessary to regain the summit of the plateau and to remount the corridor, it was decided to postpone until the morrow the work of arranging their new home.

Before leaving, Smith leaned once more over the dark pit, which sank perpendicularly to the level of the sea, and listened attentively. But no sound arose, not even that of the water, which the undulations of the surge must sometimes have agitated in these depths. A resinous torch was again thrown in, lighting up anew for an instant the walls of the well, but, no more this time than the first, was anything suspicious revealed. If any marine monster had been unexpectedly surprised by the retreat of the waters, he had already regained the open sea by the subterranean passage which extended under the beach, and which the overflow of the lake had followed before a new outlet had been offered to it.

Nevertheless the engineer stood motionless, listening attentively, his gaze plunged in the abyss, without speaking.

Then the sailor approached him, and, touching his arm:—

“Mr. Smith,” he said.

“What is it, my friend?” responded the engineer, like one returning from the land of dreams.

“The torches are nearly out.”

“Forward!” said Smith; and the little troop left the cavern and began the ascent through the dark weir. Top walked behind, still growling in an odd way. The ascension was sufficiently laborious, and the colonists stopped for a few minutes at the upper grotto, which formed a sort of landing half way up the long granite stairway. Then they began again to mount, and pretty soon they felt a fresher air. The drops, already evaporated, no longer shone on the walls. The smoky light of the torches grew pale; Neb’s went out, and they had to hasten in order to avoid having to grope their way through the profound darkness. This was done, and a little before 4 o’clock, just as the torch of the sailor was burnt out, Smith and his companions emerged from the mouth of the passage.
