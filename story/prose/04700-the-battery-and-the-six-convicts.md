# The Battery and the Six Convicts (final)

CHAPTER XLVII.

THE ENGINEER’S AFFIRMATIONS—PENCROFF’S GRAND HYPOTHESES—AN AERIAL BATTERY—THE FOUR PROJECTILES—CONCERNING THE SURVIVING CONVICTS—AYRTON HESITATES—GENEROUS SENTIMENTS OF SMITH—PENCROFF YIELDS WITH REGRET.

Thus, then, everything was explained by the submarine explosion of this torpedo. Smith, who during the war of the Union had had occasion to try these terrible engines of destruction, could not be mistaken. It was under the action of this cylinder, charged with some explosive substance, nitro-glycerine, picrate, or other material of the same nature, that the water of the channel had risen like a waterspout, and that the brig, struck in her bottom, had sunk instantly; and it was for this reason that it had been impossible to raise her, so considerable was the damage which her hull had suffered. Against a torpedo which would have destroyed an ironclad frigate as easily as a simple fishing-boat, the Speedy had not been able to hold out!

Yes! everything was explained, everything—except the presence of this torpedo in the waters of the channel!

“My friends,” then resumed Smith, “we can no longer doubt the presence of a mysterious being, a castaway like ourselves, perhaps, abandoned on our island; and I say it in order that Ayrton may be informed of all that has happened of a strange nature during these two years. Who is this beneficent unknown, whose intervention, so fortunate for us, has shown itself on so many occasions? I cannot imagine. What interest has he in acting thus, in hiding himself after rendering so many services? I cannot understand it. But his services are none the less real, and they are of such a kind that only a man disposing of a prodigious power could render them to us. Ayrton is indebted to him as we are; for if it was the unknown who saved me from the waves after the fall of the balloon, it is evidently he who wrote the document, who placed the bottle in the course of the channel, and who made known to us the situation of our companion. I will add that it was he who guided that case, so well provided with everything we wanted, and stranded it on Jetsam Point; that it was he who lighted the fire on the heights of the island, which allowed you to land there; that it was he who fired the pellet found in the body of the peccary; that it was he who sank in the channel the torpedo which destroyed the brig; in a word, that all those inexplicable facts, of which we could give no account, are due to this mysterious being. Therefore, whoever he may be, whether castaway or exile on this island, we should be ungrateful if we thought ourselves freed from all gratitude towards him. We have contracted a debt, and I hope that we shall one day pay it.”

“You are right to speak thus, my dear Cyrus,” replied Gideon Spilett. “Yes, there is a being, almost all-powerful, hidden in some part of the island, and his influence has been singularly useful to our colony. I will add that this unknown appears to me to dispose of means of action which would border on the supernatural, if in the facts of practical life the supernatural were admissible. Is it he who puts himself in secret communication with us by the well of Granite House, and has he thus knowledge of all our plans? Was it he who held out that bottle to us, when the canoe made its first excursion to sea? Was it he who threw Top out of the waters of the lake, and who killed the dugong? Is it he, as everything leads us to believe, who saved you from the waves, Cyrus, and that in circumstances in which any other who was only a man could not have acted? If it is he, then he possesses a power which makes him master of the elements.”

The reporter’s observation was just, and every one felt it.

“Yes,” replied Smith; “if the intervention of a human being is no longer doubtful to us, I agree that he has at his disposal means of action beyond those which humanity possesses. There is still a mystery there; but if we discover the man, the mystery will be discovered also. The question, then, is this: ought we to respect the incognito of this generous being, or ought we to do everything to find our way to him? What is your opinion on this matter?”

“My opinion,” replied Pencroff, “is that, whoever he may be, he is a brave man, and he has my esteem!”

“So be it,” resumed Smith; “but that is not an answer, Pencroff.”

“Master,” then said Neb, “my idea is that we may search as long as we like for this gentleman whom we are talking about, but that we shall not discover him till he pleases.”

“That is not a foolish thing that you say, Neb,” answered Pencroff.

“I am of Neb’s opinion,” replied Gideon Spilett; “but that is no reason for not attempting the adventure. Whether we find this mysterious being or whether we do not, we shall at least have done our duty towards him.”

“And you, my boy, give us your opinion,” said the engineer, turning towards Herbert.

“Ah!” cried Herbert, whose eyes brightened, “I should like to thank him, he who saved you first, and who has saved us afterwards!”

“You are not hard to please, my boy,” rejoined Pencroff, “and so should I, and all of us! I am not curious, but I would willingly give one of my eyes to see this fellow face to face! It seems to me that he must be handsome, tall, strong, with a fine beard, and hair like rays, and that he must be lying on clouds, with a great ball in his hand!”

“Why, Pencroff,” answered Gideon Spilett, “that is the portrait of God the Father that you are giving us there!”

“Possibly, Mr. Spilett,” replied the sailor, “but that is how I imagine him!”

“And you, Ayrton?” asked the engineer.

“Mr. Smith,” replied Ayrton, “I can hardly give you my opinion in this matter. Whatever you do will be well done. When you wish me to join you in your search, I shall be ready to follow you.”

“I thank you, Ayrton,” resumed Smith, “but I should like a more direct answer to the question which I have put to you. You are our companion; you have already devoted yourself for us several times, and, like every one here, you ought to be consulted when any important decision is to be taken. Speak, then.”

“Mr. Smith,” replied Ayrton, “I think that we ought to do everything to find this unknown benefactor. Perhaps he is alone? Perhaps he is suffering? Perhaps it is a life which has to be renewed? I too, as you have said, have a debt of gratitude to pay him. It was he, it could only have been he, who came to Tabor Island, who found there the wretch whom you knew, who let you know that there was an unhappy man there to be saved! It is thanks to him, then, that I have become a man again. No, I shall never forget it!”

“It is decided, then,” said Smith. “We will begin our search as soon as possible. We will not leave a part of the island unexplored. We will search it to its most secret retreats, and may this unknown friend pardon us in consideration of our intention!”

For some days the colonists were actively employed in haymaking and in the harvest. Before putting into execution their project of exploring the still unknown parts of the island, they wished all indispensable work to be finished. It was also the time at which the various vegetables from the Tabor Island plants were gathered. Everything, then, had to be stored; and, happily, there was no want of room in Granite House, where all the riches of the island might have been laid up. The products of the colony were there, methodically arranged, and in a safe place, it may be believed, as much sheltered from beasts as from men. No damp was to be feared in the midst of that thick mass of granite. Several of the natural excavations situated in the upper passage were enlarged or hollowed out, either with the pick or by blasting; and Granite House became also a general warehouse, containing the provisions, the ammunition, the tools and spare utensils, in a word, all the stock of the colony.

As to the guns from the brig, they were pretty pieces of cast steel, which, at Pencroff’s urging, were hoisted by means of tackle and cranes up to the very landing of Granite House. Embrasures were made between the windows, and the guns could soon be seen stretching their shining muzzles through the granite wall. From this height these fire-mouths truly commanded the whole of Union Bay. It was like a little Gibraltar, and any vessel which had anchored off the islet would have been inevitably exposed to the fire of this aerial battery.

“Mr. Smith,” said Pencroff one day—it was the 8th of November—“now that this armament is finished, we really must try the range of our pieces.”

“Do you think that would be of use?” replied the engineer.

“It is more than useful, it is necessary! Without it, how are we to know the distance to which we can send one of those pretty balls with which we are provided?”

“Let us try, then, Pencroff,” replied the engineer. “However, I think that we ought to make the experiment, using, not the ordinary powder, the stock of which I wish to leave untouched, but pyroxyline, which will never fail us.”

“Will those guns be able to bear the deflagration of the pyroxyline?” asked the reporter, who was not less desirous than Pencroff to try the artillery of Granite House.

“I believe so. Besides,” added the engineer, “we will act prudently.”

The engineer had reason to think that these guns were of excellent make, and he knew about such things. Made of forged steel, and loading at the breech, they ought, for that very reason, to be able to bear a considerable charge, and consequently to have an enormous range. In fact, as regards the useful effect, the trajectory described by the ball ought to be as flat as possible, and this flatness can only be obtained on condition that the projectile has a very great initial velocity.

“Now,” said Smith to his companions, “the initial velocity is in proportion to the quantity of powder used. The whole question, in the making of the pieces, comes down to the employment of a metal as resisting as possible, and steel is incontestably that one of all the metals which resists the best. I have, therefore, reason to think that our guns will bear without risk the expansion of the gases of the pyroxyline, and will give excellent results.”

“We shall be a great deal more certain of it when we have tried!” answered Pencroff.

It is needless to say that the four guns were in perfect order. Since they had been taken out of the water, the sailor had made it his task to clean them conscientiously. How many hours he had spent in rubbing them, in greasing them, in polishing them, in cleaning the mechanism of the obturator, the bolt, the pressure screw! And now these pieces were as bright as if they had been on board a frigate of the United States Navy.

That day, then, in the presence of the whole personnel of the colony, Master Jup and Top included, the four guns were tried one after the other. They were loaded with pyroxyline, account being taken of its explosive power, which, as has been said, is four times that of ordinary powder. The projectile which they were to throw was cylindro-conical.

Pencroff, holding the lanyard of the tube, was ready to fire.

At a sign from Smith, the shot was fired. The ball, directed towards the sea, passed over the islet and was lost in the offing, at a distance which, however, could not be estimated with exactness.

The second gun was pointed at the outermost rocks of Jetsam Point, and the projectile, striking a sharp stone nearly three miles from Granite House, made it fly into splinters.

It was Herbert who had pointed this gun and fired it, and he was very proud of his first shot. Only Pencroff was prouder of it than he! A shot like that, and the honor of it belonging to his dear boy!

The third projectile, aimed this time at the downs which formed the upper shore of Union Bay, struck the sand at a distance of at least four miles; then, after ricocheting, it was lost in the sea in a cloud of spray.

For the fourth piece Smith slightly increased the charge, so as to try its extreme range. Then, each one having stood aside in case it should burst, the tube was fired by means of a long cord.

A violent report was heard, but the piece had held; and the colonists, rushing to the window, could see the projectile graze the rocks of Mandible Cape, nearly five miles from Granite House, and disappear in Shark Gulf.

“Well, Mr. Smith,” exclaimed Pencroff, whose hurrahs might have rivalled the reports themselves, “what do you say of our battery? All the pirates of the Pacific have only to present themselves before Granite House! Not one shall land there now without our permission!”

“Believe me, Pencroff,” replied the engineer, “it would be better not to have to make the experiment.”

“By the way,” resumed the sailor, “and those six rascals who are prowling about the island, what are we going to do with them? Are we to let them overrun our forests, our fields, our meadows? These pirates are regular jaguars, and it seems to me that we ought not to hesitate to treat them as such. What do you think, Ayrton?” added Pencroff, turning towards his companion.

Ayrton at first hesitated to reply, and Smith regretted that Pencroff had somewhat thoughtlessly put this question to him. And he was much moved when Ayrton answered in a humble voice:—

“I have been one of those jaguars, Mr. Pencroff, and I have no right to speak—“

And with a slow step he walked away.

Pencroff had understood.

“What a confounded brute I am!” he exclaimed. “Poor Ayrton! And yet he has as much right to speak here as any one!”

“Yes,” said Gideon Spilett, “but his reserve does him honor, and it is right to respect this feeling which he has about his sad past.”

“Understood, Mr. Spilett,” answered the sailor, “and I shall not be caught at it again! I would rather swallow my tongue than cause Ayrton any pain! But let us come back to the question. It seems to me that these ruffians have no right to any pity, and that we ought to rid the island of them as soon as possible.”

“Is that really your opinion, Pencroff?” asked the engineer.

“Quite my opinion.”

“And before pursuing them without mercy, you would not wait until they had again committed some act of hostility against us?”

“Is not what they have done already enough, then?” asked Pencroff, who understood nothing of these hesitations.

“They may come round to other feelings!” said Smith, “and perhaps repent—“

“They repent!” exclaimed the sailor, shrugging his shoulders.

“Pencroff, think of Ayrton!” then said Herbert, taking the sailor’s hand. “He has become an honest man again!”

Pencroff looked at his companions one after the other. He had never thought that his proposal would raise any hesitation whatever. His rough nature could not admit that any terms should be made with the rascals who had landed on the island, with the accomplices of Bob Harvey, the murderers of the crew of the Speedy; and he looked upon them as wild beasts which must be destroyed without hesitation and without remorse.

“Come!” said he. “I have everybody against me! You wish to be generous to those villains! Very well. May we not repent of it!”

“What danger do we run,” said Herbert, “if we take care to keep on our guard?”

“Hum!” said the reporter, who did not give a very decided opinion. “They are six, and well armed. Let each of them lie in ambush in some corner and fire on one of us, and they will soon be masters of the colony!”

“Why have they not done it?” replied Herbert. “No doubt because it was not their interest to do so. Besides, we are six also.”

“Well, well!” replied Pencroff, whom no reasoning could have convinced. “Let us leave these good people to attend to their little affairs, and think no more about them!”

“Come, Pencroff,” said Neb, “do not make yourself out so wicked as all that! Suppose one of those unfortunate men were here before you, within good range of your gun, you would not fire on him—“

“I would fire on him as I would on a mad dog, Neb,” replied Pencroff coldly.

“Pencroff,” then said the engineer, “you have often shown much deference to my advice. Will you, in this matter, once more leave it to me?”

“I will do as you please, Mr. Smith,” answered the sailor, who was not at all convinced.

“Very well, let us wait, and let us attack only if we are attacked.”

Thus was decided the line of conduct to be held towards the pirates, although Pencroff augured nothing good from it. They would not attack them, but they would keep on their guard. After all, the island was large and fertile. If any feeling of honesty yet remained at the bottom of their souls, these wretches might perhaps reform. Was it not their true interest, in the conditions in which they had to live, to make themselves a new life? In any case, were it only for humanity’s sake, it was right to wait. The colonists would perhaps no longer have, as before, the ease of going and coming without mistrust. Until then they had only had to guard against wild beasts, and now six convicts, perhaps of the worst kind, were prowling over their island. It was serious, no doubt, and for less brave men it would have been the loss of all security.

No matter! For the present the colonists were right as against Pencroff. Would they be right in the future? That would be seen.
