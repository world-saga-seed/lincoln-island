### 2. Restraint — 7/10

The text strictly obeys the core negative constraint: it never mentions or hints at Captain Nemo, the *Nautilus*, or *Mobilis in Mobili*, and offers zero explanation for the origin of the float. However, the narrator repeatedly over-explains internal motives, emotions, and mechanics that the specification wanted left implicit for the reader to feel:

* **Stating Pencroff's pride and motive for secrecy (twice):** *"He was a man of impulse, bold and open as the day, yet he possessed a certain stubborn pride that was all his own. To go back up the ladder and announce... would have mortified him beyond measure. What Pencroff brought home to Granite House he liked to lay upon the table whole and proven..."* and again at the end: *"Even now, though the float was gone, Pencroff could not bring himself to speak of a prize he had spotted and lost through his own delay."*
* **Explaining Herbert's restraint:** *"The boy asked no questions, however, for he knew the sailor’s humor..."*
* **Stating the emotional effect of Herbert's barnacle analysis:** *"The sailor’s face fell a little at these words. Like every castaway... his first, unvoiced thought was always of a ship... Herbert’s quiet observation, spoken without emphasis, dimmed that hope at once."*
* **Explaining why they failed to free it (narratorial summary before the dialogue makes the point):** *"Had they possessed two picks, or even one, they might have dug the float out in twenty minutes when they first reached the bar; five men working together could have wrenched it free, net and all, before the tide turned. Two of them, with nothing but a short iron knife and their bare hands, were helpless..."*

* *"six-inch blade"* (fine, though *"half a ton of wet sand"* is slightly colloquial modern hyperbole for Verne/White).

* No outright British spellings (*colour*, *grey*, *moulded* — note: the prompt used *moulded*, and the author correctly converted it to American *"molded"* to match White's 1876 translation).

### 4. Character — 8/10

* **Pencroff:** Reads well as Verne's practical, proud, sea-dog jack-of-all-trades. His obsession with fresh fish despite Neb's meat stores (*"a table without fish was no table at all for men who lived within biscuit-toss of the Pacific"*), his maritime frame of reference (Newfoundland banks, North Sea, kedge-anchor), and his desire to lay a finished triumph on Granite House's table are true to the character. His dialogue (*"Thunder!"*, *"as sure as I am a sailor"*) fits White's rendering, though his self-awareness at the end is slightly over-narrated.
* **Herbert:** Appropriately observant, deferential to Pencroff, and grounded in natural history (*"acorn-barnacles of a deep-water pelagic species"*). His quiet economy of speech (*"Latin, I think"*, *"We had it, Pencroff. For an hour."*) captures his composure well, though the narrator occasionally robs him of subtlety by explaining why he stays silent.

### 5. Prose — 8/10

* **Comparison to Stephen W. White's 1876 Translation:** White's translation is brisk, workmanlike, slightly stiff, and driven by action, dialogue, and concrete measurement. TEXT A imitates this register remarkably well—even down to the Verne-style chapter header (`CHAPTER XIX bis.` with em-dash topic summaries) and concrete measurements (*"eighty feet up"*, *"sinking an inch"*, *"two feet of turbid water"*).
* **Where it diverges:** TEXT A is slightly more atmospheric and interior than White's translation. White rarely lingers on psychological motive or moody weather description (*"blurring the outlines of Safety Islet and dimpling the surface of the river"*) without tying it immediately to plot mechanics. Still, placed between Chapters XIX and XX, it would pass with only minor stylistic friction.

### Additional Checks

* **Word Count:** **2,341 words** (excluding the chapter heading/subheads: **2,311 words**) against the **2,500** requested (~93.6% of target—well within reasonable range, though slightly short).
* **Factual Slips Against Verne's *The Mysterious Island*:**
* **Tool inventory slip:** The text states the colony possessed *"only the pickaxe which Spilett had dropped, a spade, a couple of rough hammers, and two knives which Cyrus Smith had forged from the steel halves of Top's collar."* In Verne's text (Part I, Chapters XIV–XIX), Cyrus Smith had already forged **picks, spades, hatchets, hammers, saws, and nails** from the iron ore smelted at the Chimneys before moving into Granite House (which required extensive pick-and-spade work to excavate the passages and rooms). Claiming they owned *only* one dropped pickaxe and one spade contradicts the preceding chapters' ironworks.
* **Minor chronological/geographic note:** The text accurately places Granite House at eighty feet up, Top's collar knives, Oswego tea, the Chimneys, Mandible Cape, Safety Islet, and the Mercy, and correctly dates the move into Granite House to late May 1865 just before Whit Sunday (June 4, 1865).
