# The Balloon in the Hurricane (draft 1)

THE MYSTERIOUS ISLAND.

By JULES VERNE.

PART I / SHIPWRECKED IN THE AIR

CHAPTER I.

THE HURRICANE OF 1865—CRIES IN THE AIR—A BALLOON CAUGHT BY A WATERSPOUT—THE TORN ENVELOPE—ONLY THE SEA IN SIGHT—FIVE PASSENGERS—WHAT TOOK PLACE IN THE BASKET—LAND AHEAD!—THE END OF THE DRAMA.

“Are we going up again?”

“No. On the contrary; we are going down!”

“Worse than that, Mr. Smith, we are falling!”

“For God’s sake throw over ballast!”

“The last sack is empty!”

“And the balloon rises again?”

“No!”

“I hear, as it were, the splashing of waves!”

“The sea is under the basket!”

“It cannot be five hundred feet off!”

Then a powerful voice rent the air, and these words rang out:—

“Overboard with everything that weighs!—everything!—and God help us!”

Such were the words which rang through the air above the vast wilderness of the Pacific, towards 4 o’clock in the afternoon of the 23d of March, 1865.

Doubtless, no one has forgotten that terrible northeast gale which vented its fury in the midst of the equinox of that year, and during which the barometer fell to 710 millimetres. It was a hurricane lasting without intermission from the 18th to the 26th of March. Covering a zone 1,800 miles wide, drawn obliquely to the equator, between the 35° of north latitude and 40° south, it occasioned immense destruction both in America and Europe and Asia. Cities in ruins, forests uprooted, shores devastated by the mountains of water which rushed upon them like bores, vessels cast ashore, which the returns of the Bureau Veritas numbered by hundreds, whole territories levelled by the waterspouts which destroyed everything in their path, thousands of persons crushed to the earth or engulfed in the sea; such were the witnesses to its fury left behind by this terrible hurricane. It surpassed in disaster those storms which so frightfully ravaged Havana and Guadeloupe, the one on the 25th of October, 1810, the other on the 26th of July, 1825.

At the very moment when so many catastrophes were taking place upon the land and the sea, a scene not less thrilling was enacting in the disordered heavens.

A balloon, caught in the whirl of a column of air, borne like a ball on the summit of a waterspout, spinning around as in some aerial whirlpool, rushed through space with a velocity of ninety miles an hour(1). Below the lower end of the balloon, dimly visible through the dense vapor, mingled with spray, which trailed down to the surface of the ocean, swung a basket containing five persons.

(1) That is, 46 metres a second, or 166 kilometres an hour (nearly forty-two leagues of four kilometres).

From whence came this aerial traveller, the veritable sport of the awful tempest? From what quarter of the globe had it taken its flight? Evidently it could not have been launched during the storm, and the storm had already been raging five days, its first symptoms manifesting themselves on the 18th. One might, therefore, have been justified in believing that it had come from a very great distance, as it could not have traversed less than 2,000 miles in twenty-four hours. At any rate, the passengers had been unable to estimate the course traversed since their departure, as they had nothing with which to calculate their position; and a curious effect must even have followed, that, though borne along in the midst of this tempest, they were unconscious of its violence. They were whirled and spun about and carried along, without feeling anything of this rotation, or of their movement in a horizontal direction. Their vision could not penetrate the thick fog massed together under the basket. Around them everything was obscure. The clouds were so dense that they could not tell the day from the night. No reflection of light, no sound from the habitations of men, no roaring of the ocean had penetrated that profound obscurity in which they were suspended during their passage through the upper air. Only on their rapid descent had they become conscious of the danger threatening them by the waves.

Meanwhile the balloon, disencumbered of the heavy articles, such as munitions, arms, and provisions, had risen into the upper strata of the atmosphere, to a height of 4,500 feet, and the passengers having discovered that the sea was beneath them, and realizing that the dangers above were less formidable than those below, did not hesitate to throw overboard everything, no matter how necessary, at the same time endeavoring to lose none of that fluid, the soul of the apparatus, which sustained them above the abyss.

The night passed in the midst of anxieties that would have proved fatal to souls less courageous. Then the day reappeared, and with the day the hurricane showed a tendency to moderate. From the very beginning of this day, the 24th of March, there were some signs of abatement. At dawn, the clouds, grown more vesicular, rose high into the heavens; and, in a few hours more, the waterspout had widened and broken. The wind, from a hurricane, had subsided into a “fresh gale,” that is to say, the speed of the moving strata of the atmosphere was diminished by half. It was still what sailors would call a “three reef breeze,” but the improvement in the disturbance of the elements was none the less considerable.

Toward eleven o’clock, the lower strata of the air had cleared visibly. The atmosphere exhaled that moist limpidity which may be seen, and even felt, after the passage of great meteors. It did not seem as if the storm had gone farther westward. It appeared to have killed itself. Perhaps it had flowed off in electric sheets after the breaking of the waterspout, as sometimes happens with the typhoons of the Indian Ocean.

About the same hour, however, it might have been seen that the balloon was again sinking slowly but surely into the lower strata of the air. It seemed also as if it was gradually collapsing, and that its envelope was lengthening and passing from a spherical into an oval form. Toward noon the balloon was hovering only 2,000 feet above the sea. It held 50,000 cubic feet of gas(2), and therefore, whether soaring to a great height or moving along horizontally, it had evidently been able to maintain itself for a long time in the air. In this emergency the voyagers threw overboard the remaining articles which still weighed down the basket, the few provisions they had kept, and everything they had in their pockets, while one of the party hoisted himself into the ring to which was fastened the cords of the net, and endeavored to closely tie the lower end of the balloon. But it was evident that the voyagers could no longer keep the balloon in the higher regions, and that their gas was failing them!

(2) About 1,700 cubic metres.

They were lost!

There was no continent, not even an island, visible beneath them. The wide expanse of ocean offered no point of rest, nothing upon which they could cast anchor. It was a vast sea on which the waves were still surging with incomparable violence. It was the limitless ocean, limitless even to them from their commanding height, though their gaze then extended over a radius of forty miles. It was a liquid plain, beaten without mercy and lashed by the hurricane, which must have seemed to them like a cavalcade of tossing billows, over which had been flung a vast net-work of white crests. No land in sight, not a ship!

In order, therefore, to save themselves from being swallowed up by the waves it was necessary to arrest this downward movement, let it cost what it might. And it was evidently to the accomplishment of this that the party were directing their efforts. But in spite of all they could do the balloon continued to descend, though at the same time moving with extreme rapidity in the direction of the wind, that is to say, from northeast to southwest.

It was a terrible situation, this, of these unfortunate men. Evidently no longer masters of the balloon, their efforts availed them nothing. The envelope collapsed more and more, and the gas continued to escape, without its being in any way possible to retain it. Faster and faster they fell, until at 1 o’clock they were not more than 600 feet above the sea. It was, indeed, impossible to prevent the escape of the gas, which poured freely out of a rent in the apparatus. By lightening the basket of everything the party had been able to continue their suspension in the air for several hours, but now the inevitable catastrophe could only be delayed, and unless some land appeared before nightfall, voyagers, balloon, and basket must disappear beneath the waves.

It was evident that these men were strong and able to face death. Not a murmur escaped their lips. They were determined to struggle to the last second to retard their fall, and they tried their last expedient. The basket, constructed of willow osiers, could not float, and they had no means of supporting it on the surface of the water. It was 2 o’clock, and the balloon was only 400 feet above the waves.

Then a manly voice was heard—the voice of a man whose heart knew no fear—responded to by others not less strong:—

“Everything is thrown out?”

“No, we yet have 10,000 francs in gold.”

A heavy bag immediately fell into the sea.

“Does the balloon rise?”

“A little, but it will soon fall again.”

“Is there nothing else we can get rid of?”

“Not a thing.”

“Yes there is; there’s the basket!”

“Let us catch hold of the net, and into the sea with the basket!”

The cords which attached the basket to the hoop were cut, and the balloon, as the former fell into the sea, rose again 2,000 feet. This was, indeed, the last means of lightening the apparatus. The five passengers had clambered into the net above the hoop, and, clinging to its meshes, looked into the abyss below.

Every one knows the statical sensibility of a balloon. It is only necessary to relieve it of the lightest object in order to produce a movement in a vertical direction. The apparatus floating in air acts like a mathematical balance. One can readily understand, then, that when disencumbered of a weight relatively great, its upward movement will be sudden and considerable. It was thus in the present instance. But after remaining poised for a moment at its height, the balloon began to descend again. It was impossible to repair the rent, through which the gas was rushing, and the men had done everything they could do. No human means could save them now, and they must look to God alone for succor.

At 4 o’clock, when the balloon was only 500 feet above the sea, the loud barking of a dog was heard. It was a dog which accompanied the passengers, and clung beside its master in the meshes of the net.

“Top has seen something!” cried one, and immediately afterwards a loud voice was heard:—

“Land! Land!”

The balloon, which the wind had continued to carry towards the southwest, had since dawn passed over a distance of several hundred miles, and a tolerably high land had just appeared in that direction. But it was still thirty miles to leeward, and even supposing they did not drift, it would take a full hour to reach it. An hour! Before that time could pass, would not the balloon be emptied of what gas remained? This was the terrible question!

The party distinctly saw that solid point which they must reach at all hazards. They did not know whether it was an island or a continent, as they scarcely knew towards what part of the world the tempest had hurried them. But they knew that this land, whether inhabited or desert, hospitable or not, must be reached.

At 4 o’clock it was plain that the balloon could no longer sustain itself. It grazed the surface of the sea, and already the crests of the enormous waves had several times lapped the base of the net, making it heavier still; and the balloon, like a bird with a shot in its wing, could only half sustain itself.

A half hour later, and the land was scarcely a mile distant. But the balloon, exhausted, flabby, hanging in wrinkles, with only a little gas remaining in its upper portion, unable to sustain the weight of those clinging to the net, soon had them half plunged in the sea, which lashed them with its furious billows. The envelope of the balloon then bellied out, and the wind, rushing into it, drove it along like a ship before the wind. Perhaps by this means it would reach the shore. But when only two cables’ length away four voices joined in a terrible cry. The balloon, though seemingly unable to rise again, after having been struck by a tremendous wave, made an unexpected bound into the air, as if it had been suddenly lightened of some of its weight. It rose 1,500 feet, and encountering a sort of eddy in the air, instead of being carried directly to land, it was drawn along in a direction nearly parallel thereto. At last, two minutes later, it reapproached the shore in an oblique direction, and fell for the last time upon the sand above the reach of the breakers. The passengers, assisting each other, succeeded in disengaging themselves from the meshes of the net; and the balloon, relieved of their weight, was caught up by the wind, and, like a wounded bird recovering for an instant, disappeared into space.

The basket had contained five passengers and a dog, and but four had been thrown upon the shore. The missing one had evidently been washed off by the great wave which had struck the net, and it was owing to this accident that the lightened balloon had been able to rise for the last time before falling upon the land. Scarcely had the four castaways—for so they may be called—felt the ground beneath their feet than all, thinking of the absent one, cried:—“Perhaps he is trying to swim ashore. Save him! Let us save him!”
