# The Return and the First Tears (draft 1)

CHAPTER XXXVII.

THE RETURN-DISCUSSION—SMITH AND THE UNKNOWN—BALLOON HARBOR-THE THIRD HARVEST-A WINDMILL-THE FIRST FLOUR AND THE FIRST BREAD-THE DEVOTION OF THE ENGINEER-A TOUCHING EXPERIENCE-TEARS.

The next morning, the 20th of October, at 7 o’clock, after a voyage of four days, the Bonadventure ran gently aground on the beach at the mouth of the Mercy. Smith and Neb, who had become very anxious at the stormy weather and the prolonged absence of their companions, had climbed, at daylight, to Prospect Plateau, and had at length perceived the sloop which had been so long in coming back!

“Thank Heaven! There they are,” exclaimed Smith; while Neb, in his joy, began to dance and to turn round and round, striking his hands together, and crying, “Oh, my master!”—a pantomime more touching than the finest speech!

The engineer’s first thought, on counting the number of persons on the deck of the Bonadventure whom he could perceive, was that Pencroff had not found the castaway of Tabor Island, or that, at least, the unfortunate man had refused to leave his island and to exchange one prison for another.

And, indeed, Pencroff, Gideon Spilett, and Herbert were alone on the deck of the Bonadventure.

The engineer and Neb were on the beach at the moment the sloop arrived, and before the party had leaped ashore, Smith said:—

“We have been very anxious at your delay, my friends. Has any misfortune happened to you?” “No, indeed; everything went finely,” replied Spilett. “We will tell you all about it.”

“Nevertheless, you have failed in your search, since there are only three of you, as when you started?” “Excuse me, sir, there are four of us,” said the sailor.

“Have you found this shipwrecked man?” “Yes.”

“And brought him back?” “Yes.” “Living?” “Yes.” “Where is he, and what is he, then?” “He is, or rather, he was a human being; and that is all, Cyrus, that we can say.”

The engineer was thereupon informed of everything that had happened during the voyage. They told him how the search had been conducted, how the only dwelling on the islet had long been abandoned, and how, at last, they had captured a castaway who seemed no longer to belong to the human species.

“And,” added Pencroff, “I don’t know whether we have done right in bringing him here.”

“Most certainly you have done right, Pencroff,” replied the engineer quickly.

“But the poor fellow has no reason left?” “Not now, perhaps,” replied Smith; “but, scarcely a few months ago, this poor fellow was a man like you and me. Who knows what might happen to the last one of us, after living for a long time alone on this island? Woe to him who is alone, my friends, and we must believe that solitude quickly overthrows reason, since you have found this poor being in such a condition.”

“But, Mr. Smith,” asked Herbert, “what makes you think that the brutishness of this man has come on only within a few months?”

“Because the paper we found had been recently written, and no one but this shipwrecked man could have written it.”

“Unless,” suggested Spilett, “it had been written by a companion of this man who has since died.”

“That is impossible, Spilett.”

“Why so?”

“Because, then, the paper would have mentioned two persons instead of one.”

Herbert briefly related the incidents of the passage, and dwelt upon that curious fact, a sort of momentary resurrection which had taken place in the mind of the prisoner, when, for an instant, at the height of the storm, he had become a sailor again.

“Good, Herbert,” said the engineer, “you are right to attach great importance to this fact. This poor man cannot be incurable; despair has made him what he is. But here he will find his kindred, and since he still has a soul in him, that soul we will save!”

Then, to Smith’s great pity and Neb’s wonderment, the castaway of Tabor Island was brought up from the cabin which he occupied forward in the Bonadventure, and, once on land, he at once manifested a desire to escape. But Smith, approaching him, laid his hand authoritatively upon his shoulder and looked at him with infinite gentleness. Thereupon the poor wretch, submitting to a sort of instantaneous mastery, became quiet little by little, his eyes fell, his head dropped forward, and he made no further resistance.

“Poor abandoned creature!” murmured the engineer.

Smith regarded him attentively. To judge from his appearance, this miserable creature had nothing of the human left in him; but Smith caught in his glance, as the reporter had done, an almost imperceptible gleam of intelligence.

It was decided that the abandoned man, or rather the Unknown, for so his new companions henceforth called him, should stay in one of the rooms of Granite House, from which he could not escape. He made no resistance to being conducted there, and with good care they might, perhaps, hope that some day he would prove one companion more to the colonists of Lincoln Island.

Neb hastened to prepare breakfast, for the reporter, Herbert, and Pencroff were dying of hunger, and during the meal Smith made them relate in detail every incident of the cruise. He agreed with them in thinking that the name of the Britannia gave them reason to believe that the Unknown was either English or American; and, moreover, through that shaggy beard, and under the thicket which served him for hair, the engineer thought he recognized the features characteristic of an Anglo-Saxon.

“But, by the way, Herbert,” said the reporter, “you have never told us how you met this savage, and we know nothing, except that he would have strangled you, had we not had the good fortune to arrive in time to help you.”

“Indeed, I am not sure that I can tell just what happened,” replied Herbert. “I was, I think, gathering plants, when I heard a noise like that of an avalanche falling from a very high tree. I had hardly time to turn, when this unhappy creature, who had, doubtless, been hidden crouching in the tree, threw himself upon me in less time than it takes me to tell it; and, unless Mr. Spilett and Pencroff—”

“You were in great danger, indeed, my boy,” said Smith; “but perhaps, if this had not happened, this poor being would have escaped your search, and we would have been without another companion.”

“You expect, then, to make him a man again?” asked the reporter.

“Yes,” replied Smith.

Breakfast ended, all returned to the shore and began unloading the sloop; and the engineer examined the arms and tools, but found nothing to establish the identity of the Unknown.

The pigs captured on the islet were thought likely to prove very profitable to Lincoln Island, and they were taken to the stables, to which they would easily become accustomed. The two barrels of powder and shot and the caps were a great acquisition, and it was even agreed to make a small powder magazine, either outside Granite House or even in the upper cavern, where there would be no danger of an explosion. However, the use of the pyroxyline was to be continued, for, since it gave excellent results, there was no reason to substitute ordinary powder for it.

When the sloop was unloaded Pencroff said:—

“I think, Mr. Smith, that it would be prudent to put our Bonadventure in a safe place.”

“Is it not safe enough at the mouth of the Mercy?”

“No, sir,” replied the sailor. “Half the time she is aground on the sand, which strains her. She is a good boat, you see, and she behaved admirably in the gale which struck us so violently on our return.”

“Could not she be moored out in the stream?”

“No doubt she could, sir, but the place is unsheltered, and in an easterly wind I am afraid the Bonadventure would suffer much from the seas.”

“Very well; where do you want to put her?”

“In Balloon Harbor,” replied the sailor. “It seems to me that that little inlet, sheltered by the rocks, is just the place for her.”

“Isn’t it rather far off?”

“Bah! it is not more than three miles from Granite House, and we have a good straight road there.”

“Have your way, Pencroff, and take your Bonadventure there,” replied the engineer. “Nevertheless, I should prefer to have her more immediately under our sight. We must, when we have time, make a small harbor for her.”

“Capital!” cried Pencroff. “A harbor with a light house, a breakwater, and a dry dock! Oh, indeed, sir, everything becomes only too easy with you!”

“Yes, my good Pencroff, always provided that you assist me, for you do three fourths of all our work.”

Herbert and the sailor went aboard the Bonadventure again; the anchor was weighed, the sail hoisted, and the breeze from the sea carried her rapidly to Claw Cape. In a couple of hours the sloop rode quietly at anchor in the tranquil water of Balloon Harbor.

During the first few days that the Unknown was at Granite House, had he given any indication of a change in his savage nature? Did not a brighter light illumine the depths of his intelligence? Was not, in short, the soul returning to the body? Undoubtedly, yes; and so much so, that Smith and Spilett questioned whether this reason had ever entirely forsaken him.

At first this man, accustomed to the open air and the boundless liberty which he had enjoyed in Tabor Island, was seized with fits of sullen fury, and there was danger of his throwing himself down on the beach from one of the windows of Granite House. But little by little he grew more quiet, and he was allowed to move about without restraint.

There was reason, then, to hope, and to hope much. Already forgetting his carnivorous instincts, he accepted a less bestial nourishment than that on which he had fed on the islet, and cooked food did not produce in him the sentiment of disgust which he had shown on board the Bonadventure.

Smith had taken advantage of a time when the man was asleep to cut the hair and beard which had grown like a mane about his face, and had given him such a savage aspect. He had also clothed him more decently, after taking from him the rag of cloth which covered him, and the result was that the Unknown appeared more like a human being, and it seemed as if the expression of his eyes was softened. Certainly, formerly, when intelligence lighted it, the face of this man must have had a sort of beauty.

Every day, Smith made a point of passing some hours in his company. He worked beside him, and occupied himself in various ways to attract his attention. It might suffice, indeed, if a single flash rekindled that soul, if a single remembrance crossed his brain to recall his reason. That had been plainly seen during the storm, on board the Bonadventure! Neither did the engineer neglect to speak in a loud voice, so as to penetrate by both sound and sight to the depths of this torpid intelligence. Sometimes one or another of the party, sometimes all, joined the engineer, and they usually talked of such matters pertaining to the sea as would be likely to interest a sailor. At times the Unknown gave a sort of vague attention to what was said, and soon the colonists began to think that he partly understood them. Sometimes, even, his expression would be profoundly dolorous, proving that he suffered inwardly, for his face could not have deceived them to such a degree. Nevertheless, he did not speak, although they thought, at times, that words were about to pass his lips.

However that might be, the poor creature was calm and sad! But was not the calmness only on the surface, and the sadness the result of his confinement? They could not yet say. Seeing only certain objects and in a limited space, always with the colonists, to whom he must at last become accustomed, having no desire to satisfy, better clothed and better fed, it was natural that his physical nature should soften little by little; but was he imbued with the new life, or, to use an expression justly applicable to the case, was he only tamed, as an animal in the presence of its master? This was the important question Smith was anxious to determine, and meantime he did not wish to be too abrupt with his patient. For to him, the Unknown was but a sick person. Would he ever be a convalescent?

Therefore, the engineer watched him unceasingly. How he laid in wait for his soul, so to speak! How ready he was to lay hold of it!

The colonists followed with sincere emotion all the phases of this cure undertaken by Smith. All aided him in this work of humanity, and all, save perhaps the incredulous Pencroff, soon came to share in his hope and his faith.

The calm of the Unknown was profound, as has been said, and he showed for the engineer, whose influence over him was apparent, a sort of attachment, and Smith resolved now to test him by transporting him to another scene, to that ocean which his eyes had formerly been accustomed to look upon, to the forest border, which would recall those woods where so many years of his life had been passed!

“But,” said Spilett, “can we hope that once at liberty, he will not escape?”

“We must make the experiment,” replied the engineer.

“All right,” said Pencroff. “You will see, when this fellow snuffs the fresh air and sees the coast clear, if he don’t make his legs spin!”

“I don’t think it,” replied the engineer.

“We will try, any how,” said Spilett.

“We will try,” replied the engineer.

It was the 30th of October, and the Unknown had been a prisoner in Granite House for nine days. It was a beautiful, warm, sunshiny day.

Smith and Pencroff went to the room of the Unknown, whom they found lying near the window, gazing at the sky.

“Come, my friend,” said the engineer to him.

The Unknown rose immediately. His eye was fixed on Smith, whom he followed; and the sailor, little confident in the results of the experiment, walked behind him.

Having reached the door, Smith and Pencroff made him get into the elevator, while Neb, Herbert, and Spilett were waiting for them at the foot of Granite House. The basket descended, and in a few seconds all were standing together on the shore.

The colonists moved off a little distance from the Unknown, so as to leave him some liberty.

He made some steps forward towards the sea, and his eyes shone with extreme animation, but he made no effort to escape. He looked at the little waves, which, broken by the islet, died away on the shore.

“It is only the sea, as yet,” remarked Spilett, “and it is possible that this does not give him the idea of escaping.”

“Yes,” replied Smith, “we must take him to the plateau on the edge of the forest. There the experiment will be more conclusive.”

“Besides, he cannot get away, since the bridges are all raised,” said Neb.

“Oh, he is not the man to be troubled by such a brook as Glycerine Creek; he could leap it at a bound,” returned Pencroff.

“We will see presently,” Smith merely replied, keeping his eyes fixed on those of his patient.

The Unknown was then led towards the mouth of the Mercy, and all, ascending the left bank of the river, proceeded to Prospect Plateau.

Having reached the place where the first fine trees of the forest grew, their leaves gently stirred by the breeze, the Unknown seemed to drink in with rapture the penetrating perfume which filled the air, and a long sigh escaped from his breast.

The colonists stood some paces back, ready to seize him if he attempted to escape.

And, indeed, the poor creature was upon the point of plunging into the creek that separated him from the forest; for an instant his legs straightened like a spring—but almost immediately he drew back upon himself, half sank down, and a large tear rolled from his eyes!

“Ah!” cried Smith, “you are a man again, then, since you weep!”
