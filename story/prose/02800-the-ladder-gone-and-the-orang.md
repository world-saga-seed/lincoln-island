# The Ladder Gone and the Orang (final)

CHAPTER XXVIII.

PENCROFF’S HALLOOS—A NIGHT IN THE CHIMNEYS—HERBERT’S ARROW—SMITH’S PLAN—AN UNEXPECTED SOLUTION—WHAT HAD HAPPENED IN GRANITE HOUSE—HOW THE COLONISTS OBTAINED A NEW DOMESTIC.

Smith stood silent. His companions searched in the obscurity along the wall, in case the wind had displaced the ladder, and over the ground, in case it had come loose. But the ladder had absolutely disappeared, although it was impossible to tell in the deep darkness whether a gust of wind had not carried it up and lodged it on the first ledge, half-way up the wall.

“If this is a joke, it’s a pretty poor one,” cried Pencroff. “To get home and not be able to find the staircase to go up to one’s room, is no laughing matter for tired men.”

Neb, for his part, broke out into exclamations.

“But there has been no wind!” said Herbert.

“I’m beginning to think that strange things happen in Lincoln Island!” said Pencroff.

“Strange?” rejoined Spilett. “Why no, Pencroff, nothing is more natural. Somebody has come while we have been absent, and has taken possession of the house and drawn up the ladder!”

“Some one!” cried the sailor. “Who could it be?”

“Why, the man who shot the leaden pellet,” answered the reporter. “What would he be good for, if not to explain our mishap?”

“Very well, if any one is up there,” replied Pencroff, with an oath, for he was beginning to lose patience, “I will hail him, and he had better answer.”

And in a voice of thunder the sailor gave a prolonged “Ohe,” which was loudly repeated by the echoes.

The colonists listened, and thought that they heard a sort of chuckling proceed from the height of Granite House, though they could not make out whence it came. But there was no answering voice to the sailor, who repeated his vigorous appeal in vain.

Here was an event that would have astonished people the most indifferent, and from their situation the colonists could not be that. To them, the slightest incident was of moment, and certainly during their seven months’ residence nothing equal to this had happened.

However that might be, forgetting their fatigue in the strangeness of the event, they stood there at the foot of Granite House, not knowing what to think or what to do, questioning one another without being able to answer, and making one supposition after another, each more inadmissible than the last. Neb lamented, much disappointed at not being able to get back to his kitchen, especially as the provisions taken for the journey had all been eaten, and he had no present means of renewing them.

“There is but one thing to do, my friends,” said Smith, “to wait until daylight, and then to be governed by circumstances. Meanwhile let us go to the Chimneys, where we will be sheltered, and, even if we cannot sup, we can at least sleep.”

“But who is the ill-mannered fellow that has played us this trick?” asked Pencroff again, who could not reconcile himself to the adventure.

Whoever he was, the only thing to do was, as the engineer had said, to go back to the Chimneys and there wait for day. Top was ordered to remain under the windows of Granite House, and when Top received an order, Top obeyed it without remark. The brave dog remained at the foot of the wall, while his master and his companions took shelter among the rocks.

The colonists, tired as they were, slept but little on the sand of the Chimneys. Not only were they very anxious to learn the full meaning of this new incident, whether it was the result of some chance of which daylight would show them the natural causes, or whether, on the contrary, it was the work of a human being, but their beds were also very uncomfortable. However that might be, in one way or another, their house was occupied at present, and they were unable to get into it. Now Granite House was not only their dwelling, it was their storehouse. All the material of the colony was stored there, arms, instruments, tools, ammunition, stores of provisions, etc. If all this should be pillaged, they would have again to begin at the beginning, and to make arms and tools anew. It would be a serious thing! In their anxiety, one or the other went out every moment to see if the dog was keeping good watch. Smith, alone, waited with his accustomed patience, although his tenacious reason was exasperated at finding itself confronted by something utterly inexplicable, and he was indignant at the thought that around him, over him, perhaps, was exercising an influence to which he could give no name. Spilett fully sharing his opinion on this point, they conversed together several times, but in an undertone, of those unaccountable events which defied all their perspicacity and experience. Certainly, there was a mystery about this island, but how discover it? Herbert did not know what to imagine, and would have liked to question Smith. As to Neb, he had ended by saying to himself that this was his master’s business and not his; and if he had not feared offending his companions, the brave negro would have slept this night as soundly as if he had been in his bed in Granite House.

Pencroff, however, more than all the rest, was in a rage, and in good earnest very angry.

“It’s a joke,” he said. “It’s a joke that is played on us. Well, I don’t like such jokes, and the joker won’t like it, if I catch him!”

At dawn the colonists, well armed, followed along the shore to the reefs. Granite House, struck full by the rising sun, would soon be lighted by the dawn; and, in fact, before 5 o’clock its windows, with their shutters closed, appeared through their leafy curtain. Everything, from this side, appeared to be in order, but an exclamation escaped from the colonists when they perceived that the door which they had left closed was wide open. There could be no more doubt that some one was in Granite House. The upper ladder, which usually hung from the ledge to the door, was in its place; but the lower had been drawn up to the threshold. It was more than evident that the intruders wished to guard against a surprise. As to telling who or how many they were, that was still impossible, as none had yet shown themselves.

Pencroff shouted again, but without answer.

“The beggars!” he exclaimed, “to sleep as soundly as if they were at home! Halloo! pirates! bandits! corsairs! sons of John Bull!”

When Pencroff, as an American, called any one a “son of John Bull,” he had reached the acme of insult.

Just then, it became broad day, and the facade of Granite House was illuminated by the rays of the rising sun. But inside as well as without all was still and calm. The colonists were beginning to ask themselves whether Granite House was occupied or not; and yet the position of the ladder proved it sufficiently, and it was even certain that the occupants, whoever they might be, had not been able to get out. But how could they get up to them?

Herbert conceived the idea of shooting an arrow attached to a cord between the lower rungs of the ladder which were hanging from the doorway. They would thus be able by means of the cord to pull this ladder down, and gain access to Granite House. There was evidently nothing else to do, and with a little skill this attempt ought to succeed. Very fortunately, the bows and arrows had been left in one of the passages of the Chimneys, and they found there, also, some scores of fathoms of light hibiscus cord. Pencroff unrolled this, and fastened the end to a well-feathered arrow. Then Herbert having placed the arrow in his bow took aim with extreme care at the hanging part of the ladder.

The others stationed themselves some distance in the background to observe what might happen at the windows of Granite House, and the reporter covered the doorway with his carbine.

The bow was loosed, the arrow whistled upward with the cord, and passed between the two lower rungs of the ladder. The operation had succeeded. But just as Herbert, having caught the end of the cord, was about giving it a pull to make the ladder fall, an arm thrust quickly between the door and the wall seized the ladder and drew it within Granite House.

“You triple beggar!” cried Pencroff. “If a ball would settle you, you would not have to wait long!”

“But what is it?” demanded Neb.

“What! didn’t you see?”

“No.”

“Why, it’s a monkey, a macaque, a sapajo, a she-monkey, an orang, a baboon, a gorilla, a sagoin! Our house has been invaded by monkeys, which have climbed up the ladder while we were away.”

And at the moment, as if to prove the truth of what the sailor said, three or four quadrumana threw open the window shutters and saluted the true proprietors of the place with a thousand contortions and grimaces.

“I knew all the time it was a joke,” cried Pencroff, “but here’s one of the jokers that will pay for the others!” he added, quickly covering a monkey with his gun and firing. All disappeared but this one, which, mortally wounded, fell on the beach.

This monkey was very large and evidently belonged to the first order of quadrumana. Whether a chimpanzee, an orang, a gorilla, or a gibbon, it ranked among these anthropomorphi, so called on account of their likeness to the human race. Herbert declared it was an orang-outang, and we all know that the lad understood zoology.

“What a fine beast!” cried Neb.

“As fine as you choose!” answered Pencroff, “but I don’t see yet how we are going to get in!”

“Herbert is a good shot,” said the reporter, “and his bow is here! Let him try again—”

“Bah! these monkeys are cunning,” cried Pencroff, “and they will not come to the windows again, and we cannot shoot them; and when I think of the damage they can do in the rooms and in the magazine—”

“Have patience,” answered Smith. “These animals cannot hold us in check very long.”

“I will be sure of that when they are down here,” rejoined Pencroff. “And, first of all, can you say, Mr. Smith, how many dozens of these jokers there may be up there?”

It would have been hard to answer Pencroff, and it was not easy to try again the experiment of the arrow, as the lower end of the ladder had been drawn within the doorway, and when they pulled on the cord again, it broke, and the ladder remained, as before.

It was, indeed, vexatious. Pencroff was in a fury, and, although the situation had a certain comic aspect, he did not think it funny at all. It was evident that the colonists would, eventually, get back into their house and drive out the monkeys, but when and how they could not say.

Two hours passed, during which the monkeys avoided showing themselves; but they were there, for all that, and, three or four times, a muzzle or paw slipped by the door or the windows, and was saluted by a shot.

“Let us conceal ourselves,” said the engineer, at length. “Perhaps the monkeys will then think we have gone off, and will show themselves again. Let Herbert and Spilett remain hidden behind the rocks and fire on any that appear.”

The directions of the engineer were followed, and while the reporter and the lad, who were the best shots in the party, took their positions within good range, but out of sight of the monkeys, the others went over the plateau to the forest to shoot some game, as it was breakfast time and they had no food.

In half an hour the hunters returned with some rock pigeons, which were roasted as well as could be managed. Not a monkey had shown itself.

Spilett and Herbert went to their breakfast, while Top kept watch under the windows. Then they returned to their post. Two hours later the situation was unchanged. The quadrumana gave no sign of existence, and it seemed as if they must have disappeared; but it was more likely that, frightened by the death of one of their number and the detonations of the guns, they kept themselves hidden in the chambers or the store-room of Granite House. And, when the colonists thought of all that was stored in this latter room, the patience which the engineer had recommended turned at last into violent irritation, and indeed they could not be blamed for it.

“It is too bad!” exclaimed the reporter, at length; “and there is really no reason why it should ever come to an end!”

“We must make these beggars clear out!” cried Pencroff. “We can readily do it, even if there are twenty of them, in a hand-to-hand fight! Oh, is there no way we can get at them?”

“Yes,” replied Smith, struck by an idea.

“One?” rejoined Pencroff. “Well, that must be the right one, since there is no other! What is it?”

“Try to get into Granite House by the old weir,” answered the engineer.

“Ah! a thousand and a thousand devils!” cried the sailor. “And I never thought of that!”

This was, indeed, the only way to get into Granite House, in order to fight the band and drive them out. It is true that, if they tore down the cemented wall which closed the weir, the work would have all to be done over again; but, fortunately, Smith had not yet effected his design of hiding this opening by covering it again with the lake, for then the operation would have taken some time.

It was already past noon when the colonists, well armed and furnished with picks and mattocks, left the Chimneys, passed under the windows of Granite House, and, having ordered Top to remain at his post, made ready to climb the left bank of the Mercy, so as to reach Prospect Plateau. But they had hardly gone fifty paces, when they heard the furious barkings of the dog, as if making a desperate appeal. All halted.

“Let us run back,” cried Pencroff. And all ran back down the bank at full speed.

Arrived at the turn, they saw that the situation was changed. The monkeys, seized with a sudden fright, startled by some unknown cause, were trying to escape. Two or three were running and springing from window to window, with the agility of clowns. They did not even try to replace the ladder, by which they could easily have descended; in their fright, perhaps, they had forgotten this way of escape. Soon five or six were in such a position that they could be shot, and the colonists, taking aim at their ease, fired. Some fell, wounded or killed, within the chambers, uttering sharp cries. Others, falling to the ground without, were crushed by the fall, and a few moments afterwards it seemed as if there was not one living quadrumana in Granite House.

“Hurrah,” said Pencroff, “hurrah, hurrah!”

“Don’t cheer yet,” said Spilett.

“Why not?” asked Pencroff. “Ain’t they all killed?”

“Doubtless: but that does not give us the means of getting in.”

“Let us go to the weir!” exclaimed Pencroff.

“No doubt,” said the engineer. “Nevertheless it would have been preferable—”

And at the instant, as if in answer to the observation of the engineer, they saw the ladder slide over the door-sill and unroll itself to the ground.

“Ah! by the thousand pipes, but that is too much!” cried Pencroff, looking at Smith.

“Too much!” muttered Smith, springing first up the ladder.

“Take care, Mr. Smith!” exclaimed Pencroff, “if there should be any more of those sagoins—”

“We will soon see,” responded the other, without stopping.

All his companions followed him and in a moment were within the doorway.

They searched everywhere. No one was in the chambers or in the storeroom, which remained undisturbed by the quadrumana.

“And the ladder,” said Pencroff; “where is the gentleman who pushed it down to us?”

But just then a cry was heard, and a huge monkey, that had taken refuge in the corridor, sprang into the great hall, pursued by Neb.

“Ah, the bandit!” cried Pencroff; and, hatchet in hand, he was about to split the animal’s head, when Smith stopped him.

“Spare it, Pencroff.”

“What, show mercy to this blackamoor?”

“Yes, it is he that has thrown us the ladder,” said the engineer, in a voice so strange, that it was hard to say whether he was in earnest or not.

Nevertheless, all threw themselves on the monkey, which, after a brave resistance, was thrown down and tied.

“Ugh!” exclaimed Pencroff; “and now what will we do with it?”

“Make a servant of it,” answered Herbert, half in earnest, as the lad knew what use might be made of this intelligent race of quadrumana.

The colonists gathered round the monkey and examined it attentively. It did, in fact, belong to that species of anthropomorphi in which the facial angle is not visibly inferior to that of the Australians or Hottentots. He was an orang, and as such had neither the ferocity of the baboon, nor the thoughtlessness of the macaque, nor the uncleanliness of the sagoin, nor the impatience of the magot, nor the bad instincts of the cynocephalous. It is to this family of anthropomorphi that so many traits are referred which show in these animals an almost human intelligence. Employed in houses, they can wait on the table, do chamber-work, brush clothes, black boots, handle the knife, the spoon and the fork with skill, and even—drink the wine, as well as the best flunkey that ever went on two legs without feathers. It is known that Buffon possessed one of these monkeys, which served him a long time as a zealous and faithful servant.

The one at present tied in the hall of Granite House was a big fellow, six feet high, deep-chested, and finely built, a medium-sized head, with a facial angle of sixty-five degrees, a well-rounded skull, and a prominent nose, and a skin covered with smooth hair, soft and shining,—in short, a finished type of anthropomorphi. Its eyes, somewhat smaller than those of a human being, sparkled with intelligence; its white teeth glistened beneath its moustache, and it wore a small, curly, nut-brown beard.

“He is a fine chap,” said Pencroff. “If we only understood his language, one might talk with him!”

“Then,” said Neb, “is it in earnest, my master? Will we take it as a domestic?”

“Yes, Neb,” said the engineer, smiling. “But you need not be jealous.”

“And I hope it will make an excellent servant,” added Herbert. “It seems young; its education will be easy, and we will not have to use force to make it mind, nor to pull out its canine teeth, as is done in such cases. It cannot fail to become attached to masters who only treat it well.”

“And we will do that,” said Pencroff, who having forgotten his recent wrath against the “jokers,” approached the orang and accosted him with:—

“Hullo, my boy, how goes it?”

The orang responded with a little grunt, which seemed to denote a not bad temper.

“You want to join the colony, do you? Would you like to enter the service of Mr. Smith?”

The monkey gave another affirmative grunt.

“And you’ll be satisfied with your board as wages?”

A third affirmative grunt.

“His conversation is a little monotonous,” observed Spilett.

“Well,” replied Pencroff, “the best domestics are those that speak least. And then, no wages! Do you hear, my boy? At first we give you no wages, but we will double them later, if you suit us!”

Thus the colonists added to their number one who was to render them more than one service. As to a name, the sailor asked that he should be called, in remembrance of another monkey which he had known, Jupiter, or by abbreviation, Jup. And thus, without more ado, Master Jup was installed in Granite House.
