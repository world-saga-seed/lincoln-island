# The New Ship and the Third Winter (draft 1)

CHAPTER LVI.

THREE YEARS HAVE PASSED—THE QUESTION OF THE NEW SHIP—WHAT IS RESOLVED UPON—PROSPERITY OF THE COLONY—THE BUILDING YARD—THE COLD OF THE SOUTHERN HEMISPHERE—PENCROFF RESIGNS HIMSELF—WASHING THE LINEN—MOUNT FRANKLIN.

Three years had passed since the prisoners of Richmond had escaped, and how often, during those three years, had they spoken of their country, always present to their thoughts!

They did not doubt that the civil war was by this time ended, and it seemed impossible to them that the just cause of the North had not triumphed. But what had been the incidents of this terrible war? How much blood had it cost? Which of their own friends had fallen in the struggle? This was what they often talked of, without yet foreseeing the day when it would be granted them to see their country again. To return there, were it only for a few days, to renew the social bond with the inhabited world, to establish a communication between their native land and their island, and then to pass the longest, perhaps the best, part of their existence in this colony which they had founded, and which would then be dependent on the mother country; was this, then, a dream impossible to realize?

But there were only two ways of realizing this dream: either a ship would some day appear in the waters of Lincoln Island, or the colonists would themselves build a vessel strong enough to keep the sea as far as the nearest lands.

“Unless,” said Pencroff, “our genius himself furnishes the means of sending us home!”

And, truly, had any one come and told Pencroff and Neb that a ship of three hundred tons was waiting for them in Shark Gulf or at Port Balloon, they would not even have made a gesture of surprise. In this order of ideas they expected anything.

But Cyrus Smith, less confident, advised them to come back to reality; and he did so on the subject of building a vessel, a work truly urgent, since it was a question of depositing as soon as possible on Tabor Island a document which should make known Ayrton’s new residence.

The Bonadventure no longer existing, six months at least would be necessary for the construction of a new vessel. Now, winter was coming on, and the voyage could not be made before the following spring.

“We have time, then, to get ready for the fine season,” said the engineer, who was talking of these things with Pencroff. “I think, therefore, my friend, that, since we have to build our craft again, it will be better to give her larger dimensions. The arrival of the Scotch yacht at Tabor Island is very problematical. It may even be that she came several months ago, and has gone away again, after having vainly sought some trace of Ayrton. Would it not, then, be well to build a ship which, should occasion arise, could carry us either to the Polynesian archipelagos or to New Zealand? What do you think of it?”

“I think, Mr. Cyrus,” answered the sailor, “I think that you are quite as capable of making a large ship as a small one. We want neither wood nor tools. It is only a question of time.”

“And how many months would the building of a vessel of from two hundred and fifty to three hundred tons require?” asked Cyrus Smith.

“Seven or eight months at least,” answered Pencroff. “But it must not be forgotten that winter is coming, and that in the hard frosts wood is difficult to work. Let us reckon, then, on some weeks lost; and if our ship is ready by next November, we must think ourselves very lucky.”

“Well,” answered Cyrus Smith, “that would be precisely the favorable season for undertaking a voyage of some importance, either to Tabor Island or to some more distant land.”

“Just so, Mr. Cyrus,” answered the sailor. “Make your plans, then; the workmen are ready, and I fancy that Ayrton will be able to lend us a good hand in the business.”

The colonists, being consulted, approved the engineer’s project, and it was, in truth, the best thing to be done. It is true that the building of a ship of two or three hundred tons was a heavy task; but the colonists had a confidence in themselves which many successes already gained had well justified.

Cyrus Smith therefore busied himself with drawing the plan of the vessel and determining her model. Meanwhile his companions were employed in felling and carting the trees which were to furnish the knees, the frame and the planking. It was the forest of the Far West which gave the best kinds of oak and elm. They took advantage of the opening already made during the last excursion to make a practicable road, which took the name of the Far West Road, and the trees were carried to the Chimneys, where the building yard was established. As to the road in question, it was capriciously laid out, and it was partly the choice of the timber which determined its line; but it made easy the access to a considerable portion of Serpentine Peninsula.

It was important that this timber should be quickly felled and cut up, for it could not be used while still green, and time must be left to harden it. The carpenters therefore worked with ardor during the month of April, which was troubled only by a few rather violent equinoctial gales. Master Jup aided them adroitly, either climbing to the top of a tree to fix the ropes for felling it, or lending his robust shoulders to carry the lopped trunks.

All this timber was piled under a vast shed of planks, which was built near the Chimneys, and there it awaited the moment of being put to use.

The month of April was tolerably fine, as the month of October often is in the northern zone. At the same time the work of the fields was actively pushed forward, and soon every trace of devastation had disappeared from Prospect Plateau. The mill was rebuilt, and new buildings rose on the site of the poultry-yard. It had seemed necessary to rebuild them on larger dimensions, for the feathered population was increasing in a considerable proportion. The stables now contained five onagas, four of them vigorous and well broken, allowing themselves to be harnessed or ridden, and a little one which had just been born. The plant of the colony had been increased by a plough, and the onagas were employed in ploughing, like true oxen of Yorkshire or Kentucky. Each of the colonists took his share of the work, and their arms were never idle. And so what fine health these workers enjoyed, and with what good humor they enlivened the evenings at Granite House, forming a thousand projects for the future!

It need hardly be said that Ayrton shared the common life in every respect, and that there was no longer any question of his going to live at the corral. Nevertheless, he remained always sad and little communicative, and joined rather in the labors than in the pleasures of his companions. But he was a rough worker at a task, vigorous, adroit, ingenious, intelligent. He was esteemed and loved by all, and he could not be ignorant of it.

Meanwhile the corral was not abandoned. Every two days one of the colonists, driving the cart or mounted on one of the onagas, went to look after the flock of mouflons and goats, and brought back the milk which supplied Neb’s pantry. These excursions were at the same time occasions for hunting. And so Herbert and Gideon Spilett, Top in front, ran oftener than any of their companions along the road to the corral; and, with the excellent arms which they had at their disposal, capybaras, agoutis, kangaroos, wild boars, wild hogs for the large game, ducks, grouse, heath-cocks, jacamars, snipe for the small, were never wanting at the house. The produce of the warren, that of the oyster-bed, a few turtles which were taken, a new haul of those excellent salmon which came once more crowding into the waters of the Mercy, the vegetables of Prospect Plateau, the natural fruits of the forest; these were riches upon riches, and Neb, the head cook, was hardly able to store them all away.

It need hardly be said that the telegraph wire laid between the corral and Granite House had been restored, and that it was worked whenever one or other of the colonists was at the corral and thought it necessary to spend the night there. Besides, the island was safe now, and no aggression was to be feared,—at least on the part of men.

Nevertheless, what had happened might happen again. A descent of pirates, or even of escaped convicts, was always to be feared. It was possible that companions of Bob Harvey, accomplices still detained at Norfolk, had been in the secret of his plans, and might be tempted to imitate him. The colonists therefore did not fail to watch the approaches to the island, and every day their telescope was swept over the wide horizon which shut in Union Bay and Washington Bay. When they went to the corral, they examined the western part of the sea with no less attention; and, by climbing the spur, their eyes could range over a wide sector of the western horizon.

Nothing suspicious appeared, but still it was necessary to be always on their guard.

So the engineer, one evening, told his friends of the project which he had conceived of fortifying the corral. It seemed to him prudent to raise its palisaded enclosure, and to flank it with a sort of blockhouse, in which, should occasion arise, the colonists could hold out against a band of enemies. As Granite House must be considered impregnable from its very position, the corral, with its buildings, its stores, and the animals which it contained, would always be the object of any pirates, whoever they might be, who should land on the island. If the colonists were forced to shut themselves up there, they must be able to resist without disadvantage.

This was a project to be thought over, and its execution, besides, was necessarily put off till the next spring.

Towards the 15th of May the keel of the new vessel lay stretched along the building yard, and soon the stem and stern-post, mortised into each of its extremities, rose almost perpendicularly. This keel, of good oak, measured a hundred and ten feet in length, which would allow a breadth of twenty-five feet to the midship beam. But this was all that the carpenters could do before the arrival of the frosts and the bad weather. During the following week they still put in place the first frames of the stern; then the work had to be suspended.

During the last days of the month the weather was extremely bad. The wind blew from the east, and at times with the violence of a hurricane. The engineer felt some uneasiness for the sheds of the building yard,—which, besides, he could not have placed in any other spot near Granite House,—for the islet only imperfectly sheltered the shore from the fury of the open sea, and in great storms the waves beat directly against the foot of the granite wall.

But, very fortunately, these fears were not realized. The wind hauled rather to the south-east, and in these conditions the beach of Granite House was completely covered by the redan of Flotsam Point.

Pencroff and Ayrton, the two most zealous builders of the new vessel, went on with their work as long as they could. They were not men to trouble themselves about the wind which twisted their hair, or about the rain which wet them to the bone; and a blow of the hammer is as good in bad weather as in fine. But when a very sharp cold had succeeded this wet period, the wood, whose fibres acquired the hardness of iron, became extremely difficult to work; and towards the 10th of June the building of the vessel had to be given up altogether.

Cyrus Smith and his companions had not failed to observe how severe the temperature was during the winters of Lincoln Island. The cold was comparable to that which is felt in the States of New England, situated at about the same distance as the island from the equator. In the northern hemisphere, or at least in the part of it occupied by British America and the north of the United States, this phenomenon is explained by the flat conformation of the territories which border on the pole, where no swelling of the ground opposes any obstacle to the hyperborean winds. But here, as far as Lincoln Island was concerned, this explanation could not hold good.

“It has even been observed,” said Cyrus Smith one day to his companions, “that, in equal latitudes, islands and coast regions suffer less from cold than inland countries. I have often heard it asserted that the winters of Lombardy, for example, are more severe than those of Scotland; and this would be owing to the sea giving back during the winter the heat which it has received during the summer. Islands, therefore, are in the best condition for profiting by this restitution.”

“But then, Mr. Cyrus,” asked Herbert, “why does Lincoln Island seem to escape the common law?”

“That is difficult to explain,” answered the engineer. “However, I should be disposed to admit that this peculiarity is owing to the situation of the island in the southern hemisphere, which, as you know, my boy, is colder than the northern hemisphere.”

“Indeed,” said Herbert, “and floating ice is met with in lower latitudes in the south than in the north of the Pacific.”

“That is true,” answered Pencroff; “and when I was in the whaling trade, I saw icebergs as far up as abreast of Cape Horn.”

“Perhaps, then,” said Gideon Spilett, “one might explain the severe cold which strikes Lincoln Island by the presence of ice or ice-fields at a relatively very short distance.”

“Your opinion is very admissible, indeed, my dear Spilett,” answered Cyrus Smith, “and it is evidently to the nearness of the ice-fields that we owe our severe winters. I would point out to you also that a cause entirely physical makes the southern hemisphere colder than the northern. In fact, since the sun is nearer to this hemisphere during the summer, it is necessarily farther from it during the winter. This explains, then, why there is an excess of temperature in both directions; and if we find the winters very cold in Lincoln Island, let us not forget that the summers here, on the contrary, are very hot.”

“But why, then, if you please, Mr. Smith,” asked Pencroff, knitting his brows, “why should our hemisphere, as you call it, be so badly treated? It is not just, that!”

“Friend Pencroff,” answered the engineer, laughing, “just or not, we must submit to the situation, and this is the cause of the peculiarity. The earth does not describe a circle about the sun; it describes an ellipse, as the laws of rational mechanics require. The earth occupies one of the foci of the ellipse; and consequently, at one period of its course it is at its apogee, that is to say, at its greatest distance from the sun, and at another period at its perigee, that is to say, at its least distance. Now it happens that it is precisely during the winter of the southern countries that it is at its point farthest from the sun, and consequently in the conditions required for these regions to suffer the greater cold. There is nothing to be done about that; and men, Pencroff, however learned they may be, will never be able to change anything in the cosmographical order established by God Himself.”

“And yet,” added Pencroff, who showed a certain difficulty in resigning himself, “the world is very learned! What a big book, Mr. Cyrus, could be made of all that is known!”

“And what a bigger book still of all that is not known,” answered Cyrus Smith.

At any rate, for one reason or another, the month of June brought back the cold with its accustomed violence, and the colonists were most often confined to Granite House.

Ah! this imprisonment seemed hard to them all, and perhaps more particularly to Gideon Spilett.

“Look you,” said he one day to Neb, “I would willingly give you, by notarial deed, all the inheritances which are to come to me some day, if you would be good fellow enough to go, no matter where, and subscribe for some newspaper or other for me! Decidedly, what is most wanting to my happiness is to know every morning what has happened the day before, anywhere but here!”

Neb had begun to laugh.

“Faith,” he had answered, “what I think about is the day’s work!”

The truth is that, within doors as without, there was no want of work.

The colony of Lincoln Island was at this time at its highest point of prosperity, and three years of steady labor had made it so. The incident of the destroyed brig had been a new source of riches. Not to speak of the complete rigging, which would serve for the vessel on the stocks, utensils and tools of all sorts, arms and ammunition, clothes and instruments, now crowded the storerooms of Granite House. It had no longer even been necessary to resort to the making of coarse felt stuffs. If the colonists had suffered from cold during their first wintering, the bad season might now come without their having to dread its rigors. Linen was plentiful also, and, besides, it was kept with extreme care. From chloride of sodium, which is nothing else than sea salt, Cyrus Smith had easily extracted soda and chlorine. The soda, which it was easy to change into carbonate of soda, and the chlorine, from which he made chloride of lime and other chlorides, were employed for various domestic purposes, and particularly in bleaching the linen. Besides, they now did only four washings in the year, as was formerly the practice in families of the old time; and let it be permitted to add that Pencroff and Gideon Spilett, while waiting till the postman should bring the reporter his newspaper, showed themselves distinguished washermen.

Thus passed the winter months, June, July, and August. They were very severe, and the mean of the thermometrical observations gave no more than eight degrees Fahrenheit (13°.33 centig. below zero). It was therefore lower than the temperature of the preceding wintering. And so what a good fire blazed incessantly in the hearths of Granite House, whose smoke streaked the granite wall with long black stripes! Fuel was not spared, for it grew quite naturally a few paces off. Besides, the surplus of the timber intended for the building of the ship made it possible to save the coal, which required more laborious carriage.

Men and animals were all in good health. Master Jup showed himself a little chilly, it must be confessed. This was perhaps his only fault, and it was necessary to make him a good dressing-gown, well wadded. But what a servant, adroit, zealous, indefatigable, not indiscreet, not talkative; and he might with reason have been proposed as a model to all his two-footed brethren of the Old and the New World!

“After all,” said Pencroff, “when one has four hands at one’s service, the least one can do is to do one’s work properly!”

And, in fact, the intelligent quadrumane did it well!

During the seven months which had passed since the last searches made round the mountain, and during the month of September, which brought back the fine days, nothing at all was said of the genius of the island. His action was manifested on no occasion. It is true that it would have been useless, for no incident occurred which could put the colonists to any painful trial.

Cyrus Smith even observed that if, by chance, communications between the unknown and the inmates of Granite House had ever been established through the mass of granite, and if Top’s instinct had, so to speak, felt them, there was nothing more of the kind during this period. The growlings of the dog had entirely ceased, as well as the uneasiness of the orang. The two friends—for such they were—no longer prowled round the opening of the inner well; they did not bark, and they no longer whined in that singular way which had from the first roused the engineer’s attention. But could he be sure that all was said on this enigma, and that he would never know its answer? Could he affirm that some conjuncture would not occur again which would bring the mysterious personage back upon the scene? Who could tell what the future held in reserve?

At last the winter ended; but an event whose consequences might, after all, be serious occurred precisely in the first days which marked the return of spring.

On the 7th of September, Cyrus Smith, having observed the summit of Mount Franklin, saw a smoke curling above the crater, whose first vapors were thrown out into the air.
