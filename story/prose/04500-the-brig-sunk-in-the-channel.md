# The Brig Sunk in the Channel (final)

CHAPTER XLV.

THE MIST RISES—THE ENGINEER’S ARRANGEMENTS—THREE POSTS—AYRTON AND PENCROFF—THE FIRST BOAT—TWO OTHER BOATS—ON THE ISLET—SIX CONVICTS ON SHORE—THE BRIG WEIGHS ANCHOR—THE SPEEDY’S PROJECTILES—DESPERATE SITUATION—UNEXPECTED DENOUEMENT.

The night passed without incident. The colonists had kept on the alert, and had not left their post at the Chimneys. The pirates, on their side, did not appear to have made any attempt to land. Since the last shots had been fired at Ayrton, not a report, not even a sound, had betrayed the presence of the brig off the island. Strictly, it might have been thought that she had weighed anchor, believing she had to deal with too strong a party, and that she had gone away from those waters.

But it was not so; and when the dawn began to appear, the colonists could make out a confused mass in the morning mists. It was the Speedy.

“Here, my friends,” said the engineer then, “are the arrangements which it seems to me proper to make before this fog has completely lifted. It hides us from the eyes of the pirates, and we can act without attracting their attention. What it is most important to make the convicts believe is that the inhabitants of the island are numerous, and consequently able to resist them. I propose, therefore, that we divide into three groups, which shall post themselves, the first at the Chimneys themselves, the second at the mouth of the Mercy. As to the third, I think it would be well to place it on the islet, in order to prevent, or at least to delay, any attempt at landing. We have for our use two carbines and four guns. Each of us will therefore be armed; and, as we are amply supplied with powder and ball, we will not spare our shots. We have nothing to fear from their guns, nor even from the cannon of the brig. What could they do against these rocks? And, as we shall not fire from the windows of Granite House, the pirates will not think of sending shells there, which might do irreparable damage. What is to be dreaded is the necessity of coming to close quarters, since the convicts have numbers on their side. It is therefore every landing that we must try to oppose, but without showing ourselves. So let us not be sparing of the ammunition. Let us fire often, but let us fire true. Each of us has eight or ten enemies to kill, and he must kill them!”

Smith had reckoned up the situation clearly, speaking all the while in the calmest voice, as if it had been a question of works to direct and not of a battle to fight. His companions approved these arrangements without even uttering a word. Nothing more remained for each of them but to take his post before the mist should be completely dispersed.

Neb and Pencroff went up at once to Granite House, and brought back a sufficient supply of ammunition. Spilett and Ayrton, both very good marksmen, were armed with the two precision carbines, which carried nearly a mile. The four other guns were divided among Smith, Neb, Pencroff, and Herbert.

This is how the posts were composed.

Smith and Herbert remained in ambush at the Chimneys, and thus commanded the shore at the foot of Granite House over a pretty wide radius.

Spilett and Neb went to crouch among the rocks at the mouth of the Mercy—the bridge of which, as well as the little bridges, had been raised—so as to prevent any passage by boat, and even any landing on the opposite bank.

As to Ayrton and Pencroff, they shoved the canoe into the water, and made ready to cross the channel, in order to occupy separately two posts on the islet. In this way shots, breaking out from four different points, would lead the convicts to think that the island was at once sufficiently peopled and sternly defended.

In case a landing should be effected without their being able to prevent it, and even if they saw themselves on the point of being turned by some boat from the brig, Pencroff and Ayrton were to come back with the canoe, regain the shore, and make for the spot most threatened.

Before going to occupy their posts, the colonists shook hands for the last time. Pencroff succeeded in mastering himself enough to keep down his emotion when he embraced Herbert, his child!—and they separated.

A few moments after, Smith and Herbert on one side, the reporter and Neb on the other, had disappeared behind the rocks; and five minutes later Ayrton and Pencroff, having crossed the channel without mishap, landed on the islet, and hid themselves in the hollows of its eastern shore.

None of them could have been seen, for they themselves could still scarcely make out the brig in the fog.

It was half-past six in the morning.

Soon the fog was torn, little by little, in the upper layers of the air, and the trucks of the brig’s masts came out of the vapor. For some moments longer great rolls of mist rolled over the surface of the sea; then a breeze sprang up, which rapidly scattered this mass of mist.

The Speedy appeared in full view, moored by two anchors, her head to the north, and presenting her port quarter to the island. As Smith had estimated, she was not more than a mile and a quarter from the shore.

The sinister black flag floated from her peak.

The engineer, with his telescope, could see that the four cannon which made up the ship’s artillery had been trained on the island. They were evidently ready to fire at the first signal.

Meanwhile the Speedy remained silent. About thirty pirates could be seen going and coming on the deck. Some had mounted the poop; two others, posted in the main-topgallant crosstrees and furnished with spy-glasses, were observing the island with extreme attention.

Certainly, Bob Harvey and his crew could only with great difficulty account for what had passed during the night on board the brig. This half-naked man, who had just forced the door of the powder-magazine, and with whom they had struggled; who had discharged his revolver six times at them, who had killed one of their number and wounded two others—had this man escaped their bullets? Had he been able to reach the coast by swimming? Whence did he come? What had he come to do on board? Had his design really been to blow up the brig, as Bob Harvey thought? All this must have been somewhat confused in the minds of the convicts. But what they could no longer doubt was, that the unknown island before which the Speedy had cast anchor was inhabited, and that there was there, perhaps, a whole colony ready to defend it. And yet no one showed himself, either on the shore or on the heights. The coast appeared to be absolutely deserted. At any rate, there was no trace of a dwelling. Had the inhabitants, then, fled into the interior?

That was what the chief of the pirates must have been asking himself; and no doubt, like a prudent man, he was trying to reconnoitre the place before engaging his band in it.

For an hour and a half no sign of attack or of landing could be detected on board the brig. It was evident that Bob Harvey was hesitating. His best glasses, no doubt, had not allowed him to perceive a single one of the colonists crouched among the rocks. It was not even probable that his attention had been aroused by the screen of green branches and creepers which hid the windows of Granite House, and which stood out against the bare wall. Indeed, how could he have imagined that a dwelling was hollowed out, at that height, in the mass of granite? From Claw Cape to the Mandible Capes, over the whole circuit of Union Bay, nothing could have told him that the island was or could be occupied.

At eight o’clock, however, the colonists observed a certain movement on board the Speedy. They were hauling on the tackles of the davits, and a boat was being lowered into the sea. Seven men went down into her. They were armed with guns; one of them took the tiller, four the oars, and the two others, crouching in the bow, ready to fire, were examining the island. Their object was, no doubt, to make a first reconnaissance, but not to land, for in that case they would have come in greater numbers.

The pirates, perched aloft as high as the topgallant crosstrees, had evidently been able to see that an islet covered the coast, and that it was separated from it by a channel about half a mile wide. However, it was soon plain to Smith, on observing the direction followed by the boat, that she would not try first of all to enter this channel, and that she would make for the islet—a measure of prudence, moreover, well justified.

Pencroff and Ayrton, each hidden on his own side in narrow clefts of the rocks, saw her coming straight toward them, and they waited until she should be within good range.

The boat advanced with extreme caution. The oars dipped into the water only at long intervals. It could also be seen that one of the convicts placed in the bow held a sounding-line in his hand, and that he was trying to find the fairway hollowed out by the current of the Mercy. This showed that Bob Harvey intended to bring his brig as near to the coast as he could. About thirty pirates, scattered in the shrouds, did not lose one of the movements of the boat, and were taking the bearings of certain landmarks, which would allow them to come to land without danger.

The boat was now only two cables’ lengths from the islet when she stopped. The steersman, standing up, was looking for the best point at which he could land.

In an instant two shots rang out. A little smoke curled above the rocks of the islet. The steersman and the man with the lead fell backwards into the boat. The balls of Ayrton and Pencroff had struck them both at the same moment.

Almost immediately a louder report was heard, a dazzling jet of vapor shot from the brig’s side, and a ball, striking the top of the rocks which sheltered Ayrton and Pencroff, sent them flying in splinters. But the two marksmen had not been touched.

Horrible imprecations had burst from the boat, which at once went on her way again. The steersman was immediately replaced by one of his comrades, and the oars were briskly plunged into the water.

However, instead of returning on board, as might have been expected, the boat coasted along the shore of the islet, so as to round it by its south point. The pirates pulled hard at their oars, in order to get out of range of the balls.

They advanced thus to within five cables’ lengths of the receding part of the shore which ended in Jetsam Point, and, after rounding it in a semicircular line, still protected by the brig’s guns, they made for the mouth of the Mercy.

Their evident intention was to enter the channel by this way, and to take in the rear the colonists posted on the islet, so that these, whatever their number, should be placed between the fire of the boat and the fire of the brig, and find themselves in a very disadvantageous position.

A quarter of an hour passed thus, while the boat advanced in that direction. Absolute silence; complete calm in the air and on the waters.

Pencroff and Ayrton, although they understood that they ran the risk of being turned, had not left their post, either because they did not wish as yet to show themselves to the assailants and to expose themselves to the guns of the Speedy, or because they relied on Neb and Spilett, watching at the mouth of the river, and on Smith and Herbert, in ambush among the rocks of the Chimneys.

Twenty minutes after the first shots the boat was off the Mercy, at less than two cables’ lengths. As the tide was beginning to rise with its usual violence, which the narrowness of the strait caused, the convicts felt themselves drawn toward the river, and it was only by dint of rowing that they kept in the middle of the channel. But as they passed within good range of the mouth of the Mercy, two balls saluted them on the way, and two more of their number were laid low in the boat. Neb and Spilett had not missed their aim.

The brig at once sent a second ball at the post which the smoke of the firearms betrayed, but without any other result than to chip a few rocks.

At that moment the boat held no more than three able men. Caught by the current, she shot down the channel with the swiftness of an arrow, passed before Smith and Herbert, who, not judging her to be within good range, kept silent; then, rounding the north point of the islet with the two oars which were left to her, she made ready to regain the brig.

So far the colonists had nothing to complain of. The game was going badly for their adversaries. These already counted four men gravely wounded, perhaps dead; while they, on the contrary, without a wound, had not lost a ball. If the pirates continued to attack them in this manner, if they renewed any attempt at landing by means of the boat, they might be destroyed one by one.

It will be understood how advantageous the arrangements made by the engineer had been. The pirates might believe that they had to deal with adversaries numerous and well armed, whom they would not easily get the better of.

Half an hour passed before the boat, which had to struggle against the current from the open sea, had rejoined the Speedy. Frightful cries rang out when she came back on board with the wounded, and three or four cannon-shots were fired, which could have no result.

But then other convicts, drunk with rage, and perhaps still with the libations of the evening before, threw themselves into the boat, to the number of a dozen.

A second boat was likewise lowered into the sea, in which eight men took their places; and while the first made straight for the islet to dislodge the colonists from it, the second manoeuvred so as to force the entrance of the Mercy.

The situation was evidently becoming very dangerous for Pencroff and Ayrton, and they understood that they must regain the mainland.

However, they waited once more until the first boat was within good range, and two balls, adroitly aimed, again threw her crew into disorder. Then Pencroff and Ayrton, abandoning their post, though not before some ten shots had been fired at them, ran across the islet as fast as their legs would carry them and threw themselves into the canoe. They crossed the channel at the moment when the second boat was reaching its south point, and ran to hide in the Chimneys.

They had scarcely rejoined Smith and Herbert, when the islet was invaded, and the pirates of the first boat were ranging over it in every direction.

Almost at the same moment fresh reports broke out at the post of the Mercy, which the second boat had rapidly approached. Two of the eight men who manned her were mortally struck by Spilett and Neb, and the boat herself, irresistibly carried on to the reefs, was dashed to pieces there, at the mouth of the Mercy. But the six survivors, holding their arms above their heads to keep them from contact with the water, managed to set foot on the right bank of the river. Then, finding themselves exposed too near to the fire of the post, they fled at the top of their speed in the direction of Jetsam Point, out of range of the balls.

The present situation, then, was this: on the islet, twelve convicts, of whom several, no doubt, were wounded, but who had still a boat at their disposal; on the island, six who had landed, but who could not possibly reach Granite House, for they were unable to cross the river, the bridges of which were raised.

“All goes well!” Pencroff had said, as he rushed into the Chimneys; “all goes well, Mr. Smith! What do you think of it?”

“I think,” replied the engineer, “that the fight is going to take a new form, for it cannot be supposed that these convicts are so unintelligent as to continue it under conditions so unfavorable to them!”

“They will not cross the channel, at any rate,” said the sailor. “The carbines of Ayrton and Mr. Spilett are there to prevent them. You know well that they carry more than a mile!”

“No doubt,” replied Herbert; “but what could two carbines do against the brig’s guns?”

“Eh! The brig is not in the channel yet, I imagine!” replied Pencroff.

“And if she comes there?” said Smith.

“That is impossible, for she would risk running aground and being lost there!”

“It is possible,” replied Ayrton then. “The convicts can take advantage of high water to enter the channel, even at the risk of grounding at low water; and then, under the fire of their guns, our posts will be no longer tenable.”

“By a thousand devils!” cried Pencroff; “it really seems as if the rascals were getting ready to weigh anchor!”

“Perhaps we shall be forced to take refuge in Granite House?” observed Herbert.

“Let us wait!” answered Smith.

“But Neb and Mr. Spilett?” said Pencroff.

“They will know how to rejoin us in good time. Be ready, Ayrton. It is your carbine and Spilett’s which must speak now.”

It was only too true! The Speedy was beginning to heave in on her anchor, and showed an intention of drawing nearer to the islet. The tide had still to rise for an hour and a half, and, the flood current being already broken, it would be easy for the brig to manoeuvre. But as to entering the channel, Pencroff, contrary to Ayrton’s opinion, could not admit that she would dare to attempt it.

Meanwhile the pirates who occupied the islet had gradually moved over toward the opposite shore, and were now separated from the land only by the channel. Armed simply with guns, they could do no harm to the colonists, in ambush either at the Chimneys or at the mouth of the Mercy. But, not knowing them to be provided with long-range carbines, they did not believe, on their side, that they themselves were exposed. It was therefore quite in the open that they paced the islet and went along its edge.

Their illusion was of short duration. The carbines of Ayrton and Spilett then spoke, and no doubt said disagreeable things to two of these convicts, for they fell backwards.

It was a general rout. The ten others did not even take the time to pick up their companions, wounded or dead; they hurried back to the other side of the islet, threw themselves into the boat which had brought them, and regained the brig by dint of rowing.

“Eight fewer!” Pencroff had exclaimed. “Really, one would say that Mr. Spilett and Ayrton had passed the word to act together!”

“Gentlemen,” replied Ayrton, as he reloaded his carbine, “this is going to become more serious. The brig is getting under way!”

“The anchor is apeak!” cried Pencroff.

“Yes, and it is breaking out already.”

In fact, they could distinctly hear the clicking of the pawl as it struck on the windlass, while the brig’s crew hove round. The Speedy had first come up to her anchor; then, when it had been torn from the bottom, she began to drift toward the land. The wind blew from the open sea; the jib and the fore-topsail were hoisted, and the vessel drew little by little nearer to the land.

From the two posts of the Mercy and the Chimneys they watched her manoeuvre without giving a sign of life, but not without a certain emotion. Terrible would be the situation of the colonists when they should be exposed, at a short distance, to the fire of the brig’s guns, without being in a position to reply to it with effect. How, then, could they prevent the pirates from landing?

Smith felt this keenly, and he asked himself what it was possible to do. Before long he would be called upon to come to a decision. But what decision? To shut themselves up in Granite House, to let themselves be besieged there, to hold out for weeks, for months even, since provisions were abundant there? Good! But after that? The pirates would be none the less masters of the island, which they would ravage at their pleasure, and in time they would end by getting the better of the prisoners of Granite House.

However, one chance still remained: it was that Bob Harvey would not venture his ship into the channel, and that he would keep outside the islet. Half a mile would still separate him from the coast, and at that distance his shots might not be extremely harmful.

“Never,” repeated Pencroff, “never will that Bob Harvey, since he is a good seaman, enter the channel! He knows well that it would be risking the brig, if the sea should become ever so little rough! And what would become of him without his ship?”

Meanwhile the brig had drawn near the islet, and it could be seen that she was trying to reach its lower end. The breeze was light, and, as the current had by then lost much of its strength, Bob Harvey was absolutely master of manoeuvring as he chose.

The route followed before by the boats had allowed him to make out the fairway, and he had boldly entered it. His design was only too easy to understand: he meant to moor broadside on before the Chimneys, and from there to answer with shells and balls the bullets which had until then decimated his crew.

The Speedy soon reached the point of the islet; she rounded it with ease; the spanker was then let fill, and the brig, hauling close to the wind, found herself abreast of the Mercy.

“The bandits! They are coming!” cried Pencroff.

At that moment Smith, Ayrton, the sailor, and Herbert were rejoined by Neb and Spilett.

The reporter and his companion had judged it proper to abandon the post of the Mercy, from which they could do nothing more against the ship, and they had acted wisely. It was better that the colonists should be together at the moment when a decisive action was, no doubt, about to begin. Spilett and Neb had come by keeping under cover behind the rocks, though not without receiving a hail of bullets, which had not struck them.

“Spilett! Neb!” the engineer had cried. “You are not wounded?”

“No!” answered the reporter; “only a few bruises, from ricochets! But this confounded brig is entering the channel!”

“Yes!” replied Pencroff, “and in less than ten minutes she will have anchored before Granite House!”

“Have you a plan, Cyrus?” asked the reporter.

“We must take refuge in Granite House, while there is still time, and while the convicts cannot see us.”

“That is my opinion too,” replied Spilett; “but once shut up—”

“We shall take counsel of circumstances,” answered the engineer.

“Let us be off, then, and make haste!” said the reporter.

“You do not wish, Mr. Smith, that Ayrton and I should stay here?” asked the sailor.

“What would be the use, Pencroff?” replied Smith. “No. Let us not separate!”

There was not a moment to lose. The colonists left the Chimneys. A slight return of the granite wall prevented their being seen from the brig; but two or three reports, and the crash of the balls on the rocks, told them that the Speedy was now only a short distance off.

To rush into the elevator, to hoist themselves up to the door of Granite House, where Top and Jup had been shut up since the day before, to spring into the great hall, was the work of a moment.

It was time, for the colonists, through the branches, saw the Speedy, wrapped in smoke, running up the channel. They were even obliged to stand aside, for the discharges were incessant, and the balls of the four guns struck blindly, both on the post of the Mercy, although it was no longer occupied, and on the Chimneys. The rocks were shattered, and hurrahs accompanied each report.

However, it might be hoped that Granite House would be spared, thanks to the precaution which Smith had taken of concealing its windows, when a ball, grazing the opening of the door, entered the passage.

“Curse it! Are we discovered?” cried Pencroff.

Perhaps the colonists had not been seen; but it was certain that Bob Harvey had thought fit to send a projectile through the suspicious foliage which masked that part of the high wall. Soon, indeed, he redoubled his shots, when another ball, having rent the curtain of foliage, laid bare a gaping opening in the granite.

The situation of the colonists was desperate. Their retreat was discovered. They could oppose no obstacle to these projectiles, nor protect the stone, the splinters of which flew like grapeshot around them. Nothing remained for them but to take refuge in the upper passage of Granite House, and to abandon their dwelling to every devastation, when a dull sound was heard, which was followed by frightful cries!

Smith and his companions rushed to one of the windows....

The brig, irresistibly raised on a sort of liquid waterspout, had just split in two, and in less than ten seconds she was swallowed up with her criminal crew!
