# The Lens Fire and the Ascent (final)

CHAPTER X.

THE ENGINEER’S INVENTION—THE QUESTION WHICH PREOCCUPIES CYRUS SMITH—DEPARTURE FOR THE MOUNTAIN—THE FOREST—VOLCANIC SOIL—THE TRAGOPANS—THE MOUFFLONS—THE FIRST PLATEAU—ENCAMPING FOR THE NIGHT—THE SUMMIT OF THE CONE

A few minutes afterwards, the three hunters were before a sparkling fire. Cyrus Smith and the reporter were there. Pencroff looked from one to the other without saying a word, his cabiai in his hand.

“Well, yes, my good fellow,” cried the reporter, “a fire, a real fire, that will roast to a turn this splendid game, on which we shall presently feast!”

“But who lighted it?” said the sailor.

“The sun!”

The reporter’s answer was correct. It was the sun that had furnished the heat which so astonished Pencroff. The sailor could not believe his eyes, and was too stupefied to question the engineer.

“Had you a burning-glass, sir?” asked Herbert of Cyrus Smith.

“No, my boy,” said he, “but I made one.”

And he showed his extemporized lens. It was simply the two glasses, from his own watch and the reporter’s, which he had taken out, filled with water, and stuck together at the edges with a little clay. Thus he had made a veritable burning-glass, and by concentrating the solar rays on some very dry moss had set it on fire.

The sailor examined the lens; then he looked at the engineer without saying a word, but his look spoke volumes! If Smith was not a god to him, he was certainly more than a man. At last his speech returned, and he cried:—

“Put that down, Mr. Spilett, put that down in your book!”

“I have it down,” said the reporter.

Then, with the help of Neb, the sailor arranged the spit, and the cabiai, duly cleaned, was soon roasting, like a sucking pig, before a clear and sparkling flame. The Chimneys had again become more habitable, not only because the passages were warmed by the fire, but because the partitions of stones and sand had been restored.

It will be seen that the engineer and his companion had made good use of their day. Smith had almost entirely recovered his strength, which he had tested by climbing the plateau above. From thence his eye, accustomed to measure heights and distances, had long been fixed upon the cone whose summit he proposed to reach on the morrow. The mountain, situated about six miles to the northwest, seemed to him to reach about 3,500 feet above the level of the sea, so that an observer posted at its summit could command a horizon of fifty miles at least. It was probable, therefore, that Smith would easily solve the question of “continent or island,” to which, not without reason, he gave precedence over all others.

They had a good supper, and the meat of the cabiai was proclaimed excellent; the sargassum and the kernels of the pignon pine completed the repast. But the engineer said little; he was planning for the next day. Once or twice Pencroff put forward some ideas as to what ought to be done, but Smith, who was evidently of a methodical turn of mind, contented himself with shaking his head.

“To-morrow,” he repeated, “we will know how we are situated, and we can act accordingly.”

After supper, more armfuls of wood were thrown on the fire, and the inmates of the Chimneys, the faithful Top included, fell into a deep sleep. No incident disturbed that peaceful night, and the next morning, the 29th of March, they awoke fresh and vigorous, ready for the expedition which was to settle their fate.

Everything was ready for the start. Enough was left of the cabiai for twenty-four hours’ provisions, and they hoped to replenish their stock on the way. As the glasses had been replaced in the watches of the engineer and the reporter, Pencroff charred a little linen for tinder. As for flint, it could hardly be wanting in ground of plutonic origin.

At half-past 7 they left the Chimneys, each with a stout cudgel. By Pencroff’s advice, they took the route of the previous day through the forest, though they might return by another way. It was also the most direct way to the mountain. They turned the southern angle, and followed the left bank of the river, leaving it where it bent to the southwest. They took the beaten path under the evergreens, and at 9 o’clock reached the western border of the forest. The soil, flat and swampy, then dry and sandy, rose by a gentle slope from the shore towards the interior. Among the trees a few very shy animals had been seen, which Top briskly started; but his master at once called him back, for the time had not come to pursue them. Later, they would see. The engineer was not a man to let himself be distracted from his fixed idea. It may even be said that he observed neither the character of the ground nor its products; he was going straight for the top of the mountain.

At 10 o’clock they halted for a few minutes. As they left the forest, the mountain system of the country had come into view. The mountain was composed of two cones. The first was truncated about 2,500 feet up, and supported by fantastic spurs, branching out like the talons of an immense claw, laid upon the ground. Between these spurs were narrow valleys, thick set with trees, whose last clumps rose as high as the flat summit of the first cone. However, on the northeast side of the mountain, vegetation appeared more scanty, and the ground was seamed with rather deep streaks, which must have been currents of lava.

On the first cone lay a second, slightly rounded towards the summit. It stood a little askew, like a huge round hat cocked over the ear. It seemed formed of bare earth, pierced in many places by reddish rocks. The object of the expedition was to reach the top of this cone, and their best way was along the edge of the spurs.

“We are in a volcanic country,” Cyrus Smith had said, and his companions, following him, began to climb, little by little, up the back of a spur, which, by a winding and therefore more practicable line, led to the first plateau. Swellings were numerous on this ground, which plutonic forces had evidently convulsed. Here and there lay erratic blocks, abundant debris of basalt, pumice-stone, and obsidian. In isolated clumps rose some few of those conifers, which, some hundreds of feet lower, in the narrow gorges, formed thick masses, almost impenetrable to the rays of the sun. As they climbed these lower slopes, Herbert called attention to footprints which showed the recent passage of large animals, wild beasts or others.

“Perhaps these brutes will not willingly give up their territory to us?” said Pencroff.

“Oh well,” said the reporter, who had hunted tigers in India and lions in Africa, “we shall contrive to get rid of them. But in the meanwhile, let us be on our guard!”

Meanwhile they were gradually ascending. The way was lengthened by detours around the obstacles which could not be directly surmounted. Sometimes, too, the ground failed suddenly, and they found themselves on the brink of deep crevasses, which had to be gone round. To return thus upon their track, in order to find some available pathway, cost both time and fatigue. At noon, when the little company halted to dine at the foot of a great clump of firs, near a tiny brook which fell in a cascade, they were still half way from the first plateau, which would probably not be reached before nightfall. From this point the horizon of the sea stretched more widely; but on the right their vision was arrested by the sharp promontory of the southeast, which left them in doubt whether the coast, by an abrupt turn, joined some land lying behind. On the left they could see some miles further to the north; but from the northwest, at the point where the explorers stood, the view was cut short by the crest of a fantastically shaped spur, which formed, as it were, a massive abutment to the central cone. They could, therefore, make no approach as yet to the solving of the question which Smith wished to settle.

At 1 o’clock, the ascent was again begun. They had to bear off towards the southwest, and enter again into a rather thick copse. There, under the trees, were flying about several pairs of gallinaceæ of the pheasant family. These were “tragopans,” adorned with a sort of fleshy wattles hanging over their necks and with two slender cylindrical horns behind their eyes. Of these birds, which were about the size of a cock, the female was uniformly brown, while the male was resplendent in a coat of red, sprinkled with little white tear-drops. With a stone, dexterously and vigorously thrown, Spilett killed one of the tragopans, which Pencroff, made hungry by the open air, looked at with longing eyes.

Leaving the copse, the climbers, by mounting on each other’s shoulders, ascended for a hundred feet up a very steep hill, and reached a terrace, thinly wooded, whose soil was taking a volcanic appearance. From hence, they had to turn back towards the east, in zigzags which made the slopes more practicable, for they were now very steep, and each had to choose with care the spot where he set his foot. Neb and Herbert led the way, then came Smith and the reporter; Pencroff was last. The animals who lived among these heights, and whose traces were not wanting, must have the sure foot and the supple spine of a chamois or an izard. They saw some, but that was not the name which Pencroff gave them, for, at a certain moment:—

“Sheep!” he cried.

They all had stopped fifty paces from half-a-dozen large animals, with thick horns curved backwards and flattened at the end, and with woolly fleece, hidden under long silky fawn-colored hair. They were not the common sheep, but a species widely distributed through the mountainous regions of the temperate zone. Their name, according to Herbert, was Moufflon.

“Have they legs and chops?” asked the sailor.

“Yes,” replied Herbert.

“Then they’re sheep!” said Pencroff.

The animals, motionless among the fragments of basalt, gazed at them with astonished eyes, as if they saw human bipeds for the first time. Then, seized with sudden fear, they disappeared, bounding over the rocks.

“Good-bye till next time,” cried Pencroff to them, in a tone so comical that Cyrus Smith, Gideon Spilett, Herbert, and Neb could not forbear laughing.

The ascension continued. On certain slopes they could frequently observe traces of lava, very fantastically striated. Little solfataras sometimes cut across their route, and they had to follow round the edges. At some points sulphur had been deposited in crystalline concretions, in the midst of those matters which generally precede the outflow of lava, pozzolana of irregular and strongly roasted grains, and whitish cinders made of an infinity of little crystals of feldspar. As they neared the first plateau, formed by the truncation of the lower cone, the ascent became very difficult. Towards 4 o’clock the last belt of trees had been passed. Here and there stood only a few distorted and gaunt pines, which must have been tenacious of life to resist, at that height, the great winds from the open sea. Fortunately for the engineer and his party, the weather was fine and the air still; for a violent wind, at that altitude of 3,000 feet, would have interfered with them sadly. The purity of the sky at the zenith could be felt through the transparency of the air. A perfect calm reigned around them. They no longer saw the sun, which was hidden by the vast screen of the upper cone, masking the western half of the horizon; and its enormous shadow, stretching to the shore, grew as the radiant orb sank in its daily course. A few vapors, mists rather than clouds, began to appear in the east, and were colored by the solar rays with all the hues of the spectrum.

There were only 500 feet between the explorers and the plateau where they meant to encamp for the night, but these 500 were increased to more than two miles by the tortuous route. The ground, so to speak, gave way under their feet. The angle of ascent was often so obtuse that they slipped upon the streams of lava, when the striæ, worn by the air, did not afford a sufficient foothold. Little by little the evening set in, and it was almost night when the party, tired out by a seven hours’ climb, arrived at the top of the first cone.

Now they must pitch their camp, and think of supper and sleep. This second terrace of the mountain rose upon a base of rocks, amid which they easily found a shelter. Firewood was not plenty, yet the moss and dry brushwood which bristled on certain parts of the plateau would serve their turn. While the sailor built up a fireplace on stones which he arranged for the purpose, Neb and Herbert went after the combustibles. They soon came back with a load of brushwood; and with flint and steel, the charred linen for tinder, and Neb to blow the fire, a bright blaze was soon sparkling behind the rocks. It was meant only to combat the somewhat cold temperature of the night, and was not used to cook the pheasant, which Neb kept for the next day; they supped off the rest of the cabiai and a few dozen kernels of the pignon pine.

It was not yet half-past 6 when the meal was ended. Cyrus Smith resolved to explore, in the semi-obscurity, the great circular pediment which upheld the topmost cone of the mountain. Before taking rest, he was anxious to know whether the base of the cone could be passed, in case its flanks should prove too steep for ascent. This question troubled him not a little, for it was possible that, on the side towards which the hat leaned, that is to say, towards the north, the plateau might not be practicable. Now, if the summit of the mountain could not be reached on the one hand, and if, on the other, the base of the cone could not be passed, it would be impossible to examine the western portion of the country, and the object of the ascent would be partly lost. So, regardless of fatigue, he left Pencroff and Neb to make the sleeping arrangements, and Spilett to note down the incidents of the day, and taking Herbert with him, began to walk along the circular edge of the plateau towards the north.

The night was beautiful and still; and not yet very dark. They walked together in silence. Sometimes the plateau was wide and easy, sometimes so encumbered with fallen rock that it offered only a narrow path, on which the two could not walk abreast. Finally, after twenty minutes’ tramp, they were brought to a halt. From this point the slopes of the two cones met flush, and there was no longer any shoulder to separate the two parts of the mountain. To walk around the mountain upon an acclivity whose angle was nearly seventy degrees was impossible.

But though they had to give up their flank movement, the chance of a direct ascent was suddenly offered to them. Before them opened a deep chasm in the solid rock. It was the mouth of the upper crater, the gullet, so to speak, through which, when the volcano was active, the liquid eruptive matter escaped. Inside, hardened lava and scoriæ formed a sort of natural staircase with broad steps, which would make the approach to the summit easier. Smith saw the opportunity at a glance, and followed by the boy, he walked unhesitatingly into the huge crevasse, in the midst of the gathering darkness.

There were yet 1,000 feet to climb. Could they scale the interior wall of the crater? They would soon see. The engineer would continue his ascent as long as nothing stopped him. Fortunately, these declivities, very long and very sinuous, described a wide spiral within the volcano, and greatly helped their ascent. As to the volcano itself, there could be no doubt that it was completely extinct. Not a puff of smoke escaped from its sides; not a flame showed in its deep cavities; not a rumbling, not a murmur, not a tremor came from the dark abyss, reaching down, perhaps, to the centre of the globe. The air within retained no taint of sulphur. It was more than the sleep of a volcano; it was its complete extinction.

Cyrus Smith’s attempt was to succeed. Gradually, as the two mounted the inner walls, they saw the crater grow larger over their heads. The radius of that circular portion of the sky, framed by the edges of the cone, visibly increased. At each step, so to speak, which they made, new stars entered the field of their vision. The magnificent constellations of the southern sky shone resplendent. In the zenith glittered with pure brilliance the splendid Antares of the Scorpion, and not far off that Beta of the Centaur, which is believed to be the nearest star to our terrestrial globe. Then, as the crater opened, appeared Fomalhaut of the Fish, the Southern Triangle, and at last, almost at the Antarctic pole, the glowing Southern Cross, which takes the place of the Pole Star of the northern hemisphere.

It was nearly 8 o’clock when Cyrus Smith and Herbert set foot on the upper crest of the mountain, at the summit of the cone. The darkness was by this time complete, and they could hardly see a couple of miles around them. Did the sea surround this unknown land, or was it joined, towards the west, to some continent of the Pacific? They could not yet discover. Towards the west a band of cloud, clearly defined against the horizon, deepened the obscurity, and the eye could not discover whether sky and water there met in one circular line.

But at one point of the horizon suddenly appeared a vague light, which slowly sank as the cloud mounted to the zenith. It was the slender crescent of the moon, just about to disappear. But her light was enough to mark clearly the line of the horizon, now freed from the cloud, and the engineer could see her trembling image mirrored for an instant on a liquid surface. Cyrus Smith seized the boy’s hand, and, in a grave voice—

“An island!” said he, as the lunar crescent disappeared behind the waves.
