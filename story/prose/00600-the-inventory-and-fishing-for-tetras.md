# The Inventory and Fishing for Tétras (final)

CHAPTER VI.

THE INVENTORY OF THE CASTAWAYS—NOTHING—THE BURNT LINEN—AN EXCURSION INTO THE FOREST—THE FLORA OF THE EVERGREEN TREES—THE FLIGHT OF THE JACAMAR—TRACKS OF WILD BEASTS—THE COUROUCOUS—THE TÉTRAS—A SINGULAR FISHING WITH THE LINE.

The inventory of the articles possessed by these castaways of the air, thrown upon a coast which appeared to be uninhabited, will be quickly made.

They had nothing, save the clothes which they wore at the moment of the catastrophe. It is necessary, however, to mention a note-book and a watch, which Gideon Spilett had kept, doubtless by oversight; but not a weapon, not a tool, not even a pocket-knife. The passengers of the car had thrown everything out to lighten the balloon.

The imaginary heroes of Daniel Defoe or of Wyss, as well as the Selkirks and the Raynals, shipwrecked on Juan Fernandez or in the Auckland Islands, were never in such absolute destitution. Either they drew abundant resources from their stranded ship, in seeds, in cattle, in tools, in ammunition, or else some piece of wreckage came to the coast, which allowed them to provide for the first wants of life. They did not find themselves at the outset absolutely disarmed in the face of nature. But here, not an instrument of any sort, not a utensil. From nothing they must arrive at everything!

And if only Cyrus Smith had been with them, if the engineer could have put his practical science and his inventive mind at the service of this situation, perhaps all hope would not have been lost! Alas! they must no longer count upon seeing Cyrus Smith again. The castaways could look for nothing save from themselves, and from that Providence which never abandons those whose faith is sincere.

But, before all else, ought they to establish themselves upon this part of the coast, without seeking to learn to what continent it belonged, whether it was inhabited, or whether this shore was only the coast of a desert island?

It was an important question, and one to be solved with the least possible delay. From its solution would follow the measures to be taken. Nevertheless, by the advice of Pencroff, it appeared proper to wait a few days before undertaking an exploration. It was necessary, in fact, to prepare provisions, and to procure a more strengthening food than that of eggs or mollusks alone. The explorers, who would be exposed to long fatigues, without a shelter in which to rest their heads, must, before all else, recruit their strength.

The Chimneys offered a retreat sufficient for the time. The fire was lighted, and it would be easy to preserve some embers. For the present, shell-fish and eggs were not lacking among the rocks and on the beach. They would surely find means of killing some of those pigeons which flew by hundreds about the crest of the plateau, were it only with sticks or with stones. Perhaps the trees of the neighboring forest would yield eatable fruits? Lastly, fresh water was at hand. It was therefore agreed that for a few days they would remain at the Chimneys, so as to prepare themselves there for an exploration, either along the coast or into the interior of the country.

This plan particularly suited Neb. Stubborn in his ideas as in his presentiments, he was in no haste to abandon this part of the coast, the scene of the catastrophe. He did not believe, he would not believe, in the loss of Cyrus Smith. No, it did not seem possible to him that such a man had ended in this commonplace fashion, carried away by a wave, drowned in the sea, a few hundred paces from a shore! So long as the waves had not cast up the body of the engineer, so long as he, Neb, had not seen with his eyes and touched with his hands the corpse of his master, he would not believe in his death! And this idea took root more than ever in his obstinate heart. An illusion, perhaps; an illusion to be respected, nevertheless, and one which the sailor would not destroy! For him there was no longer any hope, and the engineer had really perished in the waves; but with Neb there was no arguing. He was like the dog which cannot leave the place where his master fell, and his grief was such that, probably, he would not survive him.

That morning, the 26th of March, at dawn, Neb had again taken the direction of the north along the coast, and had returned to the place where the sea, no doubt, had closed over the unfortunate Smith.

The breakfast that day was composed solely of pigeons' eggs and lithodomes. Herbert had found some salt deposited by evaporation in the hollows of the rocks, and this mineral substance came very opportunely.

The meal ended, Pencroff asked the reporter if he wished to accompany them into the forest, where Herbert and he were going to try to hunt! But, all things considered, it was necessary that some one should remain, in order to keep up the fire, and in case, though it was most improbable, Neb should need help. The reporter therefore remained.

"To the hunt, Herbert," said the sailor. "We shall find ammunition on our way, and we will cut our gun in the forest."

But, at the moment of starting, Herbert observed that, since they had no tinder, it might perhaps be prudent to replace it by some other substance.

"What?" asked Pencroff.

"Burnt linen," replied the boy. "That can serve, in case of need, for tinder."

The sailor thought the advice very sensible. Only it had the inconvenience of requiring the sacrifice of a piece of handkerchief. Nevertheless, the thing was worth the trouble, and Pencroff's large-checked handkerchief was soon reduced, in part, to the state of a half-burnt rag. This inflammable material was placed in the central chamber, at the bottom of a little cavity in the rock, sheltered from all wind and from all damp.

It was then nine o'clock in the morning. The weather was threatening, and the breeze blew from the south-east. Herbert and Pencroff turned the angle of the Chimneys, not without having cast a look at the smoke which was twisting about a point of rock; then they ascended the left bank of the river.

Arrived at the forest, Pencroff broke from the first tree two stout branches, which he made into clubs, and whose points Herbert wore down upon a rock. Ah! what would he not have given for a knife! Then the two hunters advanced among the tall grass, following the bank. From the bend which turned its course towards the south-west, the river narrowed by degrees, and its banks formed a deeply sunken bed, roofed over by the double arch of the trees. Pencroff, so as not to lose his way, resolved to follow the stream, which would always bring him back to his starting-point. But the bank was not without some obstacles: here, trees whose flexible branches bent down to the level of the current; there, creepers or thorns, which had to be broken with blows of the stick. Often Herbert slipped among the broken stumps with the quickness of a young cat, and disappeared in the thicket. But Pencroff called him back at once, begging him not to go far off.

Meanwhile, the sailor observed with attention the disposition and the nature of the place. On this left bank the ground was flat, and rose imperceptibly towards the interior. Sometimes it was damp, and then took on a marshy appearance. One felt there a whole underlying network of liquid threads, which, by some underground fault, must drain towards the river. Sometimes, too, a brook ran through the thicket, and was crossed without trouble. The opposite bank appeared to be more broken, and the valley, of which the river occupied the thalweg, was more clearly marked there. The hill, covered with trees ranged in tiers, formed a curtain which shut out the view. On this right bank walking would have been difficult, for the slopes fell abruptly there, and the trees, bent over the water, kept their hold only by the strength of their roots.

It is needless to add that this forest, as well as the coast already traversed, was virgin of any human trace. Pencroff noticed there only the tracks of quadrupeds, fresh trails of animals, of which he could not recognize the species. Most certainly—and this was also Herbert's opinion—some had been left by formidable wild beasts, with which they would doubtless have to reckon. But nowhere was there the mark of an axe upon the trunk of a tree, nor the remains of an extinguished fire, nor the print of a foot. For this they ought perhaps to congratulate themselves; for upon this land, in the midst of the Pacific, the presence of man would perhaps have been more to be feared than desired.

Herbert and Pencroff, scarcely speaking, for the difficulties of the way were great, advanced only very slowly, and after an hour's walk they had hardly gone a mile. So far the hunting had not been fruitful. Some birds, however, sang and fluttered under the foliage, and showed themselves very shy, as though man had instinctively inspired them with a just fear. Among other birds, Herbert pointed out, in a marshy part of the forest, a bird with a sharp and elongated beak, which anatomically resembled a kingfisher. It was distinguished from the latter, however, by its rather rough plumage, clothed with a metallic lustre.

"That must be a 'jacamar,'" said Herbert, trying to approach the creature within good range.

"This would be a good chance to taste jacamar," replied the sailor, "if that bird were in a humor to let itself be roasted!"

At that moment a stone, adroitly and vigorously thrown by the boy, struck the bird at the root of the wing; but the blow was not sufficient, for the jacamar ran off at the full speed of its legs, and disappeared in an instant.

"How clumsy I am!" cried Herbert.

"Why, no, my boy!" replied the sailor. "The blow was well aimed, and many a one would have missed the bird. Come! don't be vexed! We shall catch it another day!"

The exploration continued. As the hunters advanced, the trees, more widely spaced, became magnificent, but none of them produced eatable fruit. Pencroff looked in vain for some of those precious palms which lend themselves to so many uses of domestic life, and whose presence has been reported as far as the fortieth parallel in the northern hemisphere, and only as far as the thirty-fifth in the southern. But this forest was composed only of conifers, such as deodars, already recognized by Herbert; "douglas," like those which grow on the north-west coast of America; and admirable firs, measuring a hundred and fifty feet in height.

At that moment a flock of birds, of small size and pretty plumage, with long and shimmering tails, scattered among the branches, strewing their feathers, which were loosely attached, and which covered the ground with a fine down. Herbert picked up some of these feathers, and, after having examined them:—

"These are 'couroucous,'" said he.

"I should prefer a guinea-fowl or a heath-cock," replied Pencroff; "but, after all, if they are good to eat?"

"They are good to eat, and their flesh is even very delicate," replied Herbert. "Besides, if I am not mistaken, it is easy to approach them and to kill them with sticks."

The sailor and the boy, slipping through the grass, arrived at the foot of a tree whose lower branches were covered with little birds. These couroucous were waiting for the passing insects which serve them for food. Their feathered feet could be seen clasping tightly the middle shoots which served them for a support.

The hunters then rose up, and, using their sticks like scythes, they mowed down whole rows of these couroucous, which never thought of flying away, and stupidly let themselves be knocked down. A hundred already strewed the ground, when the others made up their minds to fly.

"Good," said Pencroff, "here is game quite within the reach of hunters like us! One could take it with the hand!"

The sailor strung the couroucous, like larks, upon a flexible switch, and the exploration continued. It could be observed that the stream curved slightly, so as to form a hook towards the south. But this détour was probably not prolonged, for the river must have its source in the mountain, and be fed by the melting of the snows which carpeted the sides of the central cone.

The particular object of this excursion was, as is known, to procure for the occupants of the Chimneys the greatest possible quantity of game. It could not be said that this end had so far been attained. So the sailor actively pursued his search, and grumbled when some animal, which he had not even time to recognize, fled among the tall grass. If only he had had the dog Top! But Top had disappeared at the same time as his master, and had probably perished with him!

Towards three o'clock in the afternoon, fresh flocks of birds were seen through certain trees, among others junipers, whose aromatic berries they were pecking. Suddenly a regular trumpet-call resounded through the forest. These strange and sonorous flourishes were produced by those gallinaceous birds which in the United States are called "tétras." Soon a few couples of them were seen, with plumage mottled with fawn and brown, and with brown tails. Herbert recognized the males by the two pointed little wings formed by the raised feathers of their necks. Pencroff judged it indispensable to get possession of one of these gallinaceæ, which were as big as a hen, and whose flesh is as good as that of the hazel-grouse. But it was difficult, for they would not let themselves be approached. After several fruitless attempts, which had no other result than to frighten the tétras, the sailor said to the boy:—

"Decidedly, since we cannot kill them on the wing, we must try to take them with a line."

"Like a carp?" cried Herbert, much surprised at the proposal.

"Like a carp," replied the sailor seriously.

Pencroff had found in the grass half a dozen tétras' nests, each having two or three eggs. He took great care not to touch these nests, to which their owners could not fail to return. It was around them that he thought of stretching his lines—not snare lines, but real lines with hooks. He led Herbert to some distance from the nests, and there he prepared his singular apparatus with the care which a disciple of Isaac Walton(1) would have brought to it. Herbert followed this work with an interest easy to understand, though he doubted its success. The lines were made of thin creepers, tied one to another, and fifteen to twenty feet long. Large and very strong thorns, with curved points, furnished by a bush of dwarf acacias, were fastened to the ends of the creepers by way of hooks. As to the bait, some large red worms, which were crawling on the ground, served for it.

(1) A celebrated author of a treatise on angling.

This done, Pencroff, passing through the grass and skilfully concealing himself, went to place the ends of his lines, armed with hooks, near the tétras' nests. Then he returned to take the other ends, and hid with Herbert behind a large tree. Both then waited patiently. Herbert, it must be said, did not count much upon the success of the inventive Pencroff.

A good half-hour passed; but, as the sailor had foreseen, several couples of tétras returned to their nests. They hopped about, pecking the ground, and in no way suspecting the presence of the hunters, who, moreover, had taken care to place themselves to leeward of the gallinaceæ.

Certainly the boy, at this moment, felt himself very keenly interested. He held his breath; and Pencroff, his eyes staring, his mouth open, his lips thrust forward as if he were about to taste a morsel of tétras, scarcely breathed.

Meanwhile, the gallinaceæ walked about among the hooks, without troubling themselves much about them. Pencroff then gave some little jerks, which moved the baits as though the worms had still been alive.

Undoubtedly the sailor, at this moment, felt an emotion far stronger than that of the angler, who does not see his prey coming through the water.

The jerks soon awakened the attention of the gallinaceæ, and the hooks were attacked with their beaks. Three tétras, very voracious no doubt, swallowed bait and hook together. Suddenly, with a sharp pull, Pencroff "struck" his tackle, and a flapping of wings told him that the birds were taken.

"Hurrah!" cried he, rushing towards the game, of which he made himself master in an instant.

Herbert clapped his hands. It was the first time he had seen birds taken with a line; but the sailor, who was very modest, assured him that this was not his first attempt, and that, besides, he could not claim the merit of the invention.

"And at any rate," added he, "situated as we are, we must expect to see many others!"

The tétras were tied by the feet, and Pencroff, happy not to return empty-handed, and seeing that the day was beginning to decline, judged it best to return to his dwelling.

The direction to follow was plainly shown by that of the river, of which they had only to descend the course. Towards six o'clock, tolerably tired with their excursion, Herbert and Pencroff arrived again at the Chimneys.
