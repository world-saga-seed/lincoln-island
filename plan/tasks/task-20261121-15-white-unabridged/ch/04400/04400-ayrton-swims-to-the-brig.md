# Ayrton Swims to the Brig (draft 1)

CHAPTER XLIV.

DISCUSSIONS—PRESENTIMENTS—AYRTON’S PROPOSAL—IT IS ACCEPTED—AYRTON AND PENCROFF ON SAFETY ISLET—NORFOLK CONVICTS—THEIR PROJECTS—HEROIC ATTEMPT OF AYRTON—HIS RETURN—SIX AGAINST FIFTY.

There was no longer room for doubt as to the pirates’ intentions. They had cast anchor at a short distance from the island, and it was evident that on the morrow they meant to land upon the shore by means of their boats!

Smith and his companions were ready to act; but, resolute as they were, they must not forget to be prudent. Perhaps their presence could yet be concealed in case the pirates were contented with landing on the coast without going up into the interior. The latter, in fact, might have nothing else in view than a supply of fresh water at the watering-place of the Mercy, and it was not impossible that the bridge, a mile and a half up stream, and the works at the Chimneys, might escape their eye.

But why that flag hoisted at the brig’s peak? Why that cannon-shot? Mere bravado, no doubt, unless it were a sign of taking possession! Smith knew now that the ship was formidably armed. And what had the colonists of Lincoln Island to answer the pirates’ cannon? A few shot-guns only.

“Still,” said Smith, “our situation is impregnable. The enemy cannot discover the opening in the weir, now that it is hidden under the reeds and grass, and consequently cannot penetrate into Granite House.”

“But our plantations, our poultry-yard, our corral,—in short everything,” cried Pencroff, stamping his foot. “They can ravage everything, destroy everything, in a few hours!”

“Everything, Pencroff!” answered Smith, “and we have no means of preventing them.”

“Are there many of them?” said the reporter. “That’s the question. If there are only a dozen, we can stop them, but forty, or fifty, or more perhaps—”

“Mr. Smith,” said Ayrton, coming up to the engineer, “will you grant me one request?”

“What, my friend?”

“To go to the ship, and ascertain how strongly she is manned.”

“But, Ayrton,” said the engineer, hesitating, “your life will be in danger.”

“And why not, sir?”

“That is more than your duty.”

“I must do more than my duty,” replied Ayrton.

“You mean to go to the ship in the canoe?” asked Spilett.

“No, sir. I will swim to her. A man can slip in under water where a boat could not pass.”

“Do you know that the brig is a mile and a quarter from the coast?” said Herbert.

“I am a good swimmer, Mr. Herbert.”

“I repeat to you that you are risking your life,” resumed the engineer.

“No matter,” answered Ayrton—“Mr. Smith, I ask it as a favor. It may raise me in my own estimation.”

“Go, Ayrton,” said the engineer, who knew how deeply a refusal would affect the ex-convict, now become an honest man.

“I will go with you,” said Pencroff.

“You distrust me!” said Ayrton, quickly. Then, he added, more humbly, “Alas!”

“No, no!” cried Smith, eagerly, “no, Ayrton! Pencroff has no such feeling. You have misunderstood him.”

“Just so,” answered the sailor; “I am proposing to Ayrton to accompany him only as far as the islet. It is possible, though not likely, that one of these rascals has gone on shore there, and if so, it will take two men to prevent him from giving the alarm. I will wait for Ayrton on the islet, and he shall go on alone to the ship, since he has proposed to do so.”

Everything thus arranged, Ayrton got ready for departure. His project was bold but not impracticable, thanks to the dark night. Once having reached the ship, Ayrton, by clinging either to the bobstays or to the chain-plates of the shrouds, might ascertain the number and perhaps the designs of the convicts. Ayrton and Pencroff, followed by their companions, walked down upon the beach. Ayrton stripped himself and rubbed himself with grease, the better to endure the chill of the water, which was still cold; for he might have to be in it several hours. Meanwhile Pencroff and Neb had gone after the canoe, fastened on the bank of the Mercy some hundreds of paces further up. When they came back, Ayrton was ready to start.

They threw a wrap over his shoulders, and shook hands with him all round. Then he got into the boat with Pencroff, and pushed off into the darkness. It was now half-past 10, and their companions went back to wait for them at the Chimneys.

The channel was crossed without difficulty, and the canoe reached the opposite bank of the islet. They moved cautiously, lest pirates should have landed there. But, after observation, it appeared certain that the islet was deserted. The two walked rapidly over it, frightening the birds from their nests in the rocks. Having reached the further side, Ayrton cast himself unhesitatingly into the sea, and swam noiselessly towards the ship, whose exact position was now shown by a few lights lately kindled. Pencroff hid himself among the rocks, to await his companion’s return.

Meanwhile, Ayrton swam strongly towards the ship, slipping through the water without making even the slightest ripple. His head scarcely appeared above the surface; his eyes were fixed on the dark hull of the brig, whose lights were reflected in the water. He thought only of the duty he had promised to perform, and nothing of the danger he encountered, not only on board the ship, but in these waters, which the sharks often frequented. The current was in his favor, and the shore was soon far behind.

Half an hour afterwards, Ayrton, without having been either seen or heard, slipped along under water, reached the ship, and clung with one hand to the bobstays of the bowsprit. Then he drew breath, and, raising himself by the chains, climbed to the end of the cut-water. There some sailors’ trousers hung drying. He put on a pair. Then, having fixed himself firmly, he listened.

They were not asleep on board of the brig. On the contrary. They were talking, singing, and laughing. These words, intermingled with oaths, chiefly struck Ayrton’s ears;—

“What a famous find our brig was!”

“The Speedy is a fast sailor. She deserves her name.”

“All the Norfolk shipping may do their best to take her. Let them catch her!”

“Hurrah for her commander. Hurrah for Bob Harvey!”

Our readers will understand what emotion was excited in Ayrton by this name, when they learn that Bob Harvey was one of his old companions in Australia, a daring sailor, who had followed out his criminal projects by getting possession, off Norfolk Island, of this brig, charged with arms, ammunition, utensils, and tools of all kinds, destined for one of the Sandwich Islands. All his band had gotten on board, and, adding piracy to their other crimes, the wretches scoured the Pacific, destroying ships and massacring their crews, more ferocious than the Malays themselves! They were drinking deep and talking loudly over their exploits, and Ayrton gathered the following facts:—

The present crew of the Speedy were composed entirely of English convicts, escaped from Norfolk Island. Now, this is what Norfolk is. In 29° 2’ south latitude, and 165° 42’ east longitude, to the east of Australia, is a little island about six leagues in circumference, which Mount Pitt commands at a height of 1,100 feet above the level of the sea. It is Norfolk Island, the seat of an establishment where are crowded together the most intractable of the transported English convicts. There are 500 of them; they undergo an iron discipline, under threat of terrible punishments, and are guarded by 150 soldiers and 150 civil servants, under the authority of a Governor. A worse set of villains cannot be imagined. Sometimes, though rarely, in spite of the extreme precautions of their jailors, some of them contrive to escape by seizing ships which they surprise, and then they rove about the Polynesian archipelagos. Thus had done Harvey and his companions. Thus had Ayrton formerly wished to do. Harvey had seized the Speedy, which was anchored within sight of Norfolk Island, had massacred the crew, and for a year the brig, now a pirate vessel, had scoured the Pacific under the command of Harvey, formerly a captain in the merchant service, now a sea-robber, and well known to Ayrton!

The convicts were most of them gathered on the poop, in the after part of the ship; but a few were lying on deck, talking in loud voices. The conversation went on amid noise and drunkenness. Ayrton gathered that chance only had brought them within sight of Lincoln Island. Harvey had never set foot there; but, as Smith had foreseen, coming upon an island not in the maps, he had determined to go on shore, and, if the land suited him, to make it the Speedy’s headquarters. As to the black flag hoisted at the Speedy’s peak, and the cannon-shot fired after the example of ships-of-war when they strike their colors, it was mere bravado of the pirates. It was no signal, and as yet there was no communication between the Norfolk runaways and Lincoln Island.

The colonists were in very serious danger. The island, with its easy water supply, its little harbor, its varied resources so well turned to account by the colonists, its secret recesses of Granite House—all these would be just what the convicts wanted. In their hands the island would become an excellent place of refuge, and the fact of its being unknown would assure them, perhaps for a long time, impunity as well as security. Of course the lives of the colonists would not be respected, and the first care of Harvey and his accomplices would be to massacre them without mercy. They could not even flee and hide in the interior, for the convicts would make the island their headquarters, and if the Speedy went on an expedition would probably leave some of the crew behind to settle there. They must fight, then; they must destroy these wretches to the last man, unworthy of pity, and against whom any means would be fair. Those were Ayrton’s thoughts, and he knew that Smith would agree with him. But were resistance, and in the end victory, possible? Everything depended on the armament of the brig and the number of her men. These were facts which Ayrton must know at any cost.

An hour after he had reached the brig the noise began to subside, and a good many of the convicts lay plunged in a drunken sleep. Ayrton determined to risk himself on the ship’s deck, which the extinguished lanterns left in profound darkness. He got in the chains by the cut-water, and by means of the bowsprit climbed to the brig’s forecastle. Creeping quietly through the sleeping crew, who lay stretched here and there on the deck, he walked completely around the vessel and ascertained that the Speedy carried four guns, from eight to ten-pounders. He even made sure, by touching them, that the guns were breech-loading, of modern make, easily worked, and terrible in effect.

There were about ten men lying on deck, but it was to be supposed that others, more numerous, were asleep inside the brig. Moreover, Ayrton had gathered from the conversation that there were some fifty on board; rather an overmatch for the six colonists. But, at least, the latter would not be surprised; thanks to Ayrton’s devotion, they would know their adversaries’ force, and would make their dispositions accordingly. Nothing remained for Ayrton but to go back to his comrades with the information he had gathered, and he began walking towards the forecastle to let himself down into the sea.

And now to this man, who wished—he had said it—to do more than his duty, there came a heroic thought. It was to sacrifice his life, but he would save the island and the colonists. Smith could not of course resist fifty bandits, armed at all points, who would get the better of them, either by forcing their way into Granite House or by starving out the besieged. Ayrton pictured to himself his preservers who had made a man of him, and an honest man, to whom he owed everything, pitilessly murdered, their labors brought to nothing, their island changed to a den of pirates. He said to himself that he, Ayrton, was the first cause of these disasters, since his old companion, Harvey, had only carried out Ayrton’s projects; and a feeling of horror came over him. Then came the irresistible desire to blow up the brig, with all on board. He would perish in the explosion, but he would have done his duty.

He did not hesitate! It was easy to reach the powder magazine, which is always in the after part of the ship. Powder must be plenty on board such a vessel, and a single spark would destroy her in an instant.

Ayrton lowered himself carefully between-decks, where he found many of the pirates lying about, overcome rather by drunkenness than sleep. A ship’s lantern was lighted at the foot of the mainmast, from which hung a rack full of all sorts of firearms. Ayrton took from the rack a revolver, and made sure that it was loaded and capped. It was all that he needed to accomplish the work of destruction. Then he glided aft, so as to come under the poop, where the powder magazine would be.

Between decks it was almost dark, and he could hardly crawl along without knocking against some half-asleep convict, and meeting with an oath or a blow. More than once he had to stop short, but at length he reached the partition separating the after-compartment, and found the door of the magazine. This he had to force, and it was a difficult matter to accomplish without noise, as he had to break a padlock. But at last, under his vigorous hand, the padlock fell apart and the door opened.

Just then a hand was laid upon his shoulder.

“What are you doing there?” said a harsh voice, and a tall form rose from the shadow and suddenly turned the light of a lantern full on Ayrton’s face.

Ayrton sprang back. By a quick flash from the lantern, he saw his old accomplice, Harvey; but the latter, believing Ayrton, as he did, to have been long dead, failed to recognize him.

“What are you doing there?” said Harvey, seizing Ayrton by the strap of his trousers. Ayrton made no answer but a vigorous push, and tried to spring into the magazine. One shot into those casks of powder, and all would have been over!

“Help, lads!” cried Harvey.

Two or three pirates, roused by his voice, sprang up and threw themselves upon Ayrton, and strove to drag him to the ground. He rid himself of them. Two shots from his revolver rang out, and two convicts fell; but he received a wound from a knife, which he could not parry, in the fleshy part of the shoulder. He saw in a moment that his project was no longer feasible. Harvey had shut the door of the magazine, and a stir between decks showed that the pirates were all waking. He must save himself to fight at Smith’s side. Nothing remained for him but flight! But was flight still possible? It was doubtful, though Ayrton was resolved to dare everything to rejoin his companions.

Four shots were left. Two of them went off, one at Harvey, which did not hit him, at least not seriously; and then, profiting by his enemies’ momentary recoil, rushed towards the ladder which led to the deck of the brig. As he passed the lantern he knocked it down with a blow from the butt-end of his pistol, and left everything in deep darkness, which would favor his flight.

Two or three pirates, awakened by the noise, were coming down the ladder at that moment. A fifth shot stretched one at the foot of the steps, and the others got out of the way, not understanding what was going on. In two bounds Ayrton was on the brig’s deck, and three seconds afterwards, after discharging his last shot in the face of a pirate who had just seized him by the neck, he climbed over the bulwarks and leaped into the sea. He had not swam six fathoms before the bullets began to whistle around him like hail.

What were the feelings of Pencroff, hidden behind a rock on the islet, and of Smith, the reporter, Herbert, and Neb, crouching in the Chimneys, when they heard these shots from the brig! They rushed out upon the shore, and, with their guns at their shoulders, stood ready to meet any attack. For them no doubt remained. Ayrton, surprised by the pirates, had been murdered by them, and perhaps the wretches were about to profit by the night to make a descent on the island! Thus half an hour passed away in torments of anxiety. The firing, however, had ceased, and neither Ayrton nor Pencroff reappeared. Was the islet, then, invaded? Ought they not to hasten to the assistance of Ayrton and Pencroff? But how? The tide, high at that moment, forbade them crossing the channel. The canoe was no longer there! Judge of the horrible uneasiness which seized Smith and his companions!

Finally, towards half-past 12, a boat with two men came along shore. It was Ayrton, with a slight wound in his shoulder, and Pencroff, safe and sound. Their friends received them with open arms.

Then all took refuge at the Chimneys. There Ayrton told them all that happened, and did not hide the plan to blow up the brig, which he had tried to carry out.

Every one grasped the man’s hand, and he did not hide from them how serious the situation was. The pirates were on the alert. They knew that Lincoln Island was inhabited, and would come down upon it only in force and well armed. They would respect nothing. If the colonists fell into their hands they had no mercy to hope for!

“Well, we shall know how to die!” said the reporter.

“Let us go in and keep watch,” said the engineer.

“Do you think there is any chance, Mr. Smith?” said the sailor.

“Yes, Pencroff.”

“Hum! Six against fifty!”

“Yes, six—not counting—”

“Who?” asked Pencroff.

Smith did not answer, but he pointed with his hand to the sky.
