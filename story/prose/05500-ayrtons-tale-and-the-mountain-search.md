# Ayrton’s Tale and the Mountain Search (final)

CHAPTER LV.

AYRTON’S RECITAL—PLANS OF HIS OLD COMRADES—TAKING POSSESSION OF THE CORRAL—THE DISPENSER OF JUSTICE ON LINCOLN ISLAND—THE BONADVENTURE—RESEARCHES ABOUT MOUNT FRANKLIN—THE UPPER VALLEYS—SUBTERRANEAN RUMBLINGS—PENCROFF’S ANSWER—AT THE BOTTOM OF THE CRATER—THE RETURN

How had it happened? Who had struck the convicts? Was it Ayrton, then? No, since the moment before he had feared their return!

But Ayrton was now in a deep slumber from which it was impossible to arouse him. After he had spoken these few words, he had fallen back motionless upon his bed, seized by an overwhelming torpor.

The colonists, terribly excited, preyed upon by a thousand confused thoughts, remained all night in Ayrton’s house, without returning to the place where the bodies of the convicts lay. As to the circumstances in which these had met their deaths, it was probable that Ayrton could tell them nothing, since he did not himself know that he was in the house at the corral. But at least he would be able to relate the facts which had preceded this terrible execution.

The next morning Ayrton awoke from his sleep, and his companions cordially demonstrated to him all their joy at finding him almost safe and sound after a hundred and four days of separation.

Then Ayrton related in a few words all that had happened, or at least all that he knew.

The day after his return to the corral, the 10th of November last, just at nightfall, he had been surprised by the convicts, who had climbed over the fence. He was tied and gagged and taken to a dark cavern at the foot of Mount Franklin, where the convicts had a retreat.

His death had been resolved upon, and he was to be killed the following day, when one of the convicts recognized him and called him by the name he had borne in Australia. These wretches, who would have massacred Ayrton, respected Ben Joyce.

From this moment Ayrton was subjected to the importunities of his old comrades. They wished to gain him over to them, and they counted upon him to take Granite House, to enter that inaccessible dwelling, and to become masters of the island, after having murdered the colonists.

Ayrton resisted. The former convict, repentant and pardoned, would rather die than betray his companions.

For four months, fastened, gagged, watched, he had remained in this cavern.

Meanwhile the convicts had discovered the corral a short time after their arrival on the island, and since then they had lived upon its stock, but did not inhabit the place.

On the 11th of November, two of these bandits, unexpectedly surprised by the arrival of the colonists, fired on Herbert, and one of them returned boasting of having killed one of the inhabitants of the island, but he returned alone. His companion, as we know, had fallen under Smith’s dagger.

One can judge of Ayrton’s anxiety and despair, when he heard of Herbert’s death! It left but four of the colonists, almost at the mercy of the convicts!

Following this event, and during all the time that the colonists, detained by Herbert’s illness, remained at the corral, the pirates did not leave their cave; indeed, after having pillaged Prospect Plateau, they did not deem it prudent to leave it.

The bad treatment of Ayrton was redoubled. His hands and feet still bore the bloody marks of the lines with which he remained bound, day and night. Each moment he expected a death from which it did not seem that he could escape.

Thus it was until the third week in February. The convicts, awaiting a favorable opportunity, rarely left their retreat, and made only a few hunting excursions, either into the interior of the island or as far as the southern coast. Ayrton had no news of his friends, and no hopes of seeing them again.

Finally, the poor unfortunate, enfeebled by bad treatment, fell in a profound prostration in which he neither saw nor heard anything. Thus, from this moment, that is to say, for two days, he could not even say what had happened.

“But, Mr. Smith,” he added, “since I was imprisoned in this cavern, how is it that I find myself at the corral?”

“How is it that the convicts are lying there, dead, in the middle of the corral?” answered the engineer.

“Dead!” cried Ayrton, half rising, notwithstanding his feebleness. His companions supported him. He wished to get up, they let him do so, and all went to the little brook.

It was broad daylight. There on the shore, in the position in which a death that must have been as sudden as a thunderbolt had surprised them, lay the five corpses of the convicts!

Ayrton was astounded. Smith and his companions looked at him without speaking. Then, at a sign from Smith, Neb and Pencroff examined the bodies, already stiffened by the cold. Not a wound was visible upon them. Only after minute search, Pencroff perceived on the forehead of one, on the breast of another, on this one’s back, and on the shoulder of a fourth, a small red mark, a hardly visible bruise, of which it was impossible to discover the origin.

“There is where they have been hit!” said Smith.

“But with what sort of a weapon?” cried the reporter.

“A weapon which strikes like a thunderbolt, and of which we have not the secret!”

“And who has struck them down like a thunderbolt?” asked Pencroff.

“The dispenser of justice on the island,” answered Smith, “he who has brought you here, Ayrton, whose influence is again manifesting itself, who does for us all that we are unable to do for ourselves, and who then hides from us.”

“Let us search for him!” cried Pencroff.

“Yes, we will search,” replied Smith; “but the superior being who accomplishes such prodigies will not be found until it pleases him at last to call us to him!”

This invisible protection, which nullified their own actions, both annoyed and affected the engineer. The relative inferiority in which it placed him was of a kind by which a proud spirit may feel itself wounded. A generosity which so studiously eluded all mark of gratitude denoted a sort of disdain for those benefited, which, in a measure, in Smith’s eyes, detracted from the value of the gift.

“Let us search,” he repeated, “and God grant that some day we be permitted to prove to this haughty protector that he is not dealing with ingrates! What would I not give to be able, in our turn, to repay him, and to render him, even at the risk of our lives, some signal service!”

From this time, this search was the single endeavor of the inhabitants of Lincoln Island. Everything urged them to discover the answer to this enigma, an answer which could be no other than the name of a man endowed with a truly inexplicable, an almost superhuman power.

In a short time, the colonists entered the house again, and their efforts soon restored to Ayrton his moral and physical energy. Neb and Pencroff carried the bodies of the convicts into the forest, at some distance from the corral, and buried them deep. Then, Ayrton was informed by the engineer of all that had happened during his imprisonment. He learned the adventures of Herbert, and through what a series of trials the colonists had passed. As for them, they had no longer hoped to see Ayrton again, and had feared that the convicts had pitilessly massacred him.

“And now,” said Smith, finishing his recital, “we have a duty to perform. Half of our task is accomplished; but if the convicts are no longer to be feared, we did not restore ourselves to the mastership of the island!”

“Very well,” replied Spilett, “let us search all the mazes of the spurs of Mount Franklin. Let us leave no cavity, no hole unexplored! Ah! if ever a reporter found himself in the presence of an exciting mystery, I am in that position, my friends!”

“And we will not return to Granite House,” said Herbert, “until we have found our benefactor.”

“Yes,” said Smith, “we will do everything that is possible for human beings to do—but, I repeat it, we will not find him till he wills it.”

“Shall we stay here at the corral?” asked Pencroff.

“Yes,” replied the engineer, “let us remain here. Provisions are abundant, and we are in the centre of our circle of investigation, and, moreover, if it is necessary, the wagon can go quickly to Granite House.”

“All right,” said Pencroff. “Only one thing.”

“What is that?”

“Why, the fine season is coming on, and we must not forget that we have a voyage to make.”

“A voyage?” asked Spilett.

“Yes, to Tabor Island. We must put up a notice there, indicating the situation of our island, where Ayrton is at present, in case the Scotch yacht returns to take him off. Who knows that it is not already too late?”

“But, Pencroff,” asked Ayrton, “how do you propose to make this voyage?”

“Why, on the Bonadventure!”

“The Bonadventure!” cried Ayrton. “It’s gone!”

“My Bonadventure gone!” shouted Pencroff, springing to his feet.

“Yes. The convicts discovered her in her little harbor, and, scarcely a week ago, they put out to sea in her, and—”

“And?” said Pencroff, his heart trembling.

“And, not having Bob Harvey to manage her, they ran her upon the rocks, and she broke all to pieces!”

“Oh! the wretches! the bandits! the infamous rascals!” exclaimed the sailor.

“Pencroff,” said Herbert, taking his hand, “we will build another Bonadventure, a larger one. We have all the iron, all the rigging of the brig at our disposal!”

“But, do you realize,” answered Pencroff, “that it will take at least five or six months to build a vessel of thirty or forty tons?”

“We will take our time,” replied the reporter, “and we will give up our voyage to Tabor Island for this year.”

“We must make the best of it, Pencroff,” said the engineer, “and I hope that this delay will not be prejudicial to us.”

“Ah! my Bonadventure! my poor Bonadventure!” exclaimed the sailor, truly dismayed at the loss of his boat, of which he was so proud.

The destruction of the sloop was evidently a thing much to be regretted by the colonists, and it was agreed that this loss must be repaired as soon as possible. This settled, they thought only of carrying to a good end the exploration of the most secret portions of the island.

This search was begun the same day, the 19th of February, and lasted throughout the week. The base of the mountain, between its spurs and their numerous ramifications, was composed of a perfect labyrinth of valleys and counter-valleys, very capriciously disposed. It was evidently here, at the bottom of these narrow gorges, perhaps even in the interior of the mass of Mount Franklin, that the explorations must be made. No other part of the island was so well suited to hide a dwelling whose occupant wished to remain unknown. But so great was the intricacy of these places that Smith was obliged to explore them by a strict system.

In the first place, the colonists visited the valley opening to the south of the volcano, in which Fall River rose. Here was where Ayrton showed them the cavern in which the convicts had taken refuge, and in which he had been confined until his transport to the corral. This place was in exactly the same condition as Ayrton had left it. They found here a quantity of food and ammunition which the convicts had carried off there, with the intention of making a reserve for themselves.

All the valley which ended at the cavern, a valley shaded by beautiful trees, among which the conifers predominated, was explored with extreme care, and then, the south-western spur having been turned at its point, the colonists entered a narrower gorge, which began at that picturesque pile of basalt on the coast. Here the trees were less numerous. The stones took the place of grass, and the wild goats and moufflons bounded among the rocks. The arid part of the island began at this part. They saw already that, of the numerous valleys ramifying from the base of Mount Franklin, three only were wooded and rich in pasture like the valley of the corral, which was bounded on the west by the valley of Fall River and on the east by that of Red Creek. These two brooks, which developed lower down into rivers by receiving a few tributaries, were formed from all the waters of the mountain, and thus caused the fertility of its southern portion. As to the Mercy, it was more directly fed by abundant springs, hidden in Jacamar Wood, and it was by springs of the same nature, spreading in a thousand rills, that the soil of Serpentine Peninsula was watered.

Now any one of these three valleys, where water was not lacking, would have answered for the retreat of some recluse, who would have found there all the necessaries of life. But the colonists had already explored each of them without detecting the presence of man. Was it then at the bottom of these arid gorges, in the midst of heaps of rocks, in the rugged ravines to the north, between the streams of lava, that they would find this retreat and its occupant?

The northern part of Mount Franklin had at its base only two valleys, wide, shallow, without any appearance of verdure, sown with erratic blocks, streaked with long moraines, paved with lava, broken by great mineral swellings, sprinkled with pieces of obsidian and labradorite. This part required long and difficult exploration. Here were a thousand cavities, not very comfortable, no doubt, but completely hidden and difficult of access. The colonists even visited sombre tunnels, made in the plutonic epoch, still blackened by the fires of other days, which plunged into the heart of the mountain. They searched these dark galleries by the light of torches, peering into their least excavations and sounding their lowest depths. But everywhere was silence, obscurity. It did not seem as if any human being had ever trodden these antique corridors or an arm displaced one of these stones. Such as they were, such had the volcano thrown them up above the waters at the time when the island emerged.

Nevertheless, if these places seemed to be absolutely deserted, if the obscurity was complete, Smith was forced to notice that absolute silence did not reign there.

Having arrived at the bottom of one of those sombre cavities, which extended several hundred feet into the interior of the mountain, he was surprised to hear deep muttering sounds which were intensified by the sonority of the rocks.

Spilett, who was with him, also heard these distant murmurs, which indicated an awakening of the subterranean fires.

Several times they listened, and they came to the conclusion that some chemical reaction was going on in the bowels of the earth.

“The volcano is not entirely extinct, then?” said the reporter.

“It is possible that, since our exploration of the crater,” answered Smith, “something has happened in its lower regions. All volcanoes, even those which are said to be extinct, can, evidently, become active again.”

“But if Mount Franklin is preparing for another eruption,” asked Spilett, “is not Lincoln Island in danger?”

“I don’t think so,” answered the engineer. “The crater, that is to say, the safety-valve, exists, and the overflow of vapors and lavas will escape, as heretofore, by its accustomed outlet.”

“Unless the lavas make a new passage towards the fertile parts of the island.”

“Why, my dear Spilett,” answered Smith, “should they not follow their natural course?”

“Well, volcanoes are capricious!” replied the reporter.

“Notice,” said Smith, “that all the slope of the mountain favors the flow of eruptive matter towards the valleys which we are traversing at present. It would take an earthquake to so change the centre of gravity of the mountain as to modify this flow.”

“But an earthquake is always to be feared under these conditions,” observed Spilett.

“Always,” replied the engineer, “especially when the subterranean forces are awakening, and the bowels of the earth, after a long repose, chance to be obstructed. So, my dear Spilett, an eruption would be a serious thing for us, and it would be much better if this volcano has not the desire to wake up; but we can do nothing, can we? Nevertheless, in any case, whatever happens, I do not think Prospect Plateau could be seriously menaced. Between it and the mountain there is quite a depression in the land, and even if the lavas took the road to the lake, they would be thrown upon the downs and the parts adjoining Shark Gulf.”

“We have not yet seen any smoke from the summit, indicating a near eruption,” said Spilett.

“No,” answered the engineer, “not the least vapor has escaped from the crater. It was but yesterday that I observed its upper part. But it is possible that rocks, cinders, and hardened lavas have accumulated in the lower part of its chimney, and that, for the moment, this safety-valve is overloaded. But, at the first serious effort, all obstacles will disappear, and you may be sure, my dear Spilett, that neither the island, which is the boiler, nor the volcano, which is the chimney, will burst under the pressure of the gases. Nevertheless, I repeat, it would be better that there should be no eruption.”

“And yet we are not mistaken,” replied the reporter. “We plainly hear dull rumblings in the depths of the volcano!”

“Indeed,” replied the engineer, after listening again with the utmost attention, “that is not to be mistaken. Something is going on there the importance of which cannot be estimated nor what the result will be.”

Smith and Spilett, on rejoining their companions, told them of these things.

“All right!” cried Pencroff. “This volcano wants to play its tricks! But let it try! It will find its master!”

“Who’s that?” asked Neb.

“Our genius, Neb, our genius, who will put a gag in the mouth of the crater if it so much as makes a show of opening it.”

As may be seen, the confidence of the sailor in the special god of his island was absolute, and, indeed, the occult power which had so far been manifested by so many inexplicable acts seemed limitless. But it had also known how to escape the minute researches of the colonists, for, in spite of all their efforts, in spite of the zeal, more than zeal, the tenacity which they brought to their exploration, the strange retreat could not be discovered.

From the 19th to the 25th of February, the circle of investigations was extended to the whole northern region of Lincoln Island, where the most secret recesses were searched. They even sounded each rocky wall, as police agents sound the walls of a suspected house. The engineer went so far as to take a very exact survey of the mountain, and he pushed his search to the last strata sustaining it. It was explored to the summit of the truncated cone which rose above the first rocky level, and from there to the upper edge of the enormous cap at the bottom of which opened the crater.

They did more; they visited the gulf, still extinct, but in whose depths the rumblings were distinctly heard. Nevertheless, not a smoke, not a vapor, no heat in the wall, indicated a near eruption. But neither there, nor in any other part of Mount Franklin, did the colonists find the traces of him whom they sought.

Their investigations were then directed over all the tract of downs. They carefully examined the high lava walls of Shark Gulf from base to summit, although it was extremely difficult to reach the water level. No one! Nothing!

These two words summed up in brief so many fatigues uselessly spent, so much obstinacy which had produced no result, and there was a sort of anger in the disappointment of Smith and his companions.

But it was necessary now to think of returning, as these researches could not be pursued indefinitely. The colonists had good reason to believe that this mysterious being did not reside upon the surface of the island, and the wildest hypotheses haunted their over-excited imaginations; Neb and Pencroff, particularly, went beyond the strange into the region of the supernatural. The 25th of February, the colonists returned to Granite House, and by means of the double cord, shot by an arrow to the door-landing, communication was re-established between their domain and the ground.

One month later, on the 25th of March, they celebrated the third anniversary of their arrival on Lincoln Island.
