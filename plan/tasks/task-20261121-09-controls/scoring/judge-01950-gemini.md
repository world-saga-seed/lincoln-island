=== THE SPECIFICATION ===

The scene. It is the first days of June 1865, the start of the island's first winter: the colonists have just moved into Granite House, and Whit Sunday (4 June) is still to come. On the morning of 1 June, Pencroff goes down at low water to his fishing lines in the Mercy. Reading the waterline as a sailor does, he sees something on a sandbar in the channel between the shore and Safety Islet: a round thing, about a foot across, half buried. The flood is already making. Not wanting to raise hopes he can't keep, he tells no one but Herbert, out of doors, and asks the boy to help him with the lines at the next morning's low water. That morning dawns in rain. The shutters of Granite House are closed, and the others are at work indoors. The two lift the lines and find them empty. Then they cross the bared sand to the bar with no tools but Pencroff's knife, one of the colony's only two, made from the halves of Top's collar; picks carried out of the house would have been asked about. The thing is a ball of dark green glass, thick with dead barnacles, still lashed to the remains of an old net buried in the sand. It is too fast to turn or pull free, and the lashing runs out of reach under it. Pencroff knows it for a net float, but not cork and not American: he has never seen its like. Herbert reads the barnacles: long dead, of the kind that grow on things kept at the surface. So it has been in the sea a long time and, he thinks, came from far off, which dims their hope of a ship nearby. Chipping the crust away with a stone, they bare part of a moulded seal: a broken run of capital letters, a B, an L, an I. "Latin, I think," Herbert says, and no more. While he chips, Pencroff counts silently what the glass would be worth, light for the shuttered house and a float for his lines, but he never says it. They spend the hour of low water reading it instead of freeing it. Five men with picks could have had it out; two cannot.

Then the squall comes in with the flood. They shelter under the granite wall near the foot of the ladder, where they can still see the float in the surf. The surge breaks what is left of the rotten mooring, and in a rift of light they see a flash on the rocks of the islet, in the spray, and it is gone. They are home by early afternoon, wet through. Pencroff makes a joke of it ("The lines took nothing, and the sea took the pair of us") and says no more, because he still means to bring something home himself and lay it on the table, a surprise. At the next low water, on 3 June, the bar has shifted, and there is nothing to find, not even the net. Pencroff: "Five of us and the picks, and we'd have had it." Herbert: "We had it, Pencroff. For an hour." The two never speak of it again, to anyone. The point of the chapter is the cost of not acting quickly, of keeping a find to themselves. For you, not for the page: the float is from Captain Nemo's Nautilus, from a net her crew lost there years before. The chapter must never say or hint whose it is, and it explains nothing.

=== TEXT A ===

CHAPTER XIX *bis*.

THE FIRST OF JUNE—PENCROFF’S LINES—SOMETHING IN THE CHANNEL—A RAINY DAWN—THE SANDBAR AT LOW WATER—WHAT THE BARNACLES HID—THREE LETTERS IN THE GLASS—THE RISING TIDE—A SECRET KEPT.

The morning of the first day of June broke gray and heavy over Lincoln Island. The wind, which had shifted to the northeast during the night, came in damp gusts across Union Bay, driving low clouds before it and curling the waters of the channel into short, muddy waves. Winter had fairly set in, and within Granite House the colonists were busy enough setting their new domain in order.

But Pencroff, like all true sailors, could never stay long between four walls, even when those walls were cut eighty feet up in the living rock. Before the rest of the household had well stirred from their beds of dried wrack-grass, the honest fellow had swung himself down the eighty-foot rope ladder to the beach, with no companion but his own thoughts and the fresh salt breeze. He wanted to visit the set-lines he had laid out two days before at the mouth of the Mercy, just where the river’s current slackened against the shoals of Safety Islet. Neb had plenty of smoked kangaroo and salted boar in the storehouse, to be sure, and the warren at the southwest corner of the lagoon promised rabbits in abundance; yet Pencroff held that a table without fish was no table at all for men who lived within biscuit-toss of the Pacific.

The tide was at the dead low of the morning ebb, and the narrow channel between the mainland and the islet was almost bare, a glistening expanse of wet sandbars, shallow pools, and dark, weed-draped stones. Pencroff walked out along the edge of the river-mouth, his boots sinking an inch into the firm, ripple-marked sand, his keen gray eyes sweeping the water-line with the practiced habit of a man who had spent forty years reading the sea’s face for shoals, driftwood, or signs of weather.

He had barely reached his first stake when his gaze was arrested by an object lying half-way across the channel, on the crest of a low sandbar that the ebb had just uncovered.

It was round, dark, and seemingly about a foot in diameter, embedded to half its depth in the wet sand. At that distance, and in the dim morning light, it might have been a water-worn boulder, a lump of drift-kelp, or the carapace of some large turtle cast up by the night’s swell. Yet there was something in its smooth, regular curve—an unnatural roundness that belonged neither to stone nor to weed—which caught the sailor’s eye and held it.

Pencroff took a dozen long strides toward the bar, then halted abruptly. Even as he watched, a broader tongue of gray water slid over the outer edge of the shoal, hissed softly around the dark sphere, and withdrew, leaving a ring of white froth behind. Another followed, fuller and higher than the first. The flood was already making, and with a brisk northeast breeze behind it, the channel would be waist-deep in twenty minutes and impassable in half an hour.

The sailor stood for a moment, rubbing his chin with his rough palm. He was a man of impulse, bold and open as the day, yet he possessed a certain stubborn pride that was all his own. To go back up the ladder and announce to Cyrus Smith, Gideon Spilett, and the rest that he had spied something strange on the bar, only to find, when they had all come down in expectation, that it was nothing better than a round stone or a rotten gourd, would have mortified him beyond measure. What Pencroff brought home to Granite House he liked to lay upon the table whole and proven, a triumph of his own sharp eyes and ready hands.

He turned back to his lines, drew them in empty—for the fish had not bitten during the night—and re-baited the bone hooks with bits of mussel. By the time he regained the foot of the cliff, the sandbar was already under two feet of turbid water, and nothing showed above the ripples.

When he climbed into the great hall, he found the others gathered about the kitchen fire, where Neb was boiling an infusion of Oswego tea. Pencroff said not a word of what he had seen. All that day, while they worked at shaping wooden frames for the beds and fitting the thick shutters more closely against the rising wind, the sailor kept his counsel. Only late in the afternoon, when he and Herbert went down together to fetch a load of cedar branches from the Chimneys for firewood, did he broach the matter.

“Herbert, my boy,” said he, dropping his voice as they walked along the beach out of earshot of the cliff, “are you a sound sleeper?”

“No, Pencroff,” answered the lad, looking up in some surprise. “Why do you ask?”

“Because tomorrow morning, at the first streak of day, I want you to slip down the ladder with me before the others are astir. The low water will fall about five o’clock. I have a notion the lines will do better at the turn of the tide, and two pairs of hands are better than one.”

Herbert looked at the sailor’s broad, weather-beaten face and saw at once that there was more behind the request than a string of fish. Pencroff’s eyes had that bright, half-secretive twinkle which always meant he had scented some small adventure. The boy asked no questions, however, for he knew the sailor’s humor, and merely nodded with a quiet smile.

“I shall be ready, Pencroff. Wake me when you get up.”

The night came on wild and wet. Rain beat steadily against the closed shutters of Granite House, and the wind howled across the face of the cliff like a pack of wolves. Within the cavern, however, all was dry, warm, and snug; the clay stove-pipe drew tolerably well despite the easterly gusts, and the colonists slept soundly after their day’s labor.

True to his word, Pencroff was awake before dawn. The small oil-lamp in the corridor had burned out, and the hall was pitch-dark save for a faint gray line under the door-sill. He stepped noiselessly across to Herbert’s corner, laid a heavy hand lightly on the boy’s shoulder, and whispered his name. Herbert was on his feet in an instant. Without waking Cyrus Smith, Spilett, or Neb—who, indeed, had plenty of indoor carpentry planned for the morning—the two unfastened the door, lowered the upper section of the rope ladder softly, and descended into the raw, rain-swept morning.

Day was just breaking behind a thick curtain of gray cloud. A fine, steady rain was falling, blurring the outlines of Safety Islet and dimpling the surface of the river. Overhead, the five windows of Granite House were tightly closed with their heavy wooden shutters, and no sound came from within.

“Now, my lad,” said Pencroff, turning up the collar of his coarse jacket, “let us look to the lines first, in case any one opens a shutter.”

They walked down to the stakes at the mouth of the Mercy and hauled in the cords. Once again the hooks came up bare; the heavy surf outside had driven the fish into deeper water. Pencroff coiled the lines over his arm without a word of disappointment, and, jerking his thumb toward the channel, led the way out across the bared flats toward the middle sandbar.

They carried no tools with them. In truth, the colony possessed few enough—only the pickaxe which Spilett had dropped, a spade, a couple of rough hammers, and two knives which Cyrus Smith had forged from the steel halves of Top’s collar. Pencroff had one of these blades in his belt; to have carried a pick or a spade out of the storehouse at that hour would have provoked a dozen questions from the engineer and the reporter, and Pencroff was determined that whatever lay on the bar should be his own discovery first.

The tide was at its lowest ebb, leaving a broad ridge of wet, dark sand exposed midway between the beach and the rocky shore of the islet. As they approached it, Herbert uttered a low exclamation.

“Look, Pencroff! What is that?”

“Aha!” said the sailor, quickening his pace. “So you see it too, my boy! I thought yesterday morning my eyes had played me a trick.”

A few moments later they were standing beside the object, stooping over it in the falling rain.

It was a hollow sphere—or what appeared to be a sphere—a good twelve inches across, composed of thick, dark-green glass, rough and dull from long immersion, and encrusted over more than half its surface with a dense coat of dead barnacles and calcareous shells. Around its girth, deeply embedded in the crevices of the shell-crust, ran a stout, dark-colored cordage, knotted in a heavy net-work of hitches; and from the lower side of this lashing, strands of coarse, half-rotted netting trailed downward into the packed sand of the bar, where they were held fast as if anchored in cement.

Pencroff dropped to one knee and seized the glass globe with both hands, bracing his feet in the wet sand. He tugged with all his considerable strength, trying first to lift it and then to twist it from its bed. It did not yield by a hair’s breadth.

“Thunder!” muttered the sailor, wiping the rain from his forehead. “It is rooted like an oak! The sand has packed over the net beneath it, and the whole thing is as fast as a kedge-anchor in stiff clay.”

He drew his iron knife from his belt and began to probe around the base of the sphere, scraping away the wet sand with the point of the blade. Three or four inches down, his fingers encountered the stiff, sodden meshes of the old net, stretching outward in every direction beneath the bar, and bound tightly to the glass by thick, tarred seizings that lay far below his reach. To cut it free without digging away half a ton of wet sand was manifestly impossible with a six-inch blade.

“What do you make of it, Pencroff?” asked Herbert, kneeling beside him and examining the strange globe with intense curiosity.

“Why, it is a float, my boy—a net-float, as sure as I am a sailor,” answered Pencroff, sitting back on his heels and eyeing it critically. “Many a time off the banks of Newfoundland, and in the North Sea too, I have seen fishermen use floats to buoy their head-ropes. Only, in our country, they use billets of cork, or barrels, or bladders. Glass floats I have heard tell of among the Norwegians and the Portuguese—thick green bottle-glass that will stand the knock of a wave without splitting. Yet I never saw one quite of this build, nor half so heavy. Look at the thickness of that wall where the light catches it!”

Herbert leaned closer, his naturalist’s eye fixed not upon the glass, but upon the thick crust of marine shells that covered its upper hemisphere. He touched one of the conical, chalky plates with his finger-nail; it crumbled slightly at the edge, empty and dry within.

“These barnacles have been dead a long time, Pencroff,” said the boy thoughtfully.

“Dead? To be sure they are dead, since the thing is high and dry at low water.”

“No, I mean long before it washed ashore here,” Herbert explained, tracing the white, furrowed shells with his finger. “These are acorn-barnacles of a deep-water pelagic species—the kind that attach themselves to floating timber or gear that stays constantly at the surface of the open ocean. Look how the young ones have grown over the old, and then all of them perished together, perhaps when the float sank or was dragged under, or before the currents brought it here. It must have been drifting in the sea for years, Pencroff, and come from a great distance.”

The sailor’s face fell a little at these words. Like every castaway, whenever the sea cast any work of human hands upon their shore, his first, unvoiced thought was always of a ship—a vessel passing near Lincoln Island, or wrecked upon some neighboring reef, whose crew might still be reached or whose signals might be answered. Herbert’s quiet observation, spoken without emphasis, dimmed that hope at once. This globe was no relic of yesterday’s storm; it was a waif of the wide, empty Pacific, drifted from some unknown shore thousands of miles away.

Yet Pencroff was not a man to brood long over a disappointment when there was something tangible before his eyes. If it brought no news of a passing ship, it was still a prize—and a rare one! As he knelt there in the drizzle, his practical mind was already reckoning what that thick sphere of green glass would be worth at Granite House. The five windows of their new dwelling were still closed with blind wooden shutters for want of glass; even a single pane of curved green bottle-glass let into the hall shutter would admit the daylight during the long winter storms! Or, better still, freed from its rotten net and slung in fresh cordage, what a buoy it would make for his deep-water lines off the capes, where wooden floats were forever waterlogging or splitting against the rocks!

He said nothing of these plans to Herbert, however. Keeping his own counsel, he picked up a flat, water-worn pebble from the sand and began to tap cautiously at the thickest patch of barnacles near the crown of the globe, where a smooth curve of dark glass showed beneath the lime.

“Let us clean a bit of this crust off,” said he. “Sometimes the makers stamp their mark on the glass when it is blown.”

Herbert found another stone, and for twenty minutes the two worked patiently side by side, chipping and scraping at the stubborn calcareous deposit, careful not to strike too hard lest they crack the glass beneath. The rain fell steadily on their backs, and away to the eastward, beyond Mandible Cape, the gray horizon had darkened to the color of slate; yet neither of them looked up from his task.

At length a patch of smooth, dark-green glass, as broad as a man’s hand, lay clean and wet before them. Near its center, raised slightly in the substance of the glass itself as though pressed into the molten metal by a die, appeared the broken remains of a circular stamp or seal. Most of the rim had been chipped away or worn smooth by the friction of sand and tide, and the middle of the device was obscured by a pit where a large shell had eaten into the surface; but along the upper arc, three large, well-formed capital letters stood out distinctly in relief.

Herbert bent his head close to the glass, squinting through the rain-drops to trace the raised characters with his forefinger.

“There is a B,” he said slowly, “and then a space where the glass is pitted—then an L—and an I.”

Pencroff peered over the boy’s shoulder, his brow knitted in deep concentration, as if by staring hard enough at the three letters he could force them to spell out the name of a ship or a port.

“B—L—I,” repeated the sailor. “What word is that? Is it English? Or French, maybe?”

Herbert shook his head, still tracing the clean, square serifs of the molded letters.

“Latin, I think,” he said simply.

And he said no more. Neither of them could guess what Latin motto or inscription had once encircled that little seal, nor what hands had blown the glass or knotted the net that held it. They lingered over it nevertheless, turning the three letters over in their minds, scraping a little further to the left and right in the vain hope of uncovering another character, until nearly an hour had slipped away—the whole precious hour of dead low water.

All at once, a cold splash of salt water struck Pencroff squarely across the boots, followed immediately by a sharp hiss of foam that washed halfway up the sandbar.

The sailor started to his feet with an oath. While they had been absorbed in deciphering the seal, the wind had backed around to the east-southeast and freshened to a gale. A heavy squall was driving in from the open sea, bringing a wall of gray rain before it, and the flood tide, driven by the wind, was already pouring through the channel in white-crested rollers.

“Up, Herbert! Back to the shore!” cried Pencroff, seizing the boy by the arm. “The flood is upon us, and there is a squall behind it!”

Had they possessed two picks, or even one, they might have dug the float out in twenty minutes when they first reached the bar; five men working together could have wrenched it free, net and all, before the tide turned. Two of them, with nothing but a short iron knife and their bare hands, were helpless against the packed sand and the rising sea.

They waded back through the deepening channel, the water already swirling nearly to their knees, and gained the foot of the great granite cliff just as the squall broke over Union Bay in full fury. The wind shrieked along the face of the wall, whipping the rain into horizontal sheets, and the rope ladder, hanging loose against the rock, danced and swayed wildly in the gusts. To climb eighty feet up that swinging cordage in the first violence of the squall would have been risky work, even for a sailor; so Pencroff drew Herbert into a shallow recess at the base of the cliff, a few paces from the ladder, where an overhang of the granite sheltered them partially from the downpour.

From this refuge they could still look out across the narrow strait toward Safety Islet. The sandbar was now entirely submerged beneath a boiling cauldron of gray surf; yet for several minutes, each time a heavy roller withdrew into the trough, the dark green crown of the glass float could still be seen gleaming wetly amidst the foam, tethered to its buried net like a buoy riding out a gale.

“It will hold till the afternoon ebb,” said Pencroff, shouting above the roar of the wind, though his tone lacked conviction. “A float that has weathered the Pacific for ten years is not going to part its moorings in a June squall.”

Even as the words left his lips, a monstrous green wave, higher and steeper than any that had preceded it, swept in from the bay, broke with a dull thunder directly over the bar, and buried the shoal under ten feet of churning white water. The rotten hemp of the old net, weakened by years of immersion and strained to the utmost by the sudden surge, must have parted at that instant. A moment later, during a brief rift in the driving rain-squall, both Pencroff and Herbert saw a sharp, bright flash of green light leap up amidst the white spray against the black basalt rocks of Safety Islet—a single, brilliant sparkle as the glass sphere was hurled against the reef—and then the curtain of rain closed down again, and nothing more was to be seen.

Neither of them spoke. For ten minutes longer they stood silently beneath the overhang of the cliff until the first fury of the squall had blown itself out toward the forests of the west. Then, drenched to the skin and shivering in the sharp wind, they climbed the eighty rungs of the ladder in single file and entered the hall of Granite House.

It was already early afternoon—for the morning had worn away faster than they knew—and the rest of the colonists were gathered around the table, where Neb had just set out a steaming dish of stewed rabbit and hot Oswego tea.

“Well, Pencroff!” cried Gideon Spilett, looking up from his plate at the two dripping figures. “Where have you and Herbert been all morning? We thought you had gone down only to look at the lines.”

“And so we did, Mr. Spilett,” answered the sailor, shaking the water from his cap with a forced laugh, “and a fine morning’s work we made of it! The lines took nothing, and the sea took the pair of us.”

He said not another word about the sandbar or the glass float, nor did Herbert volunteer a syllable. Even now, though the float was gone, Pencroff could not bring himself to speak of a prize he had spotted and lost through his own delay. He still meant, when better weather returned, to bring home something worth showing and lay it upon the table before them all, a surprise of his own finding.

Two days later, on the morning of June 3, the weather cleared briefly at dawn, and the low tide fell once more with a smooth, quiet sea. Pencroff and Herbert went down the ladder together at daybreak and walked out across the channel.

The storm had reshaped the entire shoal. The ridge of sand where they had knelt had been scoured flat by the surge and heaped up thirty yards farther toward the river-mouth. Not a trace remained of the float, nor of the rotten net that had anchored it, nor even a splinter of green glass among the shingle of Safety Islet.

Pencroff stood for a long moment looking down at the smooth, unbroken sand, then gave a short, dry shake of his head.

“Five of us and the picks,” he muttered, “and we’d have had it.”

Herbert looked out across the quiet water toward the islet, where the morning sun was just touching the wet black rocks.

“We had it, Pencroff,” said the boy quietly. “For an hour.”

The sailor glanced at him, nodded once, and turned back toward the shore. And from that day forward, neither the man nor the boy ever spoke of what they had found on the sandbar—not to Cyrus Smith, not to Spilett, not to Neb, nor even to one another.
