# The Island Is Doomed and Burns (final)

CHAPTER LXI.

SMITH’S RECITAL OF HIS EXPLORATION—HASTENING THE WORK—A LAST VISIT TO THE CORRAL—THE COMBAT BETWEEN THE FIRE AND THE WATER—WHAT REMAINS ON THE SURFACE OF THE ISLAND—THEY DECIDE TO LAUNCH THE SHIP—THE NIGHT OF THE 8TH TO THE 9TH OF MARCH.

The next morning, the 8th of January, after a day and night passed at the corral, everything being in order, Smith and Ayrton returned to Granite House.

Then the engineer assembled his companions, and told them that Lincoln Island was in fearful danger—a danger which no human power could prevent.

“My friends,” said he,—and his voice betrayed great emotion,—“Lincoln Island is not one of those islands which will last as long as the globe itself. It is doomed to destruction sooner or later; the cause is in itself, and nothing can save it!”

The colonists looked at each other and at the engineer. They did not understand him.

“Explain yourself, Cyrus,” said Spilett.

“I will,” answered Smith, “or rather I will only give you the explanation which Captain Nemo gave me, during the few minutes when I was alone with him.”

“Captain Nemo!” cried the colonists.

“Yes; it is the last service he wished to render us before he died!”

“The last service!” cried Pencroff. “The last service! You will see that, dead as he is, he will render us others yet!”

“But what did Captain Nemo tell you?” asked the reporter.

“This, my friends,” answered the engineer. “Lincoln Island is not like the other islands of the Pacific, and a particular arrangement, made known to me by Captain Nemo, will cause, sooner or later, the breaking up of its submarine framework.”

“A breaking up! Lincoln Island! What an idea!” cried Pencroff, who, in spite of all his respect for Smith, could not help shrugging his shoulders.

“Listen to me, Pencroff,” continued the engineer. “This is what Captain Nemo ascertained and what I verified myself yesterday, during the exploration which I made in Crypt Dakkar. The crypt extends under the island as far as the volcano, and is only separated from the central chimney by the wall which closes its farther end. Now this wall is seamed with fractures and cracks, through which the sulphurous gas generated in the interior of the volcano is already escaping.”

“Well?” asked Pencroff, wrinkling his forehead deeply.

“Well, I have ascertained that these fractures are widening under the pressure from within, that the basalt wall is gradually splitting, and that, sooner or later, it will give a passage to the waters of the sea with which the cavern is filled.”

“That’s all right!” exclaimed Pencroff, trying still to make light of the subject. “That’s all right! The sea will put out the volcano, and that will be the end of it.”

“Yes, that will be the end of it!” answered Smith. “On the day that the sea rushes through the wall and penetrates by the central chimney to the bowels of the island, where the eruptive matter is boiling, on that day, Pencroff, Lincoln Island will go up, as Sicily would go up, if the Mediterranean rushed into Aetna!”

The colonists made no reply to this so positive assertion of the engineer. They understood the threatened danger.

It must be said, moreover, that Smith did not in any way exaggerate. Many persons have already had the idea that volcanoes, which nearly all rise on the shores of the sea or of lakes, might perhaps be extinguished by opening a passage to their waters. But they did not know that they would thus run the risk of blowing up a part of the globe, like a boiler whose steam is suddenly raised by a fierce heat. The water, rushing into a closed space, the temperature of which may be estimated at thousands of degrees, would be turned into steam with so sudden an energy that no casing could resist it.

It was no longer doubtful, then, that the island, menaced by a frightful and speedy breaking up, would last only as long as the wall of Crypt Dakkar should last. This was not even a question of months, nor of weeks, but of days, of hours, perhaps!

The first sensation the colonists experienced was one of profound sorrow. They did not think of the peril which menaced them directly, but of the destruction of that land which had given them asylum, of that island which they had cultivated, which they loved, which they wished to render so prosperous some day! All their labor uselessly employed, all their work lost!

Pencroff could not restrain a big tear which rolled down his cheek, and which he did not attempt to hide.

They talked for some little time longer. The chances which they might still count upon were discussed; but, in conclusion, they realized that not an hour was to be lost; that the building and fitting out of the ship must be pushed on with prodigious activity, as, now, it was the only chance of safety left to the inhabitants of Lincoln Island!

All hands were required. Where was the use, now, of reaping, or harvesting, of hunting or increasing the reserve at Granite House? The present contents of the magazine and the store-rooms would suffice, and more than suffice, to provision the ship for a voyage, however long it might be! What was necessary was that the ship should be at the disposal of the colonists before the accomplishment of the inevitable catastrophe.

The work was resumed with feverish eagerness. About the 23d of January the ship was half planked. Up to this time there had been no change at the summit of the volcano. It was always the vapors, the smoke mixed with flames and pierced by incandescent stones, which escaped from the crater. But during the night of the 23d to the 24th the upper cone, which formed the cap of the volcano, was lifted off by the pressure of the lava, which had reached the level of the lower cone. A terrible noise was heard. The colonists, believing at first that the island was going to pieces, rushed out of Granite House.

It was about 2 o’clock in the morning. The heavens were on fire. The upper cone—a mass a thousand feet high, and weighing thousands of millions of pounds—had been thrown upon the island, making the earth tremble. Happily, this cone leaned to the north, and it fell upon the plain of sand and tufa which lay between the volcano and the sea. The crater, by this means greatly widened, threw towards the sky a light so intense, that, by the simple effect of reverberation, the atmosphere seemed to be incandescent. At the same time a torrent of lava swelled up over this new summit, falling in long streams, like water escaping from an overflowing vase, and a thousand fiery serpents writhed upon the talus of the volcano.

“The corral! The corral!” cried Ayrton.

It was, indeed, towards the corral that the lava took their way, owing to the direction of the new crater, and, consequently, the fertile parts of the island. The sources of Red Creek, and Jacamar Wood were threatened with immediate destruction.

At the cry of Ayrton, the colonists had rushed towards the stable of the onaggas. The wagon had been harnessed. All had but one thought. To fly to the corral and let loose the beasts confined there.

Before 3 o’clock they were there. Frightful cries indicated the terror of the moufflons and goats. Already a torrent of incandescent matter, of liquified minerals, fell over the mountain spur upon the meadow, eating away that side of the palisade. The gate was hastily opened by Ayrton, and the animals, wild with terror, escaped in every direction.

An hour later the boiling lava filled the corral, volatilizing the water of the little brook which traversed it, firing the house, which burned like a bit of stubble, devouring to the last stake the surrounding palisade. Nothing was left of the corral.

The colonists wanted to struggle against this invasion; they had tried it, but foolishly and uselessly: man is helpless before these grand cataclysms.

The morning of the 24th arrived. Smith and his companions, before returning to Granite House, wished to observe the definite direction which this inundation of lava would take. The general slope of the ground from Mount Franklin was towards the east coast, and it was to be feared that, notwithstanding the thick Jacamar Woods, the torrent would extend to Prospect Plateau.

“The lake will protect us,” said Spilett.

“I hope so,” answered Smith. But that was all he said.

The colonists would have liked to have advanced as far as the plain on which the upper cone of Mount Franklin had fallen, but their passage was barred by the lavas, which followed, on the one hand, the valley of Red Creek, and, on the other, the valley of Fall River, vaporizing these two streams in their passage. There was no possible way of crossing this torrent; it was necessary, on the contrary, to fly before it. The flattened volcano was no longer recognizable. A sort of smooth slab terminated it, replacing the old crater. Two outlets, broken in the south and east sides, poured forth unceasing streams of lava, which formed two distinct currents. Above the new crater, a cloud of smoke and cinders mixed with the vapors of the sky, and hung over the island. Peals of thunder mingled with the rumbling of the mountain. Burning rocks, escaping from its mouth, were thrown up more than a thousand feet, bursting in the sky and scattering like grape-shot. The heavens answered with lightning-flashes the eruption of the volcano.

About 7 o’clock in the morning the colonists were no longer able to keep their position on the edge of Jacamar Wood. Not only did the projectiles begin to fall about them, but the lavas, overflowing the bed of Red Creek, threatened to cut off the road from the corral. The first ranks of trees took fire, and their sap, suddenly vaporized, made them explode like fire-crackers; while others, less humid, remained intact in the midst of the inundation.

The colonists had again taken the corral road. They walked slowly, backwards, so to speak. But the torrent, owing to the slope of the land, gained eastward rapidly, and as soon as the lower layers of lava hardened, other boiling sheets immediately covered them.

Meantime the principal current in the Red Creek Valley became more and more threatening. All that part of the forest was on fire, and enormous clouds of smoke rolled above the trees, whose roots were already crackling in the lava.

The colonists stopped at the lake shore, half a mile from the mouth of Red Creek. A question of life or death was about to be decided for them. Smith, accustomed to think and reason in the presence of danger, and aware that he was speaking to men who could face the truth, whatever it might be, said to them:—

“Either the lake will arrest this current, and a part of the island will be preserved from complete devastation, or the current will invade the forests of the Far West, and not a tree, not a plant will be left upon the face of the ground. We will have, upon these rocks stripped of life, no prospect but a death for which the explosion of the island will not make us wait!”

“Then,” cried Pencroff, crossing his arms and stamping his foot on the ground, “it is useless to work on the ship! Isn’t that so?”

“Pencroff,” answered Smith, “it is necessary to do one’s duty to the end.”

At this moment, the flood of lava, after having eaten its way through the splendid trees of the forest, reached the edge of the lake. There was a certain rise in the ground, which, if it had been higher, might, perhaps, have sufficed to hold back the torrent.

“To work!” cried Smith.

The idea of the engineer was instantly understood. It was necessary to dam, so to speak, this torrent and force it into the lake.

The colonists ran to the shipyard and brought back from there shovels, picks, and hatchets, and by means of earthworks and hewn trees they succeeded, in a few hours, in raising a barrier three feet high and some hundreds of paces long. It seemed to them, when they had finished, that they had not worked more than a few minutes!

It was time. The liquified matter almost immediately reached the foot of the barrier. The flood swelled like a river in full flood seeking to overflow its banks, and threatened to pass over the only obstacle which could prevent its devastating all the Far West. But the barrier was sufficient to withstand it, and, after one minute of hesitation, which was terrible, it precipitated itself into Lake Grant by a fall twenty feet high.

The colonists, breathless, without a word, without a gesture, looked upon this struggle of the two elements.

What a sight was this, the combat between fire and water! What pen can describe this scene of marvellous horror; what pencil can portray it? The water hissed and steamed at the contact of the boiling lavas. The steam was thrown, whirling, to an immeasurable height in the air, as if the valves of an immense boiler had been suddenly opened. But, great as was the mass of water contained in the lake, it must, finally, be absorbed, since it was not renewed, while the torrent, fed from an inexhaustible source, was ceaselessly pouring in fresh floods of incandescent matter.

The first lavas which fell into the lake solidified at once, and accumulated in such a manner as soon to emerge above the surface. Over these slid other lavas, which in their turn became stone, but advancing towards the centre. A jetty was thus formed, which threatened to fill up the lake, which could not overflow, as its surplus water was carried off in steam. Hissings and shrivellings filled the air with a stunning noise, and the steam, carried off by the wind, fell in rain upon the sea. The jetty lengthened, and the blocks of solidified lava were heaped one upon another. Where formerly had been peaceable waters appeared an enormous heap of smoking rocks, as if some upheaval of the ground had raised these thousands of reefs. If one can imagine these waters tossed about by a storm, and then suddenly solidified by a cold of twenty degrees, one will have the aspect of the lake three hours after the irresistible torrent had poured into it.

This time the water was to be overcome by the fire.

Nevertheless, it was a fortunate thing for the colonists that the lavas had been turned into the lake. It gave them some days’ respite. Prospect Plateau, Granite House, and the ship-yard were safe for the moment. In these few days they must plank the vessel and caulk it with care, launch it, and take refuge upon it, rigging it after it was on the sea. With the fear of the explosion menacing the destruction of the island, it was no longer safe to remain on land. Granite House, so safe a retreat up to this time, might, at any moment, close its granite walls!

During the six days which followed, from the 25th to the 30th of January, the colonists worked on the ship as much as twenty men could have done. Sleeping but little, the light of the flames from the volcano permitted them to work by night as well as by day. The eruption continued without cessation, but, perhaps, less abundantly. A fortunate circumstance, since Lake Grant was nearly full; and if fresh lavas had slid over the surface of the former layers, they would inevitably have spread over Prospect Plateau and from there to the shore.

But while this part of the island was partially protected it was otherwise with its western portion.

The second current of lava, following the valley of Fall River, met with no obstacle. The ground on either side of the bank was low, and the incandescent liquid was spread through the forest of the Far West. At this season of the year the trees were dried by a torrid heat and took fire instantly, so that the conflagration spread at once by the foot of the trunks and by the high branches, whose interlacing hastened the progress of the conflagration. It seemed even as if the current of flame swept on more swiftly over the tops of the trees than the current of lavas at their feet.

Then the animals, wild with terror, beasts of prey and others, jaguars, wild boars, capybaras, koulas, game of fur and feather, sought refuge on the side of the Mercy and in the marshes of Tadorn’s Fens, beyond the road to Balloon Harbor. But the colonists were too busy to pay any attention even to the most formidable of these animals. They had, moreover, abandoned Granite House; they had not even sought refuge in the Chimneys, but they camped in a tent near the mouth of the Mercy.

Every day Smith and Spilett climbed up to Prospect Plateau. Sometimes Herbert went with them, but Pencroff never. The sailor did not wish to look upon the island in its new aspect, so profoundly devastated.

It was, indeed, a desolate spectacle. All its wooded part was now denuded. One single group of green trees remained on the extremity of Serpentine Peninsula. Here and there appeared some branchless and blackened stumps. The site of the forests was more barren than Tadorn’s Fens. The invasion of the lavas had been complete. Where formerly had been that admirable verdure, was now nothing but a wild heap of volcanic tufa. The valleys of Fall River and the Mercy no longer poured a single drop of water into the sea, and if Lake Grant had been completely dried up, the colonists would have had no means to slack their thirst. But fortunately its southern extremity had been spared, and formed a sort of pool, which held all the fresh water remaining on the island. To the northwest the spurs of the mountain, in jagged outline, looked like a gigantic claw grasping the ground. What a doleful spectacle! What a frightful aspect! How grievous for the colonists, who, from a domain, fertile, wooded, traversed by water-courses, enriched by harvests, found themselves, in an instant, reduced to a devastated rock, upon which, without their stores, they would not have had the means of living.

“It is heart-breaking!” said the reporter one day.

“Yes, Spilett,” answered the engineer. “And pray heaven that we are given time to finish the ship, which is now our sole refuge!”

“Does it not seem to you, Cyrus, that the volcano is subsiding? It still vomits lava, but, I think, less freely!”

“It matters little,” answered Smith. “The fire is still fierce in the bowels of the mountain, and the sea may rush in there at any moment. We are like persons on a ship devoured by a fire which they cannot extinguish, who know that sooner or later the flames will reach the powder magazine. Come, Spilett, come, we have not an hour to lose!”

For eight days longer, that is to say until the 7th of February, the lavas continued to flow, but the eruption confined itself to the limits described. Smith feared more than anything else an overflow of the lavas on to the beach, in which case the ship-yard would be destroyed. But about this time the colonists felt vibrations in the framework of the island which gave them the greatest uneasiness.

The 20th of February arrived. A month longer was necessary to fit the ship for sea. Would the island last that long? It was the intention of Pencroff and Smith to launch her as soon as her hull should be sufficiently water-tight. The deck, the upper works, arranging the interior, and the rigging could be done afterwards, but the important thing was to secure a refuge off the island. Perhaps it would be better to take the vessel round to Balloon Harbor, the point farthest from the eruptive centre, as, at the mouth of the Mercy, between the islet and the granite wall, she ran the risk of being crushed, in case of a breaking up of the island. Therefore, all the efforts of the workmen were directed to completing the hull.

On the 3d of March they were able to calculate that the ship could be launched in about ten days.

Hope returned to the hearts of these colonists, who had been so sorely tried during this fourth year of their sojourn on Lincoln Island! Even Pencroff seemed to be roused a little from the gloomy taciturnity into which he had been plunged by the ruin and devastation of his domain. He thought, it is true, of nothing else but the ship, on which he concentrated all his hopes.

“We will finish her!” he said to the engineer, “we will finish her, Mr. Smith, and it is high time, for you see how far advanced the season is, and it will soon be the equinox. Well, if it is necessary, we will winter at Tabor Island! But Tabor Island after Lincoln Island! Alas! how unlucky I am! To think that I should live to see such a thing as this!”

“Let us make haste!” was the invariable answer of the engineer.

And every one worked unceasingly.

“Master,” asked Neb, some days later, “if Captain Nemo had been alive, do you think all this would have happened?”

“Yes, Neb,” answered the engineer.

“I don’t think so!” whispered Pencroff in Neb’s ear.

“Nor I!” replied Neb seriously.

During the first week in March Mount Franklin became again threatening. Thousands of threads of glass, made by the fluid lavas, fell like rain to the ground. The crater gave forth fresh torrents of lava that flowed down every side of the volcano. These torrents flowed over the surface of hardened lava, and destroyed the last vestiges of the trees which had survived the first eruption. The current, this time following the southwest shore of Lake Grant, passed beyond Glycerine Creek and invaded Prospect Plateau. This last calamity was a terrible blow to the colonists; of the mill, the poultry-yard, the stables, nothing remained. The frightened fowls fled in every direction. Top and Jup gave signs of the utmost terror, and their instinct warned them of an impending disaster. A large number of animals had perished in the first eruption, and those which survived had found their only refuge in Tadorn’s Fens, except a few to which Prospect Plateau offered an asylum. But this last retreat was now closed from them, and the torrent of lava having reached the edge of the granite wall, began to fall over on to the shore in cataracts of fire. The sublime horror of this spectacle passes all description. At night it looked like a Niagara of molten iron, with its incandescent spray rising on high and its boiling masses below!

The colonists were driven to their last refuge, and, although the upper seams of the ship were not yet caulked, they resolved to launch their ship into the sea!

Pencroff and Ayrton made the preparations for this event, which was to take place on the morning of the next day, the 9th of March.

But, during that night of the 8th to the 9th, an enormous column of steam escaped from the crater, rising in the midst of terrific detonations to a height of more than 3,000 feet. The wall of Crypt Dakkar had evidently given way under the pressure of the gas, and the sea, pouring through the central chimney into the burning gulf, was suddenly turned into steam!

The crater was not a sufficient vent for this vapor!

An explosion, which could have been heard a hundred miles away, shook the strata of the air! Fragments of mountains fell into the Pacific, and, in a few minutes, the ocean covered the place where Lincoln Island had been!
