# The Storm and Top’s Return (final)

CHAPTER VII.

NEB HAS NOT YET RETURNED—THE REFLECTIONS OF THE REPORTER—THE SUPPER—PROSPECT OF A BAD NIGHT—THE STORM IS FRIGHTFUL—THEY GO OUT INTO THE NIGHT—STRUGGLE WITH THE RAIN AND WIND—EIGHT MILES FROM THE FIRST ENCAMPMENT.

Gideon Spilett stood motionless upon the shore, his arms crossed, gazing on the sea, whose horizon was blended towards the east with a huge black cloud mounting rapidly into the zenith. The wind, already strong, was freshening with the decline of day, the heavens had an angry look, and the first symptoms of a heavy blow were visibly manifesting themselves.

Herbert went into the Chimneys, and Pencroff walked towards the reporter, who was too absorbed to notice his approach.

“We will have a bad night, Mr. Spilett,” said the sailor. “Wind and rain enough to delight Mother Cary’s chickens(1)!”

(1) Sea-birds which take pleasure above all in the midst of tempests.

The reporter, turning, perceived Pencroff, and his first words were these:—

“How far off from the shore do you think was the basket when it was struck by the sea that carried away our companion?”

The sailor had not expected this question. He reflected an instant before answering:—

“Two cables’ lengths at the most.”

“How much is a cable’s length?” demanded Spilett.

“About 120 fathoms, or 600 feet.”

“Then,” said the reporter, “Cyrus Smith would have disappeared not more than 1,200 feet from the shore?”

“About that,” replied Pencroff.

“And his dog, too?”

“Yes.”

“What astonishes me,” added the reporter, “admitting that our companion has perished, is that Top should also have met his death, and that neither the body of the dog nor of his master has been cast upon the shore!”

“That is not astonishing with so heavy a sea,” replied the sailor. “Moreover, it may be that the currents have carried them farther up the coast.”

“Then it is really your opinion that our companion has been drowned?” asked, once more, the reporter.

“That is my opinion.”

“And my opinion, Pencroff,” said Spilett, “with all respect for your experience, is, that in this absolute disappearance of both Cyrus and Top, living or dead, there is something inexplicable and incredible.”

“I wish I could think as you do, sir,” responded Pencroff, “but, unhappily, my mind is made up!”

After thus speaking the sailor returned to the Chimneys. A good fire was crackling in the fireplace. Herbert had just thrown on an armful of dry wood, and its flames lit up the dark recesses of the corridor.

Pencroff began at once to busy himself about dinner. It seemed expedient to provide something substantial, as all stood in need of nourishment, so two tetras were quickly plucked, spitted upon a stick, and placed to roast before a blazing fire. The strings of couroucous were reserved for the next day.

At 7 o’clock Neb was still absent, and Pencroff began to be alarmed about him. He feared that he might have met with some accident in this unknown land, or that the poor fellow had been drawn by despair to some rash act. Herbert, on the contrary, drew from this absence quite different conclusions. To him, Neb’s absence was owing to some new circumstance which had induced him to prolong his researches. And anything new must be to Cyrus Smith’s advantage. Why had not Neb come back, if some hope was not detaining him? Perhaps he had found some sign, a footprint, a remnant of wreck, which had put him upon the track. Perhaps, at this moment he was following a sure trail. Perhaps, already, he was beside his master.

Thus the lad spoke and reasoned, unchecked by his companions. The reporter alone nodded approval, but Pencroff thought it more probable that Neb had pushed his search along the shore farther than on the day before, and that he could not yet have returned.

Meantime, Herbert, much agitated by vague presentiments, several times manifested a desire to go to meet Neb. But Pencroff showed him that it would be useless in the darkness and storm to attempt to find traces of Neb, and, that the better course was, to wait. If, by morning, Neb had not returned, Pencroff would not hesitate joining the lad in a search for him.

Gideon Spilett concurred with the sailor in his opinion that they had better remain together, and Herbert was obliged to give up the project; but two big tears fell from his eyes. The reporter could not help embracing the generous lad.

The bad weather had now fully declared itself. A gale from the southeast passed over the coast with unequalled violence. They heard the sea, which was then ebbing, roaring along the edge of the first rocks, off the shore. The rain, beaten into spray by the hurricane, rose like a liquid mist. It was as if rags of vapor were trailing along the shore, whose shingle rattled violently, like cartloads of pebbles being emptied. The sand, stirred up by the wind, mingled with the showers, and made their assault unbearable. The air was filled with mineral as well as aqueous dust. Between the mouth of the river and the cliff’s face, the wind whirled about as in a maelstrom, and, finding no other outlet than the narrow valley through which ran the stream, it rushed through this with irresistible violence.

Thus the smoke from the fireplace, driven back down its narrow vent, frequently filled the corridors, and rendered them uninhabitable. Therefore, when the tetras were cooked Pencroff let the fire smoulder, only preserving some embers buried under the ashes.

At 8 o’clock Neb had not returned; but it might now be admitted that the frightful weather alone was sufficient to account for his non-appearance, and that he must have sought refuge in some cavern, waiting the end of the storm, or, at least, daybreak. As to going to meet him under present circumstances, that was simply impossible.

The birds were all they had for supper, but the party found them excellent eating. Pencroff and Herbert, their appetite sharpened by their long walk, devoured them. Then each one retired to the corner where he had rested the night before, and Herbert, lying beside the sailor, extended before the fireplace, was soon asleep.

Outside, as the night advanced, the storm developed formidable proportions. It was a hurricane equal to that which had carried the prisoners from Richmond to this land in the Pacific. Such tempests, pregnant with catastrophes, terrible above all over this vast expanse, which opposes no obstacle to their fury, are frequent during the equinox. We can understand how a coast facing the east, and exposed to the full fury of the storm, was attacked with a violence perfectly indescribable.

Pencroff crept to the opening of the corridor. Very happily the heap of rocks forming the Chimneys was composed of solid, enormous blocks of granite, though some of them, imperfectly balanced, seemed to tremble upon their foundations. Pencroff, placing his hand against the walls, could feel their rapid vibrations; but he said to himself, with reason, that there was no real danger, and that the improvised retreat would not tumble about their ears. Nevertheless, he heard the sound of rocks, torn from the top of the plateau by the gusts, crashing upon the shore. Some even rolled upon the upper part of the Chimneys, or flew off there into fragments when they fell perpendicularly. Twice the sailor rose, and crept to the opening of the corridor, to look abroad. But there was no danger from these inconsiderable showers of stones, and he returned to his place before the fire, where the embers crackled under the ashes.

In spite of the fury of the hurricane, the fracas of the tempest, and the thunder of the storm, Herbert slept profoundly, and, at length, sleep took possession of Pencroff, whose sailor life had accustomed him to such demonstrations. Gideon Spilett alone was kept awake by anxiety. He reproached himself for not having accompanied Neb. We have seen that he had not given up all hope, and the presentiments which had disturbed Herbert had affected him also. His thoughts were fixed upon Neb; why had not Neb returned? He tossed about on his sandy couch, unheeding the warfare of the elements. Then, overcome by fatigue, he would close his eyes for an instant, only to be opened again by some sudden thought.

Meantime the night advanced; and it was about 2 o’clock when Pencroff was suddenly aroused from a deep sleep by finding himself vigorously shaken.

“What’s the matter?” he cried, rousing and collecting himself with the quickness peculiar to sailors.

The reporter was bending over him and saying:—

“Listen, Pencroff, listen!”

The sailor listened, but could hear no sounds other than those caused by the gusts.

“It is the wind,” he said.

“No,” answered Spilett, listening again, “I think I heard—”

“What?”

“The barking of a dog!”

“A dog!” cried Pencroff, springing to his feet.

“Yes—the barking—”

“Impossible!” answered the sailor. “And besides, how, in the roarings of the tempest—”

“Wait—listen,” said the reporter.

Pencroff listened more attentively, and, in fact, during a lull, he thought he caught the sound of distant barking.

“Well!” said the reporter, squeezing the sailor’s hand.

“Yes—yes!” said Pencroff.

“It is Top! It is Top!” cried Herbert, who had just wakened, and the three rushed to the entrance of the Chimneys.

They had extreme difficulty in getting out, as the wind drove against them with fury, but at last they succeeded, and then they were obliged to steady themselves against the rocks. They were unable to speak, but they looked about them. The darkness was absolute. Sea, sky, and earth, were one intense blackness. It seemed as if there was not one particle of light diffused in the atmosphere.

For some moments the reporter and his two companions stood in this place, as if crushed by the gusts, drenched by the rain, blinded by the sand. Then again, in the hush of the storm, they heard, far away, the barking of a dog. This must be Top. But was he alone or accompanied? Probably alone, for if Neb had been with him, Neb would have hastened, at once, to the Chimneys.

The sailor pressed the reporter’s hand, for he could not make himself heard, in a manner which signified “Wait!” and then returning to the corridor, emerged a moment later with a lighted fagot, which he threw into the darkness, at the same time whistling shrilly. At this signal, which seemed to have been looked for, the answering barks came nearer, and soon a dog bounded into the corridor, followed by the three companions. An armful of dry wood was thrown upon the coals, brightly lighting up the passage.

“It is Top!” cried Herbert.

It was indeed Top, a magnificent Anglo-Norman, uniting in the cross of the two breeds those qualities—swiftness of foot and keenness of scent—the qualities above all others of coursing dogs. It was the dog of the engineer, Cyrus Smith. But he was alone! Neither his master nor Neb accompanied him!

It seemed inexplicable how, through the darkness and storm, the dog’s instinct had directed him to the Chimneys, a place he was unacquainted with. But still more unaccountable was the fact that he was neither fatigued nor exhausted, nor even soiled with mud or sand! Herbert had drawn him towards him, and was pressing his head between his hands; and the dog let him do as he would, and rubbed his neck against the lad’s hands.

“If the dog is found, the master will be found also,” said the reporter.

“God grant it!” responded Herbert. “Come, let us set out. Top will guide us!”

Pencroff made no objection. He felt plainly that the arrival of Top might give the lie to his conjectures.

“Let us set out at once,” he said; and having carefully covered the coals of the fire, placing some pieces of wood under the ashes so that they might find fire on their return, and preceded by the dog, who seemed by short barks to invite their departure, the sailor, having gathered up the remnants of the supper, followed by the reporter and Herbert, rushed into the darkness.

The tempest, then in all its violence, was, perhaps, at its maximum intensity. The moon, then new, and therefore in conjunction with the sun, did not let the least glimmer pass through the clouds. It was difficult to follow a straight course. The better way, therefore, was to trust to the instinct of Top; which was done. The reporter and the lad walked behind the dog, and the sailor followed after. To speak was impossible. The rain, dispersed by the wind, was not heavy, but the strength of the storm was terrible.

Fortunately, as it came from the southeast, the wind was at the back of the party, and the sand, which it hurled with a violence that would have been unbearable, struck them from behind, so that, provided they did not turn round, it did not hinder their march. Indeed, they often went faster than they wished, and hurried their steps so as not to be overthrown. But an immense hope doubled their strength. This time they were not following the shore at random. They had no doubt that Neb had found his master and had sent the faithful dog to them. But was the engineer living, or had Neb summoned his companions only to render the last services to the body of the unfortunate Smith?

After having passed the smooth face of rock, which they carefully avoided, Herbert, the reporter, and Pencroff stopped to take breath. The angle of the cliff sheltered them from the wind, and they could breathe freely after this tramp, or rather race, of a quarter of an hour. They were now able to hear themselves speak, and the lad having pronounced the name of Cyrus Smith, the dog seemed to say by his short barks that his master was safe.

“Saved, is he not? Saved, Top?” repeated the boy. And the dog barked his answer.

It was about half-past 2 when the march was resumed. The sea began to rise, and this, which was a spring tide backed up by the wind, threatened to be very high. The tremendous breakers thundered against the reef, assailing it so violently as very probably to pass completely over the islet, which was then absolutely invisible. The coast was no longer sheltered by this long breakwater, but was exposed to the full fury of the open sea.

After the party were clear of the smooth face of rock the storm attacked them again with extreme fury. Crouching, with backs still to the wind, they walked very fast, following Top, who never hesitated in his course. Mounting towards the north, they had upon their right the endless line of breakers deafening them with its thunders, and upon their left a dark region of which it was impossible to make out the aspect. But they felt that the country must be comparatively flat, as the wind rushed over them without rebounding as it had done from the granite cliffs.

By 4 o’clock they estimated the distance travelled as five miles. The clouds had risen a little, and no longer trailed upon the ground. The wind, less damp, blew in very keen currents, drier and colder. Insufficiently clad, the three companions must have suffered cruelly, but no murmur passed their lips. They were determined to follow Top wherever the intelligent animal wished to lead them.

Towards 5 o’clock the day began to break. At first, overhead, where the vapors were less thick, some grey shadowings bordered the clouds, and presently, under a dark band a bright streak of light sharply defined the sea horizon. The crests of the billows were lightly touched with a tawny light and the foam revealed its whiteness. At the same time, on the left, the hilly parts of the shore were confusedly defined in grey outlines upon the blackness of the night. At 6 o’clock it was daylight. The clouds sped with extreme rapidity at a comparatively great height. The sailor and his companions were some six miles from the Chimneys, following a very flat shore, bordered in the offing by a reef of rocks whose surface only was visible above the high tide. On the left the country, broken by a few downs bristling with thistles, had the rather wild aspect of a vast sandy region. The shore was little indented, and offered no other resistance to the ocean than an irregular chain of hillocks. Here and there were one or two twisted trees, leaning their trunks and branches towards the west. Far behind, to the southwest, curved the border of the last forest.

At this moment Top gave unequivocal signs of excitement. He ran ahead, returned to the sailor, and seemed to urge him to hasten his steps. The dog had left the coast, and guided by his wonderful instinct, without any hesitation had gone among the downs. They followed him. The country appeared to be absolutely deserted; not a living creature enlivened it.

The border of the downs, very wide, was composed of hills and hillocks, unevenly scattered here and there. It was like a little Switzerland of sand, and nothing but a dog’s astonishing instinct could find the way.

Five minutes after leaving the shore the reporter and his companions reached a sort of hollow, formed in the back of a high down, before which Top stopped with a clear bark. Spilett, Herbert, and Pencroff entered the cave.

Neb was there, kneeling beside a body extended upon a bed of grass—

It was the body of the engineer, Cyrus Smith.
